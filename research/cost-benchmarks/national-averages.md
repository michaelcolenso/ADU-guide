# National ADU Cost Benchmarks (2025–2026)
## Regional Comparison Table

*Research file for Chapter 10: Budgeting and Cost Control. All figures reflect 2025–2026 market conditions and should be verified against current contractor bids before use in any planning document.*

---

## How to Use This File

National averages are less useful than regional data, but they provide orientation and a sanity check. When a homeowner reads a headline figure — "$150,000 for an ADU" or "$400 per square foot" — this table gives context for whether that figure is plausible in their market, high, or wishful.

The right way to use this data in Chapter 10 is as a planning filter, not a bid target. These figures help a homeowner ask better questions of contractors and lenders. They are not substitutes for actual bids in an actual local market. A homeowner in Dallas should not plan to the national average; a homeowner in San Jose should not plan to the national average either. Both will be wrong, in opposite directions.

A second caution: "all-in" means different things in different sources. For this file, all-in includes construction hard costs, soft costs (architecture, engineering, permits), and standard utility connection work for a project without unusual site conditions. It does not include financing costs, owner-furnished items, landscaping restoration, or the cost of a homeowner's time.

**Interpreting the ranges:**
The width of each regional range is intentional. Use the full range, not the midpoint.

## Contingency Guidance by Region

Contingency requirements track regulatory complexity, contractor market tightness, and site condition variability. The figures below reflect judgment about these factors in 2025–2026 conditions.

| Region | Recommended Contingency | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| West Coast (CA, WA, OR — urban) | 15–20% | High regulatory complexity; tight contractor capacity in major metros; geology and soil variability |
| Mountain West / Southwest | 12–15% | Moderate complexity; more contractor availability in most markets; less regulatory unpredictability |
| Midwest / Southeast | 10–15% | Lower complexity; more competitive bidding environments; fewer required consultants and studies |
| Northeast | 12–18% | High labor cost volatility; complex local permitting in urban areas; older housing stock creates discovery risk |
| Hawaii / remote markets | 20–25% | Materials shipping premium; very limited contractor capacity; long lead times for specialty items |

**How to apply these figures:** The contingency percentage applies to total estimated project cost — hard costs plus soft costs plus utility work. It is held in reserve by the owner and not included in the contractor's contract sum. It is drawn down as change orders arise, scope additions are made, or unforeseen conditions are addressed. Any contingency remaining at project completion is owner savings.

A contingency that is never touched is not waste. It is insurance that was not needed on this project.

## What Drives Regional Cost Differences

Four factors account for most of the regional variation in ADU costs. Understanding them helps a homeowner interpret their own local bids and avoid being misled by national figures.

**Labor rates.** This is the dominant variable. Construction labor — carpenters, electricians, plumbers, finish trades — is priced by local wage markets. Union-prevailing-wage markets like San Francisco and Boston carry labor rates 50–80% above national average. Non-union markets in the Southeast and Mountain West carry rates closer to the national median. When someone says "California is twice as expensive as Texas," they are mostly describing labor, not materials.

**Material costs.** Regional pricing for lumber, concrete, roofing, and mechanical equipment varies less than labor — these are commodity markets with national pricing. Transportation adds cost in remote markets (Hawaii, Alaska, rural Mountain West), and regional shortages during high-demand periods create local premiums. But as a general rule, material cost differences between, say, Atlanta and Seattle are modest compared to labor cost differences.

**Regulatory complexity.** More required consultants, more permit iterations, higher permit fees, and more inter-agency coordination all add cost. California requires a geotechnical report in many zones; Oregon requires a sewer lateral inspection; Seattle has a parallel utility application process. Each adds soft cost. Jurisdictions with simpler, more ministerial permit processes — many suburban and rural areas — reduce this category substantially. This is why a simple rural county in Oregon may cost less than a comparable project in Portland even with similar labor rates.

**Contractor capacity.** In markets where residential construction demand significantly exceeds contractor supply, GCs can price at a premium. They can also be selective about which projects they take, which means less competitive bidding and less pressure to sharpen pencils. The Puget Sound corridor, the San Francisco Bay Area, and Bend, Oregon are current examples of markets where capacity constraint is a meaningful cost driver independent of underlying wage rates. In more balanced markets — most Midwest and Southeast cities, secondary markets in most regions — competitive bidding keeps costs closer to true market rates.

## Data Currency Note

These ranges reflect 2025–2026 market conditions. Construction costs nationally moderated from the sharp peaks of 2021–2023, when materials supply chain disruptions and labor shortages drove 20–30% cost increases in some markets in a single year. As of 2025–2026, most markets have returned to more normal escalation rates, but costs remain elevated relative to pre-2020 baselines — the spikes did not fully reverse.

Verify all figures with current contractor bids in your specific market before committing to a project budget or financing plan. The most reliable cost data for any given project is a qualified contractor's detailed estimate based on actual drawings — not a per-square-foot rule of thumb, and not this table.
