## Market Context

The Pacific Northwest is not one market — it is a collection of markets with meaningfully different cost profiles, regulatory environments, and contractor capacity constraints. Understanding where your project falls in this spectrum is the first step toward a realistic budget.

The Puget Sound corridor (Seattle, Bellevue, Redmond, and surrounding communities in King and Snohomish counties) carries labor costs that rival Los Angeles and trail only the Bay Area among major West Coast markets. The construction boom of the past decade has tightened contractor capacity significantly, and experienced residential GCs in Seattle commonly carry two- to four-month booking lead times. This scarcity effect drives prices in addition to underlying wage rates.

Portland has historically been a more affordable construction market than Seattle, but that gap has narrowed as the city's growth has accelerated demand for residential construction services. The Portland metro — including Beaverton, Hillsboro, and Gresham — now prices similarly to the lower end of the Seattle market for comparable scope. Salem and Eugene offer meaningfully lower costs; contractors in those markets are less constrained and bid more competitively.

Bend, Oregon is an anomaly worth noting specifically. It is a smaller city with a high-demand residential real estate market, limited contractor capacity relative to demand, and geography that adds material transport costs. As a result, Bend prices often exceed Portland for comparable ADU work, despite being a fraction of the metro population.

Idaho — primarily Boise and the Treasure Valley — represents the fastest-growing ADU market in the region by percentage, driven by migration from higher-cost California and Washington markets. Construction costs are lower than PNW coastal cities, but the market is tightening. Eastern Washington (Spokane and the Tri-Cities) is similarly more affordable and less capacity-constrained than the Puget Sound corridor.

## Soft Costs (Pacific Northwest)

PNW soft costs are generally lower than California's equivalent category, driven by less complex energy compliance requirements and somewhat more streamlined permitting in smaller jurisdictions. Seattle and Portland are exceptions — both have robust plan check processes and permitting complexity that approaches the major California cities.

*As of 2025–2026. Ranges vary by project scope and jurisdiction.*

| Soft Cost Category | Typical Range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Architecture / design | $7,000–$25,000 | Full construction documents and CA on high end; plan-set-only service on low end |
| Structural engineering | $2,500–$7,000 | Required for detached ADUs; hillside and soft-soil sites add scope |
| Energy code compliance (WA: WAC 51-11C; OR: OEESC) | $1,000–$3,000 | Less complex than California's Title 24 in most cases; verify current code cycle [VERIFY: Confirm current WA and OR energy code versions and any 2025 updates] |
| Permit fees (WA / OR) | $1,500–$10,000 | Seattle and Portland on high end; rural county jurisdictions significantly lower |
| Geotechnical / soils report | $2,500–$6,000 | Required for hillside sites, wetland-adjacent properties, and fill areas; Seattle's glacial till presents specific conditions |
| Utility connection fees | $2,000–$8,000 | Varies by utility district and project scope |
| **Total soft costs (typical)** | **$15,000–$50,000** | Seattle and Portland projects with complex sites should assume upper end |

**Washington-specific notes:**
- Seattle requires a side sewer inspection before issuing ADU permits in most cases. This inspection — plus any required repairs — can add $2,000–$5,000 before construction begins, and is not always visible to homeowners planning their initial budget.
- Washington State's energy code (WAC 51-11C) has been updated on a code cycle that aligns with but is not identical to the International Energy Conservation Code. Verify current requirements with the local jurisdiction at permit intake.

**Oregon-specific notes:**
- Portland's Bureau of Development Services requires a sewer lateral inspection (through the Bureau of Environmental Services) before approving permits in many districts. Lateral repairs, if required, add cost that is difficult to estimate before inspection.
- Oregon's Residential Specialty Code and Oregon Energy Efficiency Specialty Code (OEESC) govern construction; local amendments can add requirements, particularly in Portland.
- Oregon's new ADU legislation has eased owner-occupancy and setback restrictions; verify current requirements with your specific jurisdiction, as local implementation is still evolving. [VERIFY: Confirm current status of Oregon HB 2001 implementation and any 2025 legislative updates.]

## Utility Costs (Pacific Northwest)

Utility costs in the Pacific Northwest follow the same pattern as California: highly variable depending on existing infrastructure, proximity to mains, and local utility requirements. The specific utility providers and their processes differ significantly from California.

*As of 2025–2026. Verify current fees and requirements with the applicable utility provider.*

| Utility Item | Typical Range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Electrical panel upgrade (if needed) | $4,500–$12,000 | Similar scope to California; Puget Sound Energy and Pacific Power territory standard |
| Sewer lateral inspection (WA/OR) | $500–$2,000 | Seattle side sewer inspection; Portland sewer lateral inspection — required before permitting in many areas |
| Sewer lateral repair or replacement | $5,000–$25,000 | If inspection reveals defects; cost depends on length and pipe material |
| Sewer extension (new connection) | $4,000–$18,000 | Distance-dependent; directional boring in paved rights-of-way adds cost |
| Water line extension | $2,500–$9,000 | Seattle Public Utilities and Portland Water Bureau have distinct processes and fee schedules |
| Gas service (if applicable) | $1,500–$5,000 | Puget Sound Energy (gas) and NW Natural (Portland) serve most urban areas; many new ADUs are all-electric |
| Trench work | $1,800–$7,000 | Per 50 linear feet; cost shared if multiple utilities run in same trench |
| **Total utility costs (if all work required)** | **$12,000–$50,000** | Garage conversions typically avoid extension costs; detached new construction on large lots faces upper-range exposure |

**Critical note for Seattle projects:** Seattle Public Utilities and Puget Sound Energy are separate utility providers with separate application processes, fee schedules, and lead times. A project requiring both sewer work (SPU) and electrical work (PSE) involves two parallel application tracks that do not coordinate with each other or with the Seattle Department of Construction and Inspections permitting process. Budget for this coordination complexity in both time and in the soft costs of whoever is managing the utility applications.

## Contingency Guidance

A **15% contingency** is appropriate for most Pacific Northwest ADU projects. This reflects:

- **Contractor capacity constraints in Puget Sound:** Even qualified GCs with good reputations build contingency into their own bids in a tight market. Change orders in the Seattle area are common and typically priced at a premium.
- **Site discovery conditions:** The PNW has significant geological variability — from Seattle's fill areas and liquefaction zones to Portland's clay-heavy soils and the region's generally high water table. Unexpected soil conditions are a frequent driver of change orders.
- **Utility coordination complexity:** Seattle's parallel utility application processes and Portland's sewer lateral inspection requirements create schedule risk that translates into cost risk.
- **Weather:** The PNW's extended rainy season is a genuine construction risk. Excavation and foundation work stalled by weather or requiring dewatering adds cost and schedule delay.

Projects in Bend, where contractor capacity is particularly constrained relative to demand, should budget 15–18% contingency. Spokane, Boise, and Eastern WA projects — with more competitive contractor markets and lower baseline costs — may be able to operate closer to 12–15%.
