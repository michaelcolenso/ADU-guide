# Chapter 1: The ADU Landscape in 2025-2026

Most homeowners think the hard part of an ADU is construction. In practice, the hard part is making good decisions before construction starts: what type to build, what it should cost in your market, and whether your local rules are actually as flexible as social media says they are.

## ADUs Are No Longer a Niche Product

ADUs moved from edge case to mainstream because state policy and local demand started aligning. California is still the clearest signal: the state reports annual ADU permits rising from 1,336 in 2016 to 26,924 in 2023, and ADUs made up more than 21% of all homes permitted statewide in 2023 [S1]. That is not a trend line you ignore.

Massachusetts is a newer example of the same shift. ADUs are now by-right statewide under its Affordable Homes Act framework, and the state reported 1,224 ADUs permitted or approved in the first year snapshot released on February 5, 2026 [S2].

Washington and Oregon show the same direction of travel with different legal mechanics. Washington requires ADU rules that allow at least two ADUs on many lots in fully planning jurisdictions, with state preemption if local rules conflict [S3]. Oregon requires cities over 2,500 and counties over 15,000 to allow at least one ADU in single-unit zones inside urban growth boundaries [S4].

At the national level, the exact state count depends on how you define "ADU law" (full by-right legalization vs. partial preemption), but reform is clearly widespread. Pew reported 18 states with ADU laws as of September 2025 [S5], and broader trackers may count more when partial reforms are included.

## What an ADU Is and the Four Types That Matter

An **accessory dwelling unit (ADU)** is an independent residence on the same lot as a primary home, with living, sleeping, cooking, and sanitation functions [S1]. In homeowner terms, there are four practical categories.

A **detached ADU** gives the best privacy and usually strongest rentability, but it typically carries the highest construction and utility-extension cost.

An **attached ADU** can save on envelope and utility routing, but layout compromises are common if the existing house geometry is awkward.

A **garage conversion ADU** is often the fastest path because you are reusing structure, but many projects still require expensive upgrades to slab, insulation, and utilities.

A **JADU** (junior ADU) is smaller and often lower-cost, but it is a different product with tighter size and program limits, so it does not fit every income or family-use strategy [S1].

The mistake is choosing type by vibe. Choose by constraints: lot geometry, utility path, budget ceiling, and intended use.

## A Conservative 2025-2026 Cost Snapshot

If you need a first-pass planning number, treat current market ranges as filters, not promises. Industry benchmarks still show wide spreads by type and region. Angi's 2025-2026 consumer cost data places many ADU projects broadly around $150 to $300 per square foot, with garage conversions often in lower bands and detached new construction in higher bands [S6].

For type-level planning, a conservative early screen is:

- Garage conversion: roughly $60,000 to $150,000 [S6]
- Attached new construction: roughly $100,000 to $216,000 [S6]
- Detached new construction: roughly $110,000 to $285,000 [S6]

These are not bid targets. They are feasibility filters. If your financing only works when a detached ADU lands at the lowest end of that range, the project is fragile.

## Who This Guide Is For

This guide is for homeowners making one of three moves: creating rental income, creating family housing, or creating flexibility for future life-stage changes. It is not written for speculative high-turnover development. The framework assumes you care about durability, code compliance, and long-term downside protection.

If you want a quick yes/no answer without doing zoning and utility homework, you are early. If you are willing to do that homework, you can avoid most six-figure mistakes.

Now that you understand the landscape, the next question is specific to your parcel: can your property actually support the ADU you have in mind without killing budget or timeline? That is Chapter 2.
