# Chapter 2: Feasibility Assessment - Can Your Property Support an ADU?

You can burn three months and $10,000 in design fees before learning your preferred ADU location does not work. This chapter is about avoiding that outcome.

## Start With Zoning, Not Sketches

Before any design conversation, confirm five things with your planning counter: ADU type allowed, maximum unit count, objective development standards, overlay constraints, and permit path.

In California, ADU applications are ministerial and must be approved or denied within 60 days once complete, for lots with existing primary dwellings [S1]. In plain English: cities can enforce objective standards, but they cannot drag your ADU into discretionary purgatory if your submittal is complete and compliant.

For homeowners outside California, the same principle applies even where legal details differ: identify objective standards first, because they decide whether your concept is viable before you spend money on full plans.

## Read Dimensional Rules Like a Builder

Most feasibility failures are dimensional, not conceptual. The biggest traps are setbacks, height, and lot coverage.

California's statewide framework allows local objective standards but blocks standards that make ADUs effectively impossible [S2]. Portland's ADU zoning page gives a useful example of how local rules become real constraints: detached ADUs are capped at 20 feet outside required setbacks, and coverage/location rules can eliminate otherwise logical siting options [S3]. Seattle's current guidance similarly ties ADU count, size, and placement to zone-specific standards [S4].

Your task is simple: identify one primary location that works with current standards and one backup location that also works. If you only have one viable footprint, your project carries more entitlement risk.

## Utility Capacity Is the Quiet Budget Killer

A site can pass zoning and still fail financially because of utilities. Sewer depth, lateral distance, electrical service capacity, and trenching constraints can move a project from feasible to marginal.

California law limits when local agencies can treat an ADU as a new residential utility connection and limits impact-fee treatment by size, including no impact fee for ADUs at or below 750 square feet of interior livable space [S5]. That helps, but it does not eliminate field realities like panel upgrades, trench conflicts, or mainline constraints.

This is where homeowners get hurt: bids look reasonable until utility scope hardens. Do a pre-design utility screen before finalizing layout.

## HOA, CC&R, and Deed Constraint Reality

Many homeowners assume state ADU policy automatically wipes out private restrictions. Sometimes it does not, or it does so only partially.

Washington now limits restrictions in many new common-interest communities and ties those limits to what cities/counties are allowed to require [S6]. Oregon law has also expanded limits on recorded instruments that prohibit ADU development in qualifying contexts [S7]. But legacy documents, enforcement posture, and local implementation details can still create risk.

Practical rule: read your governing documents early and confirm with local counsel when restrictions appear to conflict with current state law.

## Environmental and Historic Overlays

Floodplain, wildfire, coastal, habitat, and historic overlays can introduce additional review layers even when ADUs are broadly permitted. A project that is "allowed" may still be expensive, slow, or redesign-heavy under overlay rules.

Do not treat overlays as footnotes. They are feasibility variables.

## Your 30-Minute DIY Site Assessment

Walk the lot with a tape measure, parcel map, and phone photos. You are not doing design. You are testing risk.

1. Mark the likely ADU footprint and the backup footprint.
2. Measure clearances to side and rear property lines.
3. Note likely utility trench path from house to ADU location.
4. Locate panel, sewer cleanout, water meter, and major trees.
5. Photograph slope breaks, retaining walls, and access pinch points.
6. Call planning and verify standards against your marked footprints.

If steps 1-6 hold up, you are ready for schematic design and early pricing. If they do not, revise concept before spending more.
