# How to Use This Guide

This guide is organized around the sequence of decisions you will actually face — not the sequence that makes for a tidy outline. ADU projects have a natural lifecycle: you start by asking whether an ADU is even possible on your property, then move through design, financing, contractor selection, construction, and eventually monetization or occupancy. The five parts of this guide follow that lifecycle in order.

## The Five Parts

**Part I: Should You Build an ADU?** covers the decision-making that happens before you spend a dollar on design or permits. Chapter 1 gives you the landscape — what ADUs are, what they cost in broad ranges, and what the legislative environment looks like in 2025–2026. Chapter 2 is the feasibility assessment: zoning, setbacks, lot coverage, utility infrastructure, and the HOA and CC&R complications that catch homeowners off guard. Chapter 3 makes the financial case — or, when the numbers don't support it, the financial argument against. Read all three chapters before you hire anyone.

**Part II: Design and Planning** covers ADU type selection, design fundamentals, and the state-by-state regulatory reference that is the most jurisdiction-specific content in the guide. Chapter 4 helps you choose between a detached ADU, attached addition, garage conversion, or JADU based on your constraints — not based on what looks best on Instagram. Chapter 5 is the regulatory reference, organized by state, covering the legislation, key provisions, and city-level details that affect your permit process.

**Part III: Financing Your ADU** is a single chapter because financing is the decision most homeowners get wrong by choosing the first option they encounter rather than the right option for their situation. Chapter 6 covers HELOCs, home equity loans, construction loans, Fannie Mae and FHA products, state and local programs, and cash-out refinancing — with a side-by-side comparison and specific questions to ask every lender before you apply.

**Part IV: Building Your ADU** is the operational core of the guide. Chapters 7 through 10 cover contractor selection and vetting, the permit process, construction management, and cost control. This is where most of the money is made or lost, and it's where the guide is most detailed. If you're already past the design stage and heading into bids and permits, this is where to start.

**Part V: After Construction** covers the final inspection process, certificate of occupancy, closeout procedures, and the monetization options for your finished unit — long-term rental, short-term rental, multigenerational use, and the emerging condo conversion pathway in California.

## How to Navigate Based on Where You Are

If you are in the early exploration phase — you have an idea but haven't verified zoning or talked to anyone yet — read Part I in sequence. Don't skip Chapter 2. The feasibility work most homeowners consider optional is the most valuable work in the process.

If you are further along — you've verified basic feasibility, maybe gotten some design sketches — go to Chapter 4 to confirm your ADU type decision, then read Chapter 6 on financing before you commit to a design scope that doesn't match what you can actually borrow.

If you have a design in hand and you're heading into permits and bids, read Chapters 7 and 8 together. The contractor selection process and the permit process overlap more than most homeowners expect: permit timeline affects bid price locks, and your contractor's permit experience in your specific jurisdiction can dramatically affect your timeline.

If you're already under construction and something isn't going well, go directly to Chapter 9 for the construction management framework. The draw schedule management section and the change order evaluation process are the two most likely places where money is being lost.

## Which Chapters to Prioritize for Quick Decisions

Three chapters in this guide protect more money per hour of reading than any others:

**Chapter 2** (Feasibility Assessment) prevents the most common expensive mistake: spending $10,000–$30,000 on design before discovering the project isn't viable on your specific lot.

**Chapter 7** (Contractor Selection) covers the vetting process and contract essentials. Signing a bad construction contract is one of the few project mistakes that cannot be corrected during construction — only survived.

**Chapter 10** (Budgeting and Cost Control) explains how to build a budget that won't collapse under change orders and unforeseen conditions. The contingency framework alone has saved more than one project from financial crisis.

Read those three chapters carefully even if you skim everything else. They represent the highest leverage per page in the guide.
