# Chapter 6: Financing Your ADU

Most homeowners approach ADU financing the same way they approach contractor selection: they ask one or two obvious sources, pick the first thing that sounds reasonable, and assume the rest will sort itself out. It does not sort itself out. Financing decisions made in the first week of a project frequently constrain or distort every decision that follows — how much you can build, what type of ADU you pursue, whether you can absorb a cost overrun, and how long the project stays financially viable. This chapter gives you the framework to choose the right instrument, understand what lenders actually look for, sequence draws to match construction milestones, and avoid the mistakes that routinely cost homeowners $10,000–$30,000 in unnecessary interest, fees, or lost equity.

The financial case for building an ADU is examined in depth in Chapter 3. What that chapter establishes — and what shapes every decision in this chapter — is that an ADU is a capital deployment decision. You are investing real dollars in a long-lived asset. The financing instrument you choose is not just an administrative detail; it determines your cost of capital, your risk exposure, your flexibility during construction, and your ability to weather surprises.

## Your Starting Point: How Much Equity Do You Have?

Before comparing products, you need a clear-eyed equity number. **Usable equity** is not your home's market value minus your mortgage balance. It is the amount most lenders will allow you to borrow against, which is typically capped at 80%–85% of the home's appraised value, minus what you already owe. The formula:

- Appraised value × 0.80 (or 0.85, depending on lender) = maximum combined loan balance
- Maximum combined loan balance minus current mortgage balance = usable equity

If your home is worth $700,000 and you owe $350,000, your maximum combined balance at 80% LTV (loan-to-value) is $560,000. That leaves roughly $210,000 in usable equity — enough to fund a modest ADU without additional sources. If you owe $520,000 on that same home, your usable equity drops to $40,000, which will not fund a standalone construction project without other sources.

This math is the first filter. Get a current appraisal or strong comparables before you begin lender conversations. A $50,000 difference in your appraised value can change your eligible loan amount by more than you expect, and it affects whether you qualify at all for some products.

## Cash: The Cleanest Option, With Real Trade-offs

Paying cash for your ADU eliminates the most complex variable in the project — the lender. There are no draw schedules to manage, no rate exposure, no qualification process, and no monthly payment obligation while you are also managing a construction project. If you have the liquidity, cash is worth serious consideration.

The trade-off is opportunity cost and liquidity risk. Deploying $200,000 of investable assets into a construction project means that capital is not compounding elsewhere, and once committed, it is illiquid for the duration of construction and beyond. Most financial advisors would not recommend liquidating all available reserves; keeping six to twelve months of household expenses in liquid form alongside a project contingency reserve is prudent. Cash financing often makes sense for homeowners with substantial net worth who can fund the project without depleting their liquidity position — or for projects where the construction cost is modest enough that it does not strain reserves.

If cash is part of your plan, the sequencing advice in the draw schedule section of this chapter still applies. Self-funded projects are just as prone to cash-flow mismanagement as lender-funded ones. Releasing money too early to a contractor, without tying payments to completed inspected work, is a common and painful mistake regardless of the funding source.

## Home Equity Line of Credit (HELOC)

A **HELOC** is a revolving line of credit secured by your home, typically structured with a draw period of five to ten years during which you can borrow, repay, and re-borrow, followed by a repayment period of ten to twenty years. The interest rate is almost always variable, tied to the prime rate plus a margin [VERIFY: confirm current prime-plus-margin range for HELOCs with major lenders], which means your cost fluctuates with market conditions.

HELOCs typically carry interest rates in the range of prime plus 0.5%–2%, which as of early 2026 translates roughly to 8%–11% depending on lender and borrower profile [VERIFY: confirm current HELOC rate range]. Maximum draw amounts are usually 80%–85% of home value minus existing mortgage balance, and most lenders require a credit score of 680 or higher, though 720+ generally gets better terms.

The structural advantage of a HELOC for ADU financing is its flexibility. You draw only what you need, when you need it, which means you are not paying interest on the full project cost from day one. During the permit and design phase — which can run three to nine months before any physical construction begins — you may draw very little. As construction ramps up, you draw more. This matches your interest expense to your actual cash needs rather than front-loading financing costs.

The structural risk is the variable rate. A project that pencils out at 8.5% becomes materially more expensive if rates move to 10.5% during an eighteen-month construction timeline. If you choose a HELOC, model the project at rates 2%–3% higher than the current rate, and make sure it still works. The worst-case scenario is a HELOC drawn near its limit, a rate spike mid-construction, and a project that runs over budget — that combination is a serious financial stress event for most households.

HELOCs work best for homeowners with strong equity positions, strong credit, and construction timelines short enough to limit rate exposure. They are also well-suited when you are uncertain of your final cost and want the ability to draw in stages without refinancing or re-qualifying.

## Home Equity Loan (Second Mortgage)

A **home equity loan** gives you a lump sum at a fixed interest rate, secured by your home as a second mortgage. Unlike a HELOC, the rate is locked at closing — typically in the range of 7%–10% as of early 2026 [VERIFY: confirm current home equity loan rate range]. You begin making principal-and-interest payments immediately on the full balance, regardless of how much of the loan you have deployed into construction.

The fixed-rate structure is the home equity loan's primary advantage for ADU financing. It eliminates rate exposure and makes your monthly payment predictable for the life of the loan. If you have a well-defined project scope and a reliable cost estimate, and if the current rate environment feels like a reasonable long-term carry cost, the home equity loan provides stability that a HELOC does not.

The drawback is the upfront interest burden. If you close a $200,000 home equity loan six months before construction starts, you are paying interest on $200,000 during design, permitting, and pre-construction — a period when that money is sitting idle. For a project with a long permitting runway, this can add thousands of dollars to your financing cost with no corresponding benefit. The home equity loan is most efficient when the timeline from closing to construction start is short and the project scope is well-defined.

Both HELOCs and home equity loans leave your primary mortgage in place, which makes them efficient for borrowers who refinanced at low rates in earlier years and do not want to disturb that position. If your current mortgage is at 3%–4%, preserving it is a significant financial benefit worth protecting.

## Cash-Out Refinance

A **cash-out refinance** replaces your existing mortgage with a new, larger mortgage and returns the difference to you in cash at closing. If you owe $300,000 on a home worth $700,000, you might refinance into a $500,000 mortgage and receive $200,000 in cash — net of fees — to fund construction.

The critical variable with a cash-out refinance is what it does to your existing mortgage rate. In 2021 and 2022, many homeowners locked 30-year mortgages in the 2.75%–3.5% range. Refinancing those mortgages today, when 30-year rates are in the 6.5%–8% range [VERIFY: confirm current 30-year fixed mortgage rate range], means accepting a rate roughly double your current rate on your entire primary mortgage balance — not just on the new money. That trade-off can be extremely expensive over a 30-year hold.

The math matters: if you owe $400,000 at 3.25% and you refinance into $600,000 at 7.25%, you are paying a higher rate on $400,000 that you already had, not just on the $200,000 you are extracting. Over thirty years, the additional interest on the existing balance alone can exceed $150,000 [VERIFY: validate with amortization modeling]. For homeowners who refinanced at historically low rates, a cash-out refinance is often the worst available financing option for an ADU — it is essentially a financing tax on an existing asset.

The cash-out refinance is most attractive when your current mortgage rate is close to or above current market rates, or when the simplicity of a single monthly payment has strong value to you and the rate trade-off is modest. It can also make sense when you are refinancing for other reasons — dropping PMI, shortening the loan term — and the ADU financing is opportunistically bundled with a refinance you were planning anyway.

## Construction Loan

A **construction loan** is a short-term loan — typically twelve to eighteen months — designed specifically to fund construction, with funds disbursed in stages tied to completed work rather than as a lump sum at closing. Interest is usually charged only on the outstanding balance, and rates are typically variable, in the range of 7%–11% [VERIFY: confirm current construction loan rate range]. At the end of the construction period, the loan converts to permanent financing (a "construction-to-permanent" or "C2P" loan) or is paid off with a new mortgage.

The draw schedule is the defining feature of a construction loan. Rather than giving you or your contractor a lump sum, the lender releases funds in tranches tied to inspected milestones — foundation complete, framing complete, rough-in complete, drywall complete, and so on. Each draw typically requires an inspection by the lender's representative before funds are released. This structure protects both the lender and the borrower: it ensures money flows proportional to completed, verified work.

For borrowers without substantial existing equity — perhaps a recently purchased home where the equity has not yet accumulated — a construction loan can access value that a HELOC or home equity loan cannot, because the appraiser considers the future-completed value of the property ("as-improved" value) rather than only the current state. This is one of the few instruments that can fund an ADU without requiring substantial pre-existing equity.

The qualification process for construction loans is more demanding than for equity products. Lenders typically require detailed project plans, a signed contract with a licensed general contractor (GC), a construction budget, and a timeline. Expect documentation requirements similar to a home purchase, plus project-specific underwriting. Rates are higher than home equity products to compensate for construction risk, and many community banks and credit unions are more active in this space than large national lenders — your first calls should include the institutions that already know your market and the local construction environment.

## FHA 203(k) and Fannie Mae HomeStyle

Two government-backed products are specifically designed for renovation and construction financing: the **FHA 203(k)** loan and the **Fannie Mae HomeStyle** loan. Both wrap the cost of construction into a single mortgage that covers purchase or refinance plus renovation, which simplifies the financing picture for eligible borrowers.

The FHA 203(k) is a Federal Housing Administration-insured mortgage for owner-occupied properties. The Standard 203(k) allows significant structural work, including ADU construction, while the Limited 203(k) — sometimes called the Streamline — caps eligible repairs at $35,000 and is typically not sufficient for ADU construction [VERIFY: confirm current FHA 203(k) loan limits and program structure]. FHA loans require mortgage insurance regardless of down payment size, which adds ongoing cost. Maximum loan limits are tied to FHA county loan limits, which vary by metro area, and as of 2025–2026 range from roughly $498,257 in lower-cost areas to $1,149,825 in high-cost markets [VERIFY: confirm current FHA loan limit figures]. The borrower must occupy the primary residence, which makes this product unavailable for investment-only ADU projects.

The Fannie Mae HomeStyle renovation loan is a conventional (non-FHA) product that allows renovation financing of up to 75% of the "as-completed" value of the property [VERIFY: confirm current HomeStyle LTV limit and program terms]. It is available for primary residences, second homes, and investment properties, which gives it more flexibility than the 203(k). Rates are typically better than FHA because there is no mortgage insurance requirement for borrowers who can reach 20% equity on the as-completed value. The HomeStyle requires that renovation plans be approved by the lender at closing, and funds are managed through a draw process similar to a construction loan.

Both products require working with a lender approved to originate them, and not all lenders offer them. HomeStyle in particular tends to be available through larger banks and specialty renovation lenders. If you are buying a property with the intention of adding an ADU, these products can be powerful because they allow you to finance both the acquisition and the construction in a single closing — avoiding the cost and friction of sequential financing.

## State and Local Programs

A growing number of states, counties, and cities have created ADU-specific financing programs, ranging from low-interest construction loans to forgivable grants. These programs vary enormously in structure, availability, and funding levels, and they change frequently as legislatures fund, defund, or restructure them. The most relevant programs for buyers of this guide include:

**California:** The California Housing Finance Agency (CalHFA) has offered ADU grant programs in recent years, including a program that provided up to $40,000 in pre-development cost assistance [VERIFY: confirm current CalHFA ADU grant program status, amount, and eligibility — programs have changed year to year]. Several California cities — including Los Angeles, Sacramento, and San Jose — have also operated local ADU loan programs. Check CalHFA's current ADU program page and your city's housing department before assuming any specific program is active.

**Other states:** Oregon, Washington, Massachusetts, and Colorado have each had periods of state or local ADU financing assistance, often tied to the legislative reforms described in Chapter 5. The specifics change faster than a guide can track. The most reliable approach is to contact your state's housing finance agency directly and ask what ADU-specific programs are currently accepting applications.

The value of state and local programs is not just the money — it is often the technical assistance and pre-approval pathway that comes with them. A city that runs an ADU loan program has typically also streamlined the permit process for loan recipients. If your jurisdiction has an active program, it is worth pursuing before committing to market-rate financing.

## ADU-Specific and Alternative Lenders

A small but growing category of lenders specializes specifically in ADU construction financing, including companies that have built underwriting models around ADU rental income and California's as-of-right ADU law [VERIFY: verify current ADU-specific lender landscape, including any since ceased operations or changed products]. These lenders typically offer construction-to-permanent loans structured around ADU timelines and may be more flexible than conventional lenders in underwriting ADU rental income as part of the qualifying analysis.

If you have unusual circumstances — limited equity, non-traditional income, a jurisdiction with expedited ADU permitting — an ADU-specific lender may qualify you for a project that a conventional bank would decline. The trade-off is that specialty lenders sometimes carry higher rates or fees than bank portfolio products, and the field is newer and less regulated than conventional mortgage lending. Vet any ADU-specific lender carefully: check state licensing, read the term sheet in detail, and compare the all-in cost against conventional options before committing.

**Shared equity arrangements** — where an investor provides capital in exchange for a percentage of future appreciation or rental income — are another alternative for homeowners who lack sufficient equity or income to qualify for debt financing. These arrangements are complex, often illiquid, and require careful legal review. They are a last resort for most homeowners, not a first option.

**Family loans** are structurally simple but emotionally complex. If you borrow from family, document the loan with a promissory note, charge at least the IRS-required minimum applicable federal rate [VERIFY: confirm current AFR rates] to avoid gift tax implications, and make payments on a clear schedule. A family loan that is not documented like a real loan creates tax exposure and relationship risk. The IRS minimum rates are published monthly; a tax professional can walk you through the current requirements.

## The ADU Appraisal and Its Relationship to Your Loan

The appraised value of your property — both current and post-construction — is the foundation of most ADU financing. Lenders will not lend against a value higher than what a licensed appraiser confirms.

**Current appraisal** sets your available equity for HELOC, home equity loan, and cash-out refinance products. A current appraisal is strongly recommended before you engage lenders, rather than relying on automated valuation models (AVMs) or Zillow estimates, which can be off by 10%–20% in either direction.

**As-improved appraisal** is used by construction loans and renovation products (HomeStyle, 203k) to estimate what the property will be worth after the ADU is complete. The appraiser analyzes comparable properties with ADUs — called "comps" — and estimates value based on the ADU's contribution to the overall property. In markets where ADU comps are limited, the appraisal can be conservative, which sometimes constrains loan amounts to less than the project requires. If your lender is using as-improved value, ask the appraiser's methodology upfront and understand that the appraised value is an estimate, not a guarantee.

One important nuance: appraised value and actual market value are not always the same, especially in rapidly changing ADU markets. If you believe your as-improved appraisal is low, you can challenge it by providing better comps or requesting a reconsideration of value — but start this conversation early, not after construction begins.

## Sequencing Financing Around Construction Milestones

Construction does not cost money evenly. The draw schedule — the payment plan tied to specific completed milestones — is how lenders manage this reality, and you need to understand it regardless of which financing product you use.

A typical ADU construction draw schedule looks something like this:

1. **Pre-construction draw (10%–15%):** Covers design completion, permit fees, materials deposits, and mobilization costs. Released before or immediately at construction start.
2. **Foundation draw (15%–20%):** Released after foundation is complete and inspected.
3. **Framing draw (20%–25%):** Released after framing, roof sheathing, and rough window/door installation.
4. **Rough-in draw (15%–20%):** Released after electrical, plumbing, and HVAC rough-in is inspected and approved.
5. **Drywall/close-in draw (10%–15%):** Released after insulation, drywall hang, and close-up.
6. **Final draw (10%–15%):** Released after certificate of occupancy (CO) — the official sign-off from the building department confirming the structure is complete and safe for occupancy — and completion of the contractor's punch list.

The percentages above are illustrative ranges. Your lender's specific draw schedule, and your contractor's payment terms, will determine the actual structure. The important principle is that **you should never be in a position where you have paid more to your contractor than the value of work completed and inspected.** A contractor who front-loads payments — asking for 50% upfront, for example — is a red flag. See Chapter 7 for the full contractor vetting framework.

If you are using a HELOC or cash rather than a construction loan, you need to impose the same draw discipline on yourself. Release money to your contractor in tranches tied to completed, inspected milestones. Your contractor's cash flow needs are their problem to manage; your obligation is to pay for work that has been done, verified, and accepted.

## Comparing Lenders: What to Ask and Why

Getting one financing quote is not due diligence. ADU financing is a market, and rates, fees, and terms vary materially across lenders. For a $200,000 loan, a 0.5% rate difference is $1,000 per year in interest — over a ten-year payoff period, that is $10,000. It is worth spending two hours comparing offers.

When comparing lenders, focus on:

- **Interest rate** (fixed or variable, and if variable, the index and margin)
- **APR** (annual percentage rate, which includes fees and gives a better apples-to-apples comparison than rate alone)
- **Origination fees** (typically 0.5%–2% of the loan amount)
- **Closing costs** (appraisal, title, recording, prepaid interest)
- **Draw process** (how many draws are allowed, who inspects, and how long releases take)
- **Prepayment penalties** (some construction-to-permanent loans have them)
- **Conversion terms** (for construction-to-permanent products, what are the terms of the permanent phase)

Get Loan Estimate forms from at least three lenders before choosing. Federal law requires lenders to provide a standardized Loan Estimate within three business days of receiving a complete application [VERIFY: confirm current Loan Estimate regulatory requirement]. These forms make direct comparison straightforward.

Community banks, credit unions, and regional lenders are often more competitive for construction loans and home equity products than large national banks. They tend to have more flexibility on terms and more underwriters familiar with local construction markets. If your project is in a high-ADU-activity market like Los Angeles, Seattle, or Portland, there are likely lenders who have underwritten dozens of similar projects and can move faster than a national bank's standard process.

## What to Do When Your Equity Is Not Sufficient

If your equity position does not support the financing you need, you have several options — none of them simple.

The first is to reduce the project scope. A 400 square foot attached ADU or a garage conversion costs significantly less than a 800 square foot detached ADU, and may be financeable with your current equity. See Chapter 4 for the design trade-offs.

The second is to wait and build equity. If your home is appreciating and you are paying down your mortgage, your equity position improves over time. A project that is not financeable today may be straightforward in two or three years. This is not a failure — it is responsible timing.

The third is to explore state and local programs, which sometimes operate outside conventional LTV limits. A city ADU loan program may lend based on different criteria than a private lender.

The fourth is as-improved financing through a construction loan or HomeStyle, which bases the loan on the post-construction value rather than the current value. This can bridge a gap where current equity is thin but the as-improved value supports the project.

The fifth — seller financing, shared equity, investor partnerships — should be approached with significant caution and legal review. These structures can work in specific circumstances, but they are not mainstream, not standardized, and often carry terms that are difficult to exit.

## Common Financing Mistakes

The most common and costly financing mistake is treating the loan as a supply of money rather than a structured facility. Homeowners who draw their HELOC ahead of construction milestones, or who pay contractors in lump sums without tying payments to inspected work, frequently run out of money before the project is complete. An 80%-funded, un-occupiable ADU is a liability, not an asset.

The second most common mistake is underestimating financing costs in the project budget. Interest during construction, origination fees, appraisal costs, and closing costs typically add 2%–5% to the total project cost [VERIFY: validate typical all-in financing cost as percentage of project cost]. If your project budget is $200,000 and you have not modeled financing costs, your actual cost is likely $204,000–$210,000 at minimum.

The third is choosing a financing product based on the headline rate without understanding the structure. A HELOC with a lower starting rate than a fixed home equity loan may cost more over the life of the project if rates move upward, or if you carry the balance for longer than expected.

The fourth is failing to lock in rate before prices are committed. If you get contractor bids based on a financing cost assumption, and then rates move 1%–2% before you close, your pro forma is wrong. Close your financing before signing a construction contract.

## Decision Framework: Choosing Your Path

The right financing instrument depends on five variables: your existing equity, your current mortgage rate, the construction timeline, your risk tolerance for variable rates, and your qualification profile. This framework walks through the major decision points:

**You have strong equity and a low existing mortgage rate (under 5%):** Preserve the primary mortgage. Use a HELOC if you want flexibility and can tolerate rate exposure, or a home equity loan if you want rate certainty. Do not consider a cash-out refinance unless rates fall substantially from current levels.

**You have strong equity and your current mortgage rate is near or above market rates:** A cash-out refinance may make sense. The rate trade-off is modest and the simplicity of a single payment has real value. Compare the all-in cost against a home equity product before deciding.

**You have limited equity but strong income and credit:** Explore construction loans and HomeStyle renovation loans, which underwrite to as-improved value. Budget for a more demanding qualification process.

**You are in a high-ADU-activity state (California, Oregon, Washington, Massachusetts, Colorado):** Check state and local programs before committing to market-rate financing. A grant or below-market loan can materially change your project economics.

**Your qualification profile is non-standard (self-employed, recent job change, high debt-to-income):** Talk to an ADU-specific lender or a mortgage broker who specializes in renovation financing before assuming conventional bank products are available to you.

**You have sufficient liquidity:** Model the cash option seriously. The absence of financing costs and complexity is worth real money, especially on shorter-timeline projects.
