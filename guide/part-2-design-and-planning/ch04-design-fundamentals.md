# Chapter 4 — ADU Design Fundamentals

Most homeowners start their ADU design process by browsing Instagram for floor plans they like. That is the wrong starting point, and it produces a predictable set of expensive mistakes. The right starting point is constraints: what your lot allows, what your budget ceiling is, and what the ADU actually needs to do. Good design in this context is not about aesthetics — it is about fitting a maximum amount of livability into a set of real constraints without spending money you don't have to spend. Aesthetics come later, and they cost less than most people assume.

This chapter covers the four ADU types and their real tradeoffs, how to choose between them, how to work productively with the professionals who will translate your constraints into a buildable design, what documents you actually need before you can apply for a permit, and the design mistakes that are common enough and expensive enough that they deserve explicit treatment before you start spending money on drawings.

## Section 1: The Four ADU Types and Their Real Tradeoffs

Chapter 1 introduced the four ADU categories at a high level. Now we need to go deeper, because choosing the right type for your property is a financial decision, not an aesthetic one. Each type carries a different cost profile, a different set of site constraints, and a different livability outcome. Most homeowners who end up disappointed with their ADU made this choice early based on what they found appealing, not what their property and goals actually supported.

### Detached ADU

A **detached ADU** is an entirely separate structure, physically disconnected from the main house, typically built in the backyard. It offers the best combination of privacy, acoustic separation, and rentability — which is why it is the most popular type in markets where lot geometry allows it.

The tradeoffs are real. A detached ADU requires its own foundation, its own roof, its own exterior envelope, and often its own utility service connections. You are building a small house from scratch. In West Coast markets, stick-built detached ADUs of 600–800 square feet typically run $175,000–$300,000 all-in, including soft costs [VERIFY — update with current regional benchmarks]. In Mountain West and Midwest markets, ranges often run lower, roughly $120,000–$220,000, though those figures are sensitive to local labor rates and current materials pricing. The premium over other ADU types reflects the completeness of the structure: you are not leveraging any existing construction.

Detached ADUs also require the most site clearance. You need adequate rear yard depth to meet setback requirements, enough clear area for the structure footprint plus required side yard clearances, and a drivable or walkable path for construction equipment and material delivery. Lots under 5,000 square feet in urban infill markets frequently cannot accommodate a detached ADU at all — not because the zoning doesn't allow it, but because the math on setbacks and lot coverage leaves no buildable footprint.

The rental income case for a detached ADU is typically the strongest of any ADU type. Full privacy, a separate entrance, no shared walls, and independent utilities produce the most tenant-attractive product. If your goal is long-term rental income and your lot can support the footprint, the detached ADU almost always pencils better over a ten-year horizon despite its higher upfront cost.

### Attached ADU

An **attached ADU** shares at least one wall with the primary residence. It might be a new addition off the back of the house, a conversion of an attached garage with additional square footage added, or an upper-floor addition over an existing attached garage. It is physically connected to the main structure, which creates both cost opportunities and complications.

The cost opportunity is in the shared wall: you eliminate one full exterior wall worth of framing, insulation, sheathing, siding, and weatherproofing. On a small structure, that can represent meaningful savings — often $15,000–$35,000 compared to a fully detached design of the same square footage [VERIFY]. The complication is that the shared wall creates acoustic and privacy challenges that require specific design attention. Sound transmission between the main house and the ADU is a livability issue for both your household and your tenant. Proper design requires at minimum a double-stud or staggered-stud party wall assembly with acoustic insulation — a detail that should be called out explicitly in your design documents, not left to the framing crew to figure out.

Utility connections for an attached ADU are often simpler and cheaper than a detached unit because you can route new plumbing and electrical through shared interior spaces rather than running new service from the street. Depending on your existing house's mechanical capacity — panel amperage, water heater output, HVAC system — you may be able to share systems or extend them rather than installing entirely new infrastructure.

The limitation of the attached configuration is layout flexibility. Your design is constrained by the geometry and structural logic of the existing house. You cannot always put the kitchen, bedroom, and bath where they would be optimal, because the attachment point, the load-bearing walls, and the existing utility locations in the house create a kind of pre-existing pressure on the floor plan. Attached ADUs frequently produce more awkward layouts than detached units at the same size.

### Garage Conversion ADU

A **garage conversion** repurposes an existing attached or detached garage into a habitable ADU. It is often the fastest and cheapest path to an ADU, and that combination of speed and cost makes it appealing in ways that can obscure the real scope of work required.

The garage structure exists, which is the starting point for the cost savings. But the gap between what a garage is built to and what a code-compliant habitable space requires is significant. Garages are typically built to lower standards than living space: minimal insulation, no thermal envelope to speak of, concrete slabs that may not be sealed or vapor-controlled, no finished plumbing, and electrical service designed for a few circuits of lighting and outlets rather than a full kitchen, bathroom, and bedroom. Bringing a garage up to habitable standards means adding insulation throughout, upgrading the slab or adding a subfloor system, running full plumbing, installing a complete HVAC system, upgrading the electrical panel or adding a subpanel, and replacing the garage door opening with a framed wall with window and door.

All-in costs for a garage conversion ADU typically run $80,000–$175,000, with the range driven by the size of the garage, the condition of the existing structure, and the extent of utility upgrades required [VERIFY — current regional benchmarks]. The low end of that range requires a large, well-built garage in good condition, a straightforward utility upgrade path, and a jurisdiction that is not adding significant impact fees. The high end applies to smaller garages with more complex upgrades, or markets with higher labor costs.

One specific complication for detached garage conversions is utility connections. If the garage is currently on its own electrical circuit from the main panel but has no plumbing, you will need to run water supply and drain lines from the house — or from the street if the distance or grade makes a house connection impractical. In some lots, the distance and grade between the garage and the main sewer line make a gravity-drain sewer connection difficult or impossible, which can require a sewage ejector pump (adding $3,000–$8,000 to the project) or a re-evaluation of the whole approach. This is exactly the kind of issue a site feasibility assessment catches before you've spent money on design.

The parking complication is real in some jurisdictions. Converting a garage to an ADU removes your covered parking. Many jurisdictions — particularly in car-dependent suburban markets — require replacement parking when garage parking is eliminated, which can mean adding a carport or paved parking pad and significantly reducing the cost savings of the garage conversion strategy. California eliminated replacement parking requirements for ADU garage conversions in most circumstances starting in 2020; Washington and Oregon have similar protections. Other states may not. Check this early in your feasibility process, not after you've paid for design drawings.

### Junior ADU (JADU)

A **junior ADU**, or JADU, is a conversion of space within the existing primary residence itself — a bedroom suite, finished basement area, or interior room with an entrance. It is the most constrained ADU type: California limits JADUs to 500 square feet [VERIFY against current state-level rules], and most states that recognize the category impose similar size ceilings. The JADU must have its own entrance (which can be through the primary dwelling's existing entrance, with an interior connecting door permitted), a kitchenette with food preparation capacity, and its own sanitation facilities that may be shared with the main house if a shared bathroom is accessible from the JADU's entrance.

The cost advantage is significant. Because a JADU repurposes existing conditioned space within the main structure, the work is essentially a heavy interior renovation: adding a kitchenette, potentially adding or modifying a bathroom, adding an exterior entrance if one doesn't already exist, and meeting the minimum habitability requirements. Well-executed JADUs can be built for $40,000–$100,000 in most markets, a fraction of what a detached or attached ADU of comparable functionality costs.

The limitation is the use case. A JADU of 400–500 square feet within your main house is a different product from any other ADU type. The tenant is, by definition, in very close proximity to the primary household — sharing a wall at minimum, and in many configurations sharing a hallway or entrance sequence. This is well-suited to family housing (a college-aged child returning home, an aging parent who wants proximity but some independence) and less suited to tenant rental where full privacy is a priority. Owner-occupancy requirements — where the homeowner must live in either the primary dwelling or the JADU — still apply to JADUs in many jurisdictions that have eliminated or relaxed them for standard ADUs. Check your local rules carefully.

## Section 2: Matching ADU Type to Your Property and Goals

The framework for this decision has three legs: what your site allows, what your budget ceiling is, and what the ADU needs to do. All three matter, and the right choice is always the intersection of all three — not the type you find most appealing before you've done this analysis.

Start with site constraints, because they are the least negotiable. If your rear yard depth after setbacks leaves you with a buildable envelope of 18 by 20 feet, a detached ADU becomes a space-planning exercise in small-structure efficiency. If you don't have a garage to convert, that option is off the table. Run your site constraints first using the feasibility assessment framework from Chapter 2. This eliminates the types that simply don't fit before you spend money on design.

Budget ceiling is the second filter. If your financing tops out at $120,000 (see Chapter 6 for the financing options most commonly used for ADUs at different budget levels), a detached ADU in a high-labor-cost market is probably not available to you — not because you can't build one, but because the cost to build one that actually meets your quality and livability standards exceeds what you can finance. This is not a failure of the strategy; it is a constraint that points you toward garage conversion or JADU.

Use case is the third filter, and it requires honesty about what you actually need. Rental income prioritizes privacy, acoustic separation, and independent access — which favors detached, and secondarily attached. Family housing has more tolerance for proximity and shared infrastructure — which opens up JADU or attached configurations that wouldn't work for unrelated tenants. If you are building for a specific family member with mobility considerations, that adds a design layer around accessibility and universal design, which is covered below.

When the constraints are genuinely ambiguous — your lot can support either a detached unit or a garage conversion, and both fit your budget — run the ten-year financial comparison. A detached ADU will typically command $200–$400 more per month in rent than a garage conversion of the same size in the same market [VERIFY]. Over ten years, that rent differential is $24,000–$48,000. If the detached ADU costs $60,000–$80,000 more to build than the garage conversion, you are looking at a 15–20 year payback on the premium — which may or may not make sense depending on your time horizon and how you value the certainty of the higher-quality rental product.

## Section 3: Working with Designers, Architects, and Design-Build Firms

Once you know what type of ADU you're building, the next decision is who designs it. This choice matters more than most homeowners expect. The quality of your design documents — specifically their completeness and buildability — will have a direct effect on your construction cost, your permit approval timeline, and the number of change orders your contractor generates during construction. An experienced ADU designer who has permitted dozens of projects in your jurisdiction is producing documents that the local building department knows how to process. A less experienced designer producing their first ADU in your city is producing a learning experience that may cost you several months and several rounds of plan check comments.

### Pre-Approved Plans

Before engaging any design professional, check whether your jurisdiction offers **pre-approved ADU plan sets** — standardized designs that have already been reviewed and approved by the building department, requiring only a site-specific review before permit issuance. Los Angeles has operated a standard plan program since 2019, and the ADU Express program has substantially shortened the plan check timeline for qualifying designs [S1]. Seattle maintains pre-approved plan options. San Jose, Sacramento, and several other California cities have developed similar programs. Oregon has a state-level pre-approved plan program open to all jurisdictions. [VERIFY — confirm current status of Oregon state pre-approved plan program]

Pre-approved plans are not for every project, but they are worth evaluating early. If your lot's geometry, setbacks, and utility configuration can accommodate a standard plan, you can potentially cut 8–16 weeks from your design-to-permit timeline, pay lower design fees, and start with documents that the building department has already blessed. The tradeoff is exactly what the name implies: the design is standardized, not tailored to your specific preferences or site conditions. Some homeowners find the available designs perfectly adequate for their goals; others find them constraining enough to justify custom design. Evaluate the available plans in your jurisdiction before assuming you need custom drawings.

### Hiring an Architect

An architect licensed in your state brings the broadest design capability and the most comprehensive professional responsibility to the project. For a complex lot — significant grade change, unusual geometry, structural constraints, or a design with specific aesthetic requirements — a licensed architect is often the right choice.

Architecture fees for ADU design typically run 8–15% of construction cost, though some architects in active ADU markets have moved to fixed-fee structures — often $8,000–$20,000 for a complete ADU design package depending on complexity [VERIFY]. The higher end applies to custom detached ADUs with significant site challenges or design complexity. The lower end applies to straightforward attached additions or conversions where the design scope is limited.

What you are paying for with an architect is not just drawings. You are paying for their professional liability — they carry errors and omissions insurance that covers design mistakes. You are paying for their coordination capacity — they can manage the structural engineer, mechanical designer, and other consultants as a unified team. And you are paying for their ability to navigate the plan check process, respond to building department comments, and revise drawings efficiently when required.

If you hire an architect, establish the scope of their engagement in writing before work begins. Full architectural services include schematic design, design development, construction documents, and construction administration (the period during which the architect reviews contractor work against the drawings). Construction administration is the piece most homeowners skip to save fees, and it is frequently the piece that would have prevented expensive problems. An architect who reviews the framing before walls close can catch a structural detail that was misread from the drawings. One who doesn't may leave that mistake embedded in your walls until it becomes a failing inspection.

### Working with a Residential Designer

In most states, an ADU of reasonable size and scope — typically under 3,000 square feet, though limits vary — can be designed by a licensed building designer or residential designer who is not a full licensed architect. These professionals typically charge less and may have more direct ADU-specific experience in active markets, because ADU design has become a specialized practice that many non-architect designers have pursued intensively.

Residential designers often produce documents that are functionally equivalent to architectural drawings for permit purposes. What they cannot always do is stamp drawings that require architectural or engineering seal in your jurisdiction, or take on the professional liability that a licensed architect carries. For a straightforward ADU on a flat lot with standard utility connections, a residential designer may be entirely appropriate. For a complex project, verify that their professional credentials cover the scope of work your jurisdiction requires.

### Design-Build Firms

Design-build firms, as described in Chapter 7 in the context of contractor selection, bundle design and construction under a single contract. For ADU projects specifically, this model has real advantages: integrated delivery, fewer coordination gaps, often a library of pre-permitted designs that reduce timeline, and a single party accountable for both the documents and the construction that follows from them.

The caution with design-build for design-focused homeowners is that the design component of a design-build firm's offering is typically optimized for their construction system, not for unlimited client customization. You will work within their design vocabulary. If that is acceptable — and for many homeowners who simply want a livable, well-built ADU without strong aesthetic preferences — design-build is often the most efficient path. If you have specific design intentions or a complex site, you may find design-build constraining.

## Section 4: What Documents You Actually Need

Understanding the document requirements before you engage a designer helps you scope the engagement correctly, evaluate proposals accurately, and avoid paying for work you don't need — or failing to get work you do need.

A complete ADU permit package in most jurisdictions requires:

A **site plan** showing the property boundaries, the location and footprint of all existing structures, the proposed ADU footprint, setback dimensions, utility service connections, and — if relevant — grading and drainage. The site plan is typically drawn at a scale that fits the full parcel and is the first document the plan checker reviews.

**Architectural drawings**, which at minimum include: a floor plan for each level, elevations showing all four building faces, a building section, a roof plan, and door and window schedules. For conversions, existing-conditions drawings showing what is being modified are usually required alongside proposed drawings.

**Structural calculations and drawings** for the structural elements of the project. In California and seismically active markets, structural engineering is required for new construction and most significant additions. Some jurisdictions accept prescriptive design (following published standards without project-specific calculations) for small, simple structures.

A **Title 24 energy compliance report** (in California) demonstrating that the design meets the California Energy Code requirements for insulation, glazing, mechanical systems, and lighting. Other states have their own energy compliance requirements, and even in states without a California-equivalent code, your jurisdiction may require compliance documentation with the current International Energy Conservation Code.

**Mechanical, electrical, and plumbing (MEP) plans** showing system design. In some jurisdictions, these can be submitted separately from the architectural package and reviewed by different departments. In others, the full package must be submitted together. Your designer or architect will know the local protocol.

A **soils or geotechnical report** may be required for new foundations in areas with seismic hazard, expansive soils, or known fill conditions. Not every jurisdiction requires this for small residential structures, but waterfront properties, hillside lots, and sites with known soil conditions may trigger the requirement. Your structural engineer will tell you whether a soils report is needed for their design to proceed.

The above is a general framework. What your specific jurisdiction requires may differ — some require a complete package at initial application; others use a streamlined single-sheet process for qualifying small projects. Ask your building department for their ADU submittal checklist before your designer begins drawing anything. That checklist defines what you are buying when you engage a design professional.

## Section 5: Universal Design Considerations

Universal design — designing for accessibility across a range of physical abilities — matters to ADU projects for two reasons that are often underweighted in early planning. The first is the multigenerational use case: if you are building an ADU for an aging parent, or you intend to eventually use it yourself as you age, accessibility features that are cheap to build in during construction are enormously expensive to retrofit later. The second is that universal design features increase rentability to a broader population — tenants with disabilities, older renters, and anyone who simply values living in a space that does not require full physical ability to navigate comfortably.

The key features that are cheap to build in but expensive to add later are: wider doorways (32–36 inches clear, not the standard 24–28 inches), lever-style door hardware rather than knobs, curbless shower entry (a roll-in shower with a linear drain rather than a shower pan with a threshold), blocking in bathroom walls for future grab bars (the blocking costs almost nothing at rough framing; the grab bars themselves can be added later as needed), and a first-floor bedroom and bathroom configuration that does not require using stairs to reach either. In a small ADU, this last requirement is often inherently met because the unit is single-story — but in a two-story design, confirm that the bedroom and full bath configuration is achievable on the ground level.

Exterior accessibility matters too. A firm, slip-resistant path from the parking area to the ADU entrance, with a slope of no more than 1:20 (5%), and an entry threshold that does not require stepping up to cross, costs almost nothing in design intent but can require significant remediation if the site is graded without attention to it. The entry landing should be large enough to allow someone using a wheelchair or walker to operate the door without backing off the landing — at least 5 feet by 5 feet.

None of this is about building a medical facility. It is about building a space that remains useful across life stages without requiring another round of construction.

## Section 6: Common Design Mistakes That Cost Money in the Field

The following mistakes appear in ADU projects across markets and across all ADU types. They are mentioned here not to frighten you but because they are preventable — if you know to look for them before construction begins.

**Undersizing mechanical systems.** A 600-square-foot ADU with a kitchenette, bathroom, and bedroom needs adequate heating, cooling (in most climates), and ventilation. The mistake is treating the ADU as an afterthought in the mechanical design — running a branch off the main house's HVAC system that was designed for the house's existing load, not a new space. The result is a unit that is uncomfortable in summer and winter, which produces tenant complaints, higher turnover, and eventually an HVAC retrofit that is significantly more expensive than sizing it correctly the first time. Specify an independent mechanical system for the ADU — a mini-split heat pump is often the right answer for a small, well-insulated unit — and size it based on a Manual J load calculation, not guesswork.

**Skipping the acoustic design.** Sound transmission between the main house and an attached ADU, or between the garage and the living space above in a two-story configuration, is one of the most common sources of tenant dissatisfaction and the most common thing that is designed around rather than designed for. A party wall assembly that achieves a Sound Transmission Class (STC) rating of 50 or higher requires specific framing and insulation details — details that need to be on the drawings before the framing crew shows up. Once walls are framed and insulated, adding acoustic performance is disruptive and expensive. Put it in the design documents.

**Locating the ADU entrance facing the main house.** When the ADU's primary entrance faces directly toward the main house's living areas, both households feel surveilled in their own outdoor space. This is an avoidable design decision. Position the ADU entrance, if possible, to face the property boundary opposite the main house, or use landscaping, fence elements, or a covered walkway design to create a privacy screen. This costs almost nothing in design but significantly improves the lived experience for both households.

**Designing for maximum square footage rather than maximum livability.** There is a version of ADU design where the homeowner pushes the building to the maximum allowed footprint on every side, and the result is a structure that is large by square footage but poorly organized by livability — too much hallway, awkward room proportions, no outdoor connection, and windows positioned for code compliance rather than light quality. A well-designed 550-square-foot ADU lives better than a poorly designed 750-square-foot one. Ask your designer to show you layouts at different sizes and evaluate them by how the space actually works, not by the square footage number.

**Not resolving utility routing before the design is finalized.** The location of the new ADU's sewer connection, water service, and electrical service has to be physically achievable given the grade, the distance from existing connections, and the routing path through the site. These are not design-phase details — they are design-phase decisions. A plumbing engineer or experienced contractor should confirm that the proposed utility routing is practical before your architect finalizes the site plan. Finding out after permit approval that your gravity sewer connection does not work requires a permit revision, redesign fees, and schedule loss.

**Choosing finishes before establishing a contingency.** Design decisions happen in a sequence. The sequence matters. Structural decisions come first, then MEP system decisions, then finish materials. The mistake is spending design energy and owner decision-making time on flooring, countertops, and cabinet styles before the structural and mechanical scope is confirmed. Finish upgrades are the easiest and cheapest thing to add later; structural and mechanical changes are the most expensive. Establish your contingency reserve (see Chapter 10 for the right contingency framework for ADU projects), lock your structural and MEP scope, and then treat finishes as the variable that adjusts to available budget — not the other way around.

## Closing: From Design to Documents

A completed design package — site plan, architectural drawings, structural calculations, energy compliance, and MEP design — is your ticket to the permit process. The quality of that package determines how smoothly and quickly you get a permit, and it establishes the foundation on which your contractor will price the work. Incomplete, ambiguous, or structurally underspecified drawings produce incomplete bids, excessive change orders, and slow permits. Complete, coordinated drawings produce accurate bids, minimal surprises, and a project that can actually be built for the price you agreed to.

Chapter 5 covers the regulatory landscape in detail — the zoning and state law framework that determines what you're allowed to build and how the approval process works in your jurisdiction. That knowledge shapes your design before a single drawing is produced, and understanding it before you engage a designer will save you both time and money.
