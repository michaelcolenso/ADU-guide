# Chapter 5 — Navigating Regulations

Here is the most common regulatory mistake homeowners make: they call their city's planning department, ask whether ADUs are allowed on their property, hear "yes," and proceed as if the regulatory work is done. It is not. Knowing that ADUs are allowed in your jurisdiction is the starting point, not the destination. What you actually need to know is what is allowed — specifically, for your parcel, under the current version of your local code, taking into account any state preemption laws that expand what your city is required to permit even if the city hasn't updated its own ordinance yet.

This chapter gives you the framework for reading and interpreting the regulations that govern your ADU. It covers how to find and understand your local zoning code, what the key numerical standards mean, the difference between processes that put you in control and processes that give neighbors and officials discretionary power over your project, how state law interacts with local rules in the states where that interaction is most significant for homeowners, the HOA complication, and how to handle a non-compliant existing structure. After this chapter, you will know what questions to ask, what documents to pull, and what language in the code actually controls your situation.

---

## Section 1: How to Find and Read Your Zoning Code

Every incorporated city and most counties in the United States maintain a municipal code or county code that includes a zoning ordinance. The zoning ordinance is the primary local document that controls what can be built on your parcel. Most jurisdictions publish their current municipal code online through either their own website or a third-party municipal code publisher. Municode (municode.com) and American Legal Publishing host the codified ordinances of hundreds of jurisdictions; if you cannot find your jurisdiction's code through a direct search, try searching "[your city name] municipal code Municode" or "[your city name] zoning ordinance PDF."

Once you have the zoning code, you need to identify two things before you read a single regulation: what zoning district your parcel is in, and what the code says about ADUs in that district. Your zoning district is typically available through your jurisdiction's online parcel map or GIS viewer — search "[your city name] zoning map" and look for a portal where you can search your address or parcel number. The zoning district designation will be something like R-1, RS-5, Single Family Residential, or LDR (Low Density Residential) — the exact terminology varies. Once you have your district designation, search the zoning code for your district's regulations and for any separate ADU ordinance your jurisdiction has adopted.

The ADU regulations in a municipality may live in several places: they might be fully embedded in the primary residence zone rules, they might be a standalone chapter or section of the zoning code, or they might be governed by a separate ADU ordinance that supersedes parts of the primary zoning code. Most jurisdictions with significant ADU activity have a dedicated ADU section. Search for "accessory dwelling unit," "accessory living unit," "secondary dwelling unit," or "in-law unit" — different jurisdictions use different terms. If you find multiple sections, read all of them, because the rules in one section may be modified by exceptions in another.

## Section 2: What to Look For — The Key Numerical Standards

Once you have found the relevant sections of your zoning code, there are six categories of numerical standards that control what you can build. Each of these can make or break a project configuration before you've spent a dollar on design.

**Setbacks** are the minimum distances required between the walls of your ADU and the property lines — or in some codes, between the ADU and the main house. **Setback** is defined in most codes as the minimum horizontal distance from a structure to a specified reference point, typically the property line. Rear and side setbacks for ADUs range dramatically across jurisdictions: some allow ADUs within 4 feet of the rear and side property lines (California's state-mandated minimum for qualifying new construction), while others require 10, 15, or even 20 feet. Front setbacks are typically the same as what the primary dwelling must observe, which in residential zones is often 15–25 feet.

The practical consequence of setback requirements is that they determine the buildable footprint on your specific parcel. If your lot is 100 feet deep and your rear setback is 20 feet and your house sits 40 feet from the front property line, your available rear yard depth — after the setback — is 40 feet. That 40-foot depth, minus the structure clearances you need on the sides, defines the maximum footprint of a detached ADU on your lot. Running this math before you engage a designer is not optional; it determines whether the project is feasible at all.

**Height limits** establish the maximum building height for an ADU, typically measured from grade to the peak of the roof or to the top of the wall plate, depending on the jurisdiction's definition. For detached ADUs, local height limits range from 14 feet in some suburban jurisdictions to 25–28 feet in more progressive cities that allow two-story ADUs. California state law now mandates a minimum height allowance of 16 feet for detached ADUs in single-family zones, and 18 feet in transit-adjacent contexts, regardless of local ordinances that might otherwise allow less [S1]. Washington state law prohibits local height limits below 24 feet for ADUs in covered jurisdictions [S2]. Oregon sets no statewide numeric height floor; local code controls. If your jurisdiction's code specifies a height limit lower than your state law requires, the state law typically overrides — but your city may not have updated its published code to reflect that yet.

**Lot coverage** is the percentage of your lot area that can be covered by structures — typically measured as the total footprint of all structures (including the primary dwelling, garage, ADU, and sometimes sheds and covered patios) divided by the total lot area. A 40% lot coverage limit on a 6,000-square-foot lot allows 2,400 square feet of structure footprint in total. If your house plus garage already covers 2,000 square feet of lot, you have 400 square feet of remaining coverage allowance — which is enough for a small ADU footprint but severely constrains your design options. This is one of the constraints most commonly overlooked until late in the design process.

**Floor area ratio (FAR)** is a related but different metric: it measures the ratio of total building floor area (including all floors of all buildings on the lot) to lot area. A FAR of 0.5 on a 6,000-square-foot lot allows 3,000 square feet of total floor area across all structures. FAR can be a binding constraint in some jurisdictions even when lot coverage alone would permit a larger footprint, because it also captures second-story space.

**Owner-occupancy requirements** are provisions in some local codes that require the property owner to live in either the primary dwelling or the ADU as their primary residence. Owner-occupancy requirements have been significantly curtailed by state law in several states. In California, AB 976 (effective January 1, 2024) eliminated local owner-occupancy requirements for standard ADUs entirely [S1]. Washington's HB 1337 framework similarly prohibits owner-occupancy requirements in covered jurisdictions [S2]. Oregon's ORS 197A.425 limits owner-occupancy mandates in UGB single-dwelling zones [S3]. If you are in one of these states, your local code may still contain an owner-occupancy provision that has not been removed — but state law overrides it, and your building department is not permitted to enforce it. In states without preemption, owner-occupancy requirements can significantly affect your ability to rent the ADU while living elsewhere.

**Minimum unit size** requirements specify the smallest floor area an ADU can have. Some jurisdictions impose a minimum of 150, 200, or even 300 square feet on ADUs, particularly for internal conversions or JADUs. These minimums are usually not a practical constraint — most homeowners are not trying to build a 100-square-foot unit — but they are worth checking if you are pursuing a very small conversion.

## Section 3: By-Right Approval vs. Discretionary Review

The most consequential regulatory distinction for ADU projects is the difference between **by-right approval** (also called ministerial approval) and **discretionary review**. This difference determines not just your timeline but your exposure to neighbor opposition, political resistance, and unpredictable conditions that planners can impose on your project.

A **by-right** or **ministerial** approval means that if your project meets the objective standards in the code — setbacks, height, lot coverage, and other dimensional requirements — the jurisdiction must approve it. A ministerial permit cannot be denied because a neighbor objects, because a planning commissioner has aesthetic concerns about your design, or because the city council has mixed feelings about ADUs in general. If you meet the standards, you get the permit. Period.

A **discretionary review** process gives decision-makers the authority to approve, deny, or condition your project based on their judgment about community impact, neighborhood compatibility, design quality, and any number of other factors. A discretionary project can be denied because neighbors showed up to a hearing and complained. It can be conditioned with requirements that are not in the code — additional landscaping, particular exterior materials, setbacks larger than the code requires. It can be delayed for months while environmental review is completed or while the planning staff composes a staff report.

For almost all ADU projects in states with strong ADU preemption laws, ministerial review is now the required path. California Government Code § 66333 requires ministerial processing for qualifying ADU applications on lots with existing dwellings; the jurisdiction must act on a complete application within 60 days, and the decision cannot be based on subjective criteria [S1]. Washington's HB 1337 framework requires ministerial treatment for qualifying ADU applications in covered jurisdictions [S2]. Oregon's ORS 197A.425 establishes ministerial approval for at least one ADU in covered UGB single-dwelling areas [S3].

What this means in practice: if you are in California, Washington, or Oregon (and in most other states with enacted ADU laws), and your project meets the objective standards of your local code, your application should be processed ministerially. You should not need to appear at a planning commission hearing. You should not be subject to neighbor comments that can affect the outcome. If your jurisdiction is attempting to route your ADU application through a discretionary process that state law has superseded, you have a legal basis to object — and the California HCD's enforcement process, for instance, allows homeowners to file complaints against local agencies that violate state ADU law [S1].

The caveat is that ministerial review still requires that your project comply with all applicable objective standards. Standards are objective when they are measurable and do not require subjective interpretation. Setbacks, height limits, and lot coverage percentages are objective. Requirements that an ADU be "compatible with the neighborhood's character" or "harmonious with adjacent structures" are subjective, and state preemption laws in California and Washington have specifically constrained local agencies' ability to impose subjective design requirements on ADU applications.

## Section 4: How State Law Interacts with Local Rules

The relationship between state ADU law and local zoning is one of the most consequential and most misunderstood aspects of the regulatory landscape. State preemption — where state law limits what local governments can require — has fundamentally shifted what is possible in the most active ADU states. Understanding this dynamic can mean the difference between a project that is feasible under state law and a project that your local planning department incorrectly tells you is not allowed.

### California

California has the most comprehensive and most litigated ADU preemption framework in the country. Under Government Code Chapter 13 (Sections 66310–66342), the state has established a set of baseline ADU standards that local agencies must permit regardless of local zoning — including specific size allowances, height floors, setback limits, ministerial review requirements, and prohibitions on owner-occupancy requirements, impact fee imposition for small ADUs, and replacement parking demands for garage conversions [S1].

The practical effect for a California homeowner is significant. If your city's ADU ordinance says that ADUs must be at least 8 feet from the rear property line, but your project meets the state's 4-foot setback standard for qualifying new construction, the state standard overrides the local standard and your city must process your application accordingly. If your city's code still includes an owner-occupancy requirement, that provision is unenforceable for standard ADUs under AB 976. If your city's height limit would restrict your ADU to 14 feet, the state's 16-foot minimum floor overrides it.

The nuance is that California's preemption protects you from local restrictions that are *more* restrictive than the state baseline, but does not prevent local agencies from applying local standards that are *less* restrictive. A city that allows 6-foot rear setbacks instead of 4 feet is being more permissive than the state, which is fine. A city that requires 8-foot rear setbacks is being more restrictive, which is preempted. The floor is state law; the ceiling is local discretion in the favorable direction.

California's most significant practical advice for homeowners: always check both your local ADU ordinance and the current state law, because the more permissive of the two is typically the minimum that your city must allow. When the two conflict and the state standard is more permissive, you can invoke state law. The California HCD publishes the current ADU Handbook, which describes the state framework in homeowner-accessible language and includes the complaint process for local noncompliance [S1].

### Washington

Washington's HB 1337 (effective 2023, with implementation deadlines for local jurisdictions based on their Growth Management Act update cycles) establishes a two-ADU-per-lot baseline in fully planning GMA jurisdictions and constrains local restrictions across a range of parameters [S2]. Under RCW 36.70A.681, covered jurisdictions must allow: at least two ADUs per lot (attached, detached, or combination); ADUs of at least 1,000 square feet; ADU heights of at least 24 feet; no owner-occupancy requirements; and impact fees capped at no more than 50% of what the primary dwelling is charged.

The important complication in Washington is timing. GMA jurisdictions must comply with these requirements by their next periodic comprehensive plan update deadline, not immediately upon HB 1337's enactment. If your jurisdiction has not yet updated its code to reflect HB 1337, the state's preemption provision still applies — but your building department may not be aware of it, or may be slow to implement it. If you encounter resistance from a local jurisdiction in Washington, the state's Department of Commerce publishes implementation guidance and is the appropriate resource for understanding what state law requires of your jurisdiction [S2].

Washington's new SHB 1353 (effective July 27, 2025) adds an optional self-certification pathway for detached ADUs in GMA cities, allowing licensed architects or engineers to certify that a project meets code standards and expedite the review timeline. This is not available in all jurisdictions and requires working with a licensed professional who has elected to participate, but where it is available, it can materially shorten the permit timeline.

### Oregon

Oregon's regulatory framework is codified in ORS 197A.425, which requires cities over 2,500 in population and counties over 15,000 to allow at least one ADU per detached single-family dwelling within urban growth boundaries [S3]. Oregon also limits local owner-occupancy requirements and off-street parking mandates for ADUs in covered areas, consistent with DLCD guidance implementing HB 2001 and subsequent updates.

Oregon's framework differs from California's in an important way: while California sets specific statewide numerical standards (4-foot setbacks, 16-foot height floors), Oregon primarily establishes the right to have one ADU without specifying most of the dimensional standards — those remain local. This means that in Oregon, unlike California, you cannot typically invoke a state-prescribed setback or height floor; instead, you invoke the right to have the ADU approved at all, while the dimensional parameters your city applies remain local. Portland, Eugene, and other active Oregon ADU cities have their own detailed ADU regulations that are largely favorable to homeowners; the state framework mostly ensures that cities cannot simply prohibit ADUs.

Portland maintains a system development charge (SDC) waiver program for ADUs that allows homeowners to waive sewer, water, and transportation SDCs in exchange for recording a covenant agreeing not to use the ADU as a short-term rental for a specified period [S3]. This can save $8,000–$20,000 in connection charges, though the covenant restriction matters if short-term rental was part of your income strategy. Verify current SDC waiver terms with the Portland Bureau of Development Services before relying on this program, as the terms have changed over time.

### States Without Strong Preemption

In states that have not enacted ADU preemption legislation, local zoning is the only governance layer that applies, and it governs entirely. This is the majority of states as of early 2026. In these jurisdictions, the path is simpler: you read the local code, you comply with its requirements, and if you want a variance from any dimensional requirement, you apply for one — which is a discretionary process that involves public notice, a hearing, and the possibility of denial. Pursuing a variance is expensive (typically $2,000–$8,000 in application fees plus legal and design costs), time-consuming (3–6 months at minimum in most jurisdictions), and uncertain. Design your project to comply with the code as written rather than rely on a variance as part of your strategy.

## Section 5: The HOA Complication

If your property is subject to a **homeowners association (HOA)** with **CC&Rs** (covenants, conditions, and restrictions) that restrict or prohibit ADU construction, you face a complication that state ADU laws have only partially resolved. In California, Civil Code § 4751 prohibits HOAs from unreasonably restricting the construction of ADUs that are permitted under state law [S4]. This means a California HOA cannot simply issue a blanket prohibition on ADUs in a CC&R — that provision is unenforceable. What an HOA can still do is enforce objective architectural and design standards, require a review process for exterior changes, and impose reasonable conditions that do not rise to the level of prohibition.

The line between "reasonable design standard" and "unreasonable restriction" is not always clear, and it is a line that has been litigated in California. If your HOA's CC&Rs contain ADU prohibition language, or if the HOA's architectural review committee denies your ADU application in a way you believe is pretextual, you have a legal basis to challenge that denial — but doing so requires a real estate attorney, time, and money. The more practical path, where the CC&Rs are old and the HOA is responsive, is to engage the HOA before you finalize design, understand what their review process requires, and design in a way that meets their objective standards while staying within your state's legal framework.

In states without Civil Code § 4751 or equivalent HOA preemption provisions, the HOA's CC&Rs are typically enforceable private contracts, and an ADU prohibition in those CC&Rs can stop your project entirely regardless of what the zoning code allows. Check your CC&Rs — all of them, including any recorded amendments, not just the summary your HOA management company gave you when you closed on your home — before you invest in feasibility assessment or design. The CC&Rs are recorded documents, accessible through your county recorder's office or through the closing documents from your purchase.

Outside California, whether state ADU law preempts HOA restrictions is an unsettled question in most states. The general legal principle is that state law overrides conflicting provisions in private contracts when the state law explicitly addresses the conflict, but most state ADU laws were drafted with a focus on government zoning restrictions, not private covenant restrictions. If your state does not explicitly address HOA restrictions in its ADU law, do not assume your CC&Rs are automatically preempted. Get legal advice.

## Section 6: How to Handle a Non-Compliant Existing Structure

A significant number of homeowners discover, during the ADU feasibility process, that they have an existing structure on their property that was built without permits, or built with permits that do not reflect its current configuration. This is the garage that was converted to a bedroom 30 years ago without a permit. The shed that grew into a workshop that grew into a sleeping space. The addition built by a previous owner that the permit record does not include.

This matters for your ADU project in two ways. First, when you apply for an ADU permit, the building department will review your property's permit history and, in many cases, will conduct a site inspection that identifies unpermitted structures or work. If they discover unpermitted work, they will typically require you to either obtain a retroactive permit and bring the work up to current code, or remove the unpermitted work. This can add significant cost and delay to your ADU project, and in some cases — where the unpermitted work was structural and cannot be easily remediated — it can make the overall project infeasible.

Second, unpermitted living space affects your property's assessed value, insurance, and legal status in ways that matter independently of your ADU project. Insurance claims involving unpermitted structures are frequently denied or reduced. Unpermitted additions may not be counted as legal living area in a sale appraisal. If a tenant is injured in an unpermitted structure, the liability picture is complicated.

The pragmatic approach is to assess what unpermitted work exists on your property before you apply for any ADU permit, understand whether you can or want to bring it into compliance, and factor that cost and timeline into your ADU project budget. In many jurisdictions, unpermitted garage conversions or additions from before a certain date can be legalized through a retroactive permit process that applies current standards on a practical basis rather than requiring full code compliance with all current requirements. Ask your building department explicitly about their "legalization permit" or "as-built permit" process.

If you are committed to building an ADU and your property has a significant unpermitted structure, get a pre-application consultation with your building department — discussed further in Chapter 8 — before you finalize your ADU design. Understanding the legalization path (or the impossibility of it) is information you need before you have paid for design documents.

## Section 7: A Practical Workflow for Regulatory Due Diligence

Pulling together everything in this chapter, here is the sequence of steps to complete your regulatory due diligence before you engage a designer and before you finalize an ADU type:

First, identify your parcel's zoning district through your jurisdiction's online GIS or parcel viewer, and pull the full text of the ADU regulations applicable to that district. If your zoning code is hard to find, contact your planning department and ask for the current ADU ordinance in force — most jurisdictions will email or provide a PDF.

Second, note the numerical standards: setbacks (front, side, and rear), height limit, lot coverage, FAR, and any minimum or maximum ADU size. Calculate what those standards mean for your specific parcel geometry, using your lot dimensions and the footprint of your existing structures.

Third, determine whether your project will be processed ministerially (by-right) or through a discretionary process, and understand why. If your jurisdiction requires discretionary review for ADUs that your state law exempts from discretionary review, document that conflict — it is information you will need if the jurisdiction resists ministerial processing.

Fourth, check whether your state has enacted ADU preemption legislation that is more permissive than your local code, and identify any specific local provisions that state law overrides. Chapter 5's state-specific reference sections (and the full state regulation files in this guide's appendix) cover the five Foundation-tier states in detail. See the State Regulation Index for a quick reference to what each state covers.

Fifth, if your property is subject to HOA CC&Rs, pull and read the full CC&R document — not a summary — and identify any provisions that restrict exterior modifications, require architectural review, or explicitly address ADUs or accessory structures.

Sixth, identify any existing unpermitted structures and assess your appetite for addressing them before or concurrent with your ADU project.

This due diligence step, done thoroughly and honestly, takes most homeowners four to eight hours. It is the work that prevents a much larger amount of wasted time and money later. A designer who doesn't know your setbacks and height limit cannot produce a buildable design. A contractor who doesn't know about your unpermitted garage cannot give you an accurate bid. And a homeowner who doesn't understand whether their project is by-right or discretionary will be surprised by a neighbor-driven design hearing at the worst possible time.

Chapter 6 moves to the financing side of the project — which is the other half of the pre-design decision. What you can afford to borrow, and in what structure, shapes your ADU type and size as surely as your setback requirements do. Those two inputs — regulatory constraints and financing constraints — are what determine your actual project, as opposed to the project you would build if neither mattered.
