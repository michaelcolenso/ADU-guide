# Chapter 11 — Inspections, Certificate of Occupancy, and Project Closeout

---

Most homeowners treat inspections as something that happens to their project — a bureaucratic hurdle that the contractor handles while you wait for news. That framing leads to two problems. First, it leaves you dependent on your contractor to tell you whether you passed or failed, and what the consequences are. Second, it means you arrive at the final inspection without having seen the work that's supposed to be there, which makes it very hard to advocate for yourself if something is missing or wrong.

Inspections are your checkpoints. The building department is not your adversary — inspectors are the independent set of eyes confirming that the work your contractor promised was actually done correctly. That's useful information for you, not just a requirement to satisfy. This chapter walks through the full inspection sequence, what each inspection is checking for, how to prepare for each one, and what happens when things don't go as planned. It then covers the certificate of occupancy, the punch list, warranty enforcement, and the documentation package you should have at the end of the project.

---

## Section 1: The Inspection Sequence

Permitted ADU construction requires a series of required inspections at defined stages. The exact sequence varies by jurisdiction, but the framework is consistent across virtually all building departments in the country. Missing a required inspection, or covering work before it's been inspected, can force you to open walls or remove work to allow the inspector access — at your expense. Your contractor should be scheduling inspections as a routine part of the construction sequence, but you should know the sequence well enough to verify that's happening.

**Foundation inspection** occurs after the excavation is complete and the forms are set, but before concrete is poured. The inspector is checking that the excavation depth reaches undisturbed soil (as specified by the structural engineer or soils report), that the forms are correctly positioned and dimensioned per the approved plans, that rebar is correctly placed and tied, and that any hold-down hardware or anchor bolts are positioned correctly. This is a critical inspection because you cannot go back and fix a foundation without extraordinary cost and schedule impact. If the inspector flags a problem here — incorrectly sized rebar, insufficient embedment depth, misaligned anchor bolts — it must be corrected before the pour. Experienced contractors know this and schedule accordingly; less experienced ones sometimes rush the inspection to maintain schedule pressure. Your job is to make sure the inspection actually happens before any concrete goes in the ground, not after.

**Framing inspection** happens once the structural shell is complete — walls, floor systems, roof structure, and sheathing — but before any insulation or drywall is installed. This is one of the most comprehensive inspections in the sequence. The inspector is verifying that the framing matches the approved structural drawings: wall stud size and spacing, header sizing over openings, floor and roof joist sizing and bearing, shear wall nailing patterns, hurricane ties or similar connectors at roof-to-wall connections, hold-downs at shear wall ends, and window and door rough openings. The inspector may also check fire blocking between floor levels and at the top of stud cavities, and verify that any beam-to-post connections include the specified hardware. Framing inspections commonly generate correction items — not because contractors are doing bad work, but because the nailing patterns for shear walls are exacting, and small deviations from the specified pattern can be a legitimate structural issue. Corrections at framing are still manageable. Corrections after drywall is installed are expensive.

**Rough mechanical, electrical, and plumbing inspection** — often called the rough MEP inspection or rough-in inspection — takes place after all the pipes, conduit, ducts, and wiring have been run through the wall and floor cavities, but before those cavities are closed up with insulation and drywall. This is actually a series of inspections, typically done by separate inspectors: a building inspector for framing and general rough-in, plus specialty inspectors for electrical (often from the local utility or a separate electrical inspector), plumbing, and mechanical (HVAC). Each is checking that the rough installations meet code: wire sizing and stapling for electrical, pipe sizing and support for plumbing, duct sizing and sealing for HVAC. Plumbing will include a pressure test — the drain, waste, and vent system is typically capped and filled with water or air to confirm there are no leaks before the walls close. Electrical inspectors are checking wire gauge, breaker sizing, box fill calculations, and grounding. HVAC inspectors are verifying duct sizing, ventilation rates, and equipment sizing per the energy compliance report.

The rough MEP inspection is the last opportunity to correct any of these systems before the walls close. If a plumber has used the wrong pipe size, or an electrician has routed a circuit through an area with inadequate protection, it is far better to know now than after drywall is installed. Insist on confirmation that all rough inspections have passed before authorizing the insulation or drywall contractor to proceed.

**Insulation inspection** (required in many jurisdictions) verifies that insulation type, thickness, and installation quality meet the energy compliance requirements for the project. In California, this is a Title 24 verification — the specific R-values, window U-factors, and installation details that were shown on the energy compliance report must actually be present in the field. In other states, equivalent energy codes apply. Inspectors at this stage are looking for gaps in the insulation envelope, compressed batts that don't achieve rated R-value, and missing air sealing at penetrations. [VERIFY: insulation inspection requirement varies by jurisdiction — confirm whether this is a distinct inspection or part of framing/rough in your specific building department]

**Final inspection** is the last stop before the certificate of occupancy. By this point, the ADU is complete — all finishes are in, fixtures are installed, appliances are in place, exterior work is done, and the site has been cleaned up. The inspector is doing a comprehensive walk of the completed unit and confirming that the construction matches the approved plans, that all required elements are present (smoke and CO detectors in required locations, GFCI outlets in required locations, egress windows at required sizes in sleeping rooms), that exterior grading drains away from the foundation, and that the landscaping or hardscaping disturbed by construction has been restored to the condition required by the permit. The inspector may also verify utility connections, test smoke and CO detectors, and confirm that address numbers are posted.

## Section 2: Preparing for Each Inspection

The single most important thing you can do to prepare for any inspection is to walk the work yourself before the inspector arrives. This requires a copy of the approved plans and the ability to compare what's in the field to what's on paper. You don't need to be a structural engineer to verify that the rebar shown on the drawings is actually present before the foundation inspection. You do need to be willing to look.

Before the foundation inspection, verify with your contractor that the excavation depth is correct, that the forms are set, that rebar is in place and tied, and that anchor bolts are positioned per the drawings. Ask whether the structural engineer has done a pre-pour site visit, which is standard practice on engineered foundations and which some jurisdictions require. If the structural engineer has signed off, you're in a good position.

Before the framing inspection, confirm that the contractor has completed all framing and sheathing, and that the approved plans are on-site. The inspector will be cross-referencing against the plans, and so should you. Look for hold-down hardware at shear wall corners, hurricane ties at roof framing, and the header sizes over window and door openings. If anything looks like it might be different from the plans, ask your contractor to explain the deviation before the inspection — not during it.

Before the rough MEP inspections, confirm with your GC that each trade has completed their rough-in work and is ready to be inspected. The plumber should have completed the pressure test. The electrician should have labeled the panel. Request from your GC the inspection request dates so you know when to expect inspectors on site.

Before the final inspection, do your own comprehensive walkthrough — ideally 48–72 hours before the scheduled inspection, so there's time to address anything you find. Walk every room and check: smoke detectors and CO detectors are installed and functional in required locations, all light fixtures and outlets are operational, all plumbing fixtures are connected and functional with no visible leaks, HVAC equipment is operational, exterior doors and windows open and close properly and lock, grading slopes away from the foundation, and the address is posted in a visible location. Compare the unit to the approved plans and confirm that all required elements shown on the plans are present. The pre-inspection walkthrough checklist in the Complete tier appendix covers more than 50 specific items for this walkthrough.

## Section 3: When You Fail an Inspection

Failing an inspection is not a catastrophe. It is a normal part of the construction process, and any experienced contractor has been through it many times. What matters is how quickly and completely the correction is made and the re-inspection scheduled.

When an inspector finds a deficiency, they will document it on a correction notice or inspection failure report, which becomes part of the permit record. Your contractor receives this document and is responsible for making the corrections. As the owner, you should receive a copy of every correction notice — request this explicitly if your contractor doesn't provide it automatically. The correction notice tells you exactly what the inspector found, which lets you verify that the correction actually addresses the specific deficiency rather than something adjacent to it.

Re-inspections typically require the contractor to submit a new inspection request. Depending on the jurisdiction, re-inspections may carry a fee ($50–$250 per re-inspection is common, though this varies widely) [VERIFY: re-inspection fee ranges with specific jurisdiction data]. The inspector may do a targeted re-inspection of the correction items, or may re-inspect the entire scope depending on the nature of the failure. If the failure was minor — a few nails missing from a shear wall nailing pattern, an outlet that wasn't connected — a targeted re-inspection is typical. If the failure revealed a more systemic problem — undersized framing across multiple openings, incomplete plumbing rough-in — expect a comprehensive re-inspection after all corrections are made.

From a contract management standpoint, inspection failures caused by deficient contractor work (not owner changes) should not generate change orders. The contractor is responsible for delivering work that passes inspection. If a failure results from an owner-initiated design change or a field condition that genuinely wasn't anticipatable, that may be a legitimate change order item. Know the distinction, and push back on change orders that are really the contractor's cost to fix their own work.

## Section 4: The Certificate of Occupancy

The **certificate of occupancy (CO)** is the document issued by the building department after all inspections have passed, confirming that the structure is legally habitable. It is not a formality — it is the legal authorization for the building to be occupied. Without a CO, you cannot legally rent the unit, you may not be able to insure it as an occupied dwelling, and you have a code violation that can affect your property title. Some lenders will not release final loan proceeds without a CO. [VERIFY: CO and lender final draw requirements vary by loan product and institution]

The CO unlocks several things that matter to you immediately. If you've been holding back a portion of the contractor's payment as a retainage until CO is issued, this is when that retainage becomes due — subject to resolution of any punch list items. If you've been waiting to begin marketing the unit for rental, you can now do so with confidence. And if you have a construction loan, the CO typically triggers the conversion to a permanent loan product, or enables the payoff of the construction loan with permanent financing.

In some jurisdictions, the building department issues a temporary certificate of occupancy (TCO) when the work is substantially complete but minor items remain outstanding. A TCO allows occupancy while outstanding items are resolved — typically within 30–90 days. Be cautious with TCOs: the outstanding items documented in the TCO are exactly the items you're trying to get the contractor to finish, and once a tenant is in the unit, the contractor's incentive to return and complete punch list items drops considerably. Where possible, complete all outstanding items before occupancy, even if it means a short delay.

## Section 5: The Punch List Process

The **punch list** is the final list of incomplete or deficient items that need to be resolved before the project is formally complete. The term comes from the old practice of physically punching a hole next to each item on a printed list as it was completed. The concept is still the same: a documented list, tracked to completion.

Do not let the punch list be prepared by the contractor alone. The contractor has an inherent interest in a short punch list, and items that don't make the list don't get fixed. Do your own comprehensive walkthrough to create the punch list, ideally accompanied by your GC so there are no disputes about what was observed. Walk every square foot of the unit systematically: ceiling to floor in each room, then exterior, then mechanical systems. Document every item in writing with enough specificity that there's no ambiguity about what "complete" looks like. "Paint touch-ups" is not a punch list item. "Paint touch-up at drywall corner damage, southwest corner of bedroom, approximately 6 inches, to match existing wall color" is.

Photograph every item. A time-stamped photo with a brief description is your record in the event of a dispute, and it's far more effective than a written description alone for showing what condition something was in at a specific date.

The completed punch list, signed by both you and your contractor, becomes a binding document: the contractor agrees that these items exist and agrees to complete them within a defined timeframe. Tie the completion of the punch list to the release of retainage (typically 5–10% of the contract price), which is the primary financial leverage you have to ensure completion. Once retainage is released without a completed punch list, the incentive for the contractor to return diminishes sharply.

Establish a clear deadline — two to three weeks for typical punch list work is reasonable. If items aren't complete by the deadline, you have the right to hire other contractors to complete them and charge the cost against remaining payments owed. This is a contractual remedy that your contract should already provide for. See Chapter 7 for the contract provisions that protect this right.

## Section 6: Warranty Obligations and Enforcement

Warranties on construction work exist at two levels: the contractor's own warranty and the manufacturer warranties on products and systems installed in the unit.

Your GC should provide a written one-year workmanship warranty on the work performed. This warranty covers defects in materials and workmanship — things that were done incorrectly during construction that become apparent after completion. Common warranty items include: drywall cracks at corners and openings (caused by framing shrinkage or inadequate taping), paint adhesion failures, tile grout cracking, caulk failures at penetrations, minor settling cracks, and mechanical/electrical/plumbing items that malfunction within the warranty period. A one-year warranty is the industry standard minimum. [VERIFY: state-level implied warranty of habitability and construction warranty provisions — vary significantly by state]

Manufacturer warranties cover the specific products installed: roofing (typically 20–50 year manufacturer warranty, 1–10 year contractor installation warranty), windows (typically 10–20 years on glass, 1–5 years on hardware), HVAC equipment (typically 5–10 years on parts, 1 year on labor), appliances (typically 1 year on parts and labor), and water heaters (typically 6–12 years). These warranties are transferable to tenants in many cases, and they're the mechanism for getting equipment replaced or repaired during the warranty period.

To enforce warranty claims, you need documentation. Keep a copy of every warranty document — both the contractor's warranty letter and manufacturer warranty cards — in the project closeout package. When a warranty item appears, document it immediately: photograph it, note the date, and send a written notice to the appropriate party (the GC for workmanship items, the manufacturer or manufacturer's representative for product items). Don't call and leave a message — send an email so you have a timestamp and a record. Most reputable contractors will address legitimate warranty items promptly, but the documentation practice protects you if they don't.

For items that the GC refuses to address under warranty, you have several options: mediation or arbitration per your contract's dispute resolution clause, small claims court for smaller amounts, or contractor licensing board complaints in states where GCs are licensed and regulated. The licensing board option is often underused by homeowners — it is effective precisely because contractors care about their license status, and a documented warranty failure complaint creates real incentive to resolve the matter.

## Section 7: Utility Connections and Final Hookups

The utility connection process at project completion requires coordination with your local utilities, and it can take longer than most homeowners expect. Electrical service activation, gas service reconnection, and water meter installation each involve utility-side scheduling that is outside your or your contractor's control. In high-demand markets, utility connection appointments can run 2–6 weeks out. [VERIFY: utility connection lead times by market — varies significantly by utility provider and jurisdiction]

Your contractor should submit utility connection requests several weeks before anticipated completion so that the utilities can schedule their work in coordination with the project completion date. If your project is connecting to the utility grid for the first time (as opposed to extending existing service), the electrical service connection requires a utility inspection in addition to the building department inspection, and the sequence matters: building department final inspection before utility energization in most jurisdictions.

Solar and battery storage systems, which are increasingly common in ADU projects in California and other states with solar incentive programs, add another layer of utility interconnection process. The application for Permission to Operate (PTO) from the utility is a separate process from the building permit, and it typically takes 2–8 weeks after the system is installed and passes building department inspection. [VERIFY: solar PTO timelines by utility and state]

If the ADU shares utilities with the main house (sharing an electric meter or a gas meter), confirm that this arrangement is acceptable under your jurisdiction's rules and under your insurance policy. Some jurisdictions require separate meters for rentals. Some lenders require metering separation. And tenants have legitimate practical concerns about sharing utility costs with a landlord — separate metering avoids disputes and is the cleaner long-term setup. See Chapter 12 for the tenant management implications.

## Section 8: The Project Documentation Package

When your ADU project is complete, you should have a documentation package that you keep permanently. This package is not a nice-to-have — it's the record that protects you when questions arise about the project, when you sell the property, when you file insurance claims, or when warranty items appear.

The complete documentation package includes the following elements.

**Permit documentation:** Your original permit application, the approved plan set (often called the "permit set" — the drawings stamped by the building department), the permit card that was posted on the job site, and the final inspection sign-off confirming that all inspections passed. Keep the approved plan set in full-size form if possible, or at minimum in a high-quality scan. This is the legal record of what was approved and built.

**As-built drawings:** The approved plan set shows what was designed. The as-built drawings show what was actually constructed — which is sometimes different, particularly for rough MEP. If your electrician routed a circuit differently than shown on the plans, if the plumber changed the drain configuration to work around a footing, those changes should be marked up on a copy of the plans and kept as the record of what's actually in the walls. Ask your GC to provide marked-up as-builts at project completion. If they don't provide them, make your own notes during the walkthrough before walls are closed — photographs of the rough MEP locations, annotated with measurements, serve the same purpose.

**Warranty documents:** The GC's one-year warranty letter (make sure it's in writing, signed, dated, and describes the scope), and all manufacturer warranty documentation for roofing, windows, HVAC, appliances, water heater, and any other warranted systems. Organize these by system with the product model numbers noted — you'll need those if you ever file a claim.

**Owner's manuals and maintenance schedules:** HVAC equipment, water heaters, ventilation fans, appliances, and any other mechanical systems should come with owner's manuals. Keep them together in the documentation package. Include the manufacturer's recommended maintenance schedule for each system — HVAC filter changes, water heater anode rod inspection, exhaust fan cleaning. Failure to perform required maintenance can void warranties.

**Subcontractor contacts:** A list of the subcontractors who performed each trade — roofing, plumbing, electrical, HVAC, flooring — with their company name, license number, and contact information. If a warranty item appears and your GC is unresponsive, you may need to contact the sub directly or reference their license number for a licensing board complaint.

**Certificate of occupancy:** A copy of the CO issued by the building department. This is the document you'll need when you apply for rental permits, when you add the unit to your insurance policy, and when you sell the property.

**Lien release documentation:** Final unconditional lien releases from the GC and all major subcontractors and suppliers, confirming that all parties have been paid in full and that no liens remain against your property. This is the protection against a situation where your GC didn't pay their subs — without lien releases, those subs have legal rights against your property even if you paid the GC. See the lien discussion in Chapter 10.

## Section 9: Transitioning to Occupancy or Tenant Move-In

The final step of the construction phase is the transition to use — whether that means a family member moving in, a long-term tenant taking occupancy, or the owner using the space themselves.

If you're renting to a tenant, do a thorough pre-occupancy inspection with them present. Walk every room, test every fixture, and document the condition of the unit with photographs at the time of move-in. This move-in inspection, signed by you and the tenant, establishes the baseline condition for security deposit purposes at move-out. Skipping this step is one of the more common mistakes new landlords make, and it creates significant friction at move-out.

Confirm that the unit is insured before occupancy. A standard homeowner's policy may not cover the ADU as an occupied rental — you will likely need to add a landlord rider or a separate dwelling fire policy. Confirm coverage with your insurance agent before the first day of occupancy, not after. See Chapter 12 for the insurance framework specific to ADU rentals.

If you're doing a final utility setup — programming the smart thermostat, confirming internet service is available, setting up trash service for the unit — do it before the tenant moves in, not after. The transition from a construction project to a habitable home is a moment where the homeowner's attention is fractured across a dozen logistics, and small details get missed. Make a checklist. Walk it before the keys change hands.

---

> **Pro Tip:** The documentation package I've described above takes about two hours to assemble if you're collecting materials throughout the project, and about two days to reconstruct from scratch if you wait until the end. Build the habit of filing documents as they arrive — every warranty card, every inspection approval, every signed change order — in a single folder (physical or digital). By the time you reach project completion, the package practically assembles itself. The homeowners I've seen struggle most at closeout are the ones who treated paperwork as someone else's problem throughout construction. It's never someone else's problem.

---

Getting from permit issuance to a completed, occupied ADU is a process with a defined sequence. Inspections, certificate of occupancy, punch list, warranty documentation, utility connections, and move-in preparation are each discrete steps, and each one depends on the previous one being handled correctly. When you complete this sequence with the same discipline you applied to the design and bidding phases, you arrive at the end of the project holding the documentation, the legal occupancy authorization, and the contractual protections that make the ADU a long-term asset rather than a source of ongoing liability.
