# Chapter 10 — Budgeting and Cost Control

---

The budget conversation almost always starts in the wrong place. A homeowner asks a contractor, "What does an ADU cost?" The contractor names a number — or, more dangerously, a low number to win the job — and that figure becomes the mental anchor for everything that follows. The homeowner tells their spouse. They tell their financial advisor. They run the rental math against it. By the time the real number emerges, three months later, the gap between expectation and reality has become a source of genuine financial stress.

This chapter is about building a real budget before you commit to anything — one that accounts for everything you'll actually spend, not just the part that shows up in the contractor's bid.

---

## Section 1: What a Budget Actually Includes

There's a persistent misconception that the contractor's contract price and the project budget are the same number. They're not. The contract price is what you've agreed to pay your general contractor (GC) for the work covered in the contract. The budget is the total financial exposure of the project, from the first dollar spent on feasibility to the day you hand keys to a tenant. The difference between those two numbers is typically 30–50% of the contract price, and most of the surprises live in that gap.

A complete ADU budget has five categories.

**Hard costs** are the physical construction work — everything that the GC and subcontractors install, frame, pour, wire, and finish. This is the number on the contract. For a detached 600–800 sq ft ADU, hard costs in most markets currently run $120,000–$220,000 on the West Coast, $90,000–$170,000 in the Mountain West and Midwest, and $180,000–$300,000 in high-cost coastal markets like the San Francisco Bay Area or greater Boston. [VERIFY: regional cost benchmarks with current RSMeans or Craftsman Cost Data] These figures assume standard site conditions — a reasonably flat lot with existing utility connections nearby, no significant grading, no soil remediation. Add a garage conversion or a project with difficult site access, and the range shifts meaningfully.

**Soft costs** are everything that isn't physical construction: design and architecture (typically $8,000–$18,000 for ADU-scale work), structural engineering ($3,000–$8,000), civil engineering for grading or drainage plans ($2,500–$7,500 if required), soils testing ($1,500–$4,000 if required), energy compliance reports ($800–$2,500), permit fees ($2,000–$12,000 depending on jurisdiction and project valuation), plan check fees, inspection fees, and third-party testing. The aggregate soft cost number for a typical ADU project runs $20,000–$45,000 — a figure that surprises almost every homeowner who hasn't been through a permitted building project before.

**Contingency** is the reserve you hold for unknown conditions, errors, and circumstances you couldn't have predicted at the time of contracting. Ten percent of hard costs is the absolute minimum for a well-documented project with a reliable contractor. Fifteen percent is prudent for most ADU projects. Twenty percent is appropriate if your site has any of the following: unknown utility conditions, older foundation work on the main house that may need to be addressed, an older electrical service that might need upgrading, or any history of drainage problems. Contingency is not a slush fund and it's not padding — it is a real financial reserve for real costs that will likely appear. Homeowners who don't set aside genuine contingency are the ones who end up negotiating with a half-built ADU and an empty bank account.

**Financing costs** are frequently omitted from initial budgets and then discovered mid-project. If you're using a construction loan, you'll pay origination fees (typically 1–2% of the loan amount), interest during the construction period (often 10–18 months depending on project pace and draw timing), and possibly a permanent loan origination fee if you're rolling into a long-term product at the end. On a $200,000 construction loan at 8% interest over 14 months with average balance of 60%, the interest cost alone runs $13,000–$15,000. See Chapter 6 for a full breakdown of financing product costs by type.

**Owner costs** are the miscellaneous project expenses that the homeowner absorbs directly: temporary storage for personal items displaced during construction, landscaping repair after construction, utility reconnection fees, appliance purchases, window coverings, moving costs if renting the main house during construction, and the time cost of serving as your own project manager. These are real costs that belong in the budget. On most ADU projects, owner costs run $5,000–$20,000 depending on how much disruption the project creates and how finished a condition the homeowner expects at turnover.

Add those five categories together, and you get the true project budget. For a typical detached 600–800 sq ft ADU in a mid-cost market, that total frequently falls in the $170,000–$280,000 range. In high-cost coastal markets, $250,000–$400,000 is not unusual for a well-finished detached ADU.

## Section 2: How Budgets Break Down

Understanding why ADU budgets fail is more useful than simply knowing that they do. In twenty-plus years of managing construction projects, I've watched budgets absorb predictable hits in predictable places. The sequence is almost always the same.

The first pressure point is **pre-construction discovery**. Once your architect begins design and your soils engineer or civil engineer engages, you start learning things about your site that weren't visible from the street. The existing sewer lateral may be clay tile and failing — a replacement that runs $8,000–$18,000. The electrical service to the main house may be 100-amp, insufficient to support an ADU without a service upgrade at $4,000–$8,000. The grade between the main house and the ADU site may require engineered retaining walls. None of this appears in an early budget estimate because none of it was known. This is why the contingency reserve exists, and why I'd rather homeowners understand this dynamic before they commit than discover it after they've signed a contract.

The second major pressure point is **change orders** — and this is where the largest dollar amounts move. Change orders are formally documented changes to the scope of work after the contract is signed. Some are owner-initiated (you decide you want quartz countertops instead of laminate, or you add a covered porch), some are contractor-initiated (unforeseen site conditions that weren't in the original scope), and some are required by the building department (an inspector requires additional work not in the original permit set). The change order management section below covers this in detail.

The third common pressure point is **schedule extensions**, which generate costs in ways that homeowners rarely anticipate: additional months of loan interest, extended temporary living arrangements if you vacated during construction, carrying costs on any bridge financing, and contractor overhead for extended general conditions. A project that runs three months over schedule can easily absorb an additional $10,000–$25,000 in indirect costs — costs that show up nowhere in the contractor's contract.

Finally, **budget underestimation at the outset** remains the single most common root cause of overruns. A homeowner hears a rough number from a neighbor or reads a national average online, sets their mental budget at that figure, and then filters information through that anchor throughout the planning process. The discipline required is to resist the anchor, do the full budget exercise in this chapter, and set expectations — with your family, your lender, and yourself — at the real number before you begin.

## Section 3: Reading the Schedule of Values

When you sign a contract with a GC, you'll receive a **schedule of values** — a line-item breakdown of the contract price showing how much is allocated to each phase or trade. This document is not optional, and you should request it before signing if it isn't offered. It serves two critical functions: it tells you whether the contractor's pricing is reasonable across categories, and it becomes the basis for tracking progress payments against actual work installed.

A typical ADU schedule of values might have 12–18 line items: site preparation and demolition, concrete foundations, framing, roofing, exterior siding and trim, windows and exterior doors, rough plumbing, rough electrical, rough HVAC, insulation, drywall, interior finishes (cabinets, countertops, tile), interior doors and hardware, flooring, painting, fixtures and appliances, and final plumbing/electrical/HVAC. Each line carries a dollar value and a percentage of the total contract.

When you receive this document, look for two things. First, check whether the front-end items (site prep, foundation, framing) are loaded — meaning they carry a disproportionately large share of the contract value relative to the work they represent. Some contractors front-load the schedule of values because early draws fund their mobilization and overhead. This isn't necessarily dishonest, but it means you're paying for work that hasn't been installed yet, which reduces your leverage later in the project. A reasonable schedule of values tracks reasonably closely to the actual cost of work installed at each phase. If site prep is listed at 25% of the contract when the work involved is minimal clearing and grading, that's a signal worth questioning.

Second, check whether the line items include adequate allowances for finish work. If flooring is listed at $3,000 for an 800 sq ft unit, and you're expecting anything beyond basic vinyl plank, that number is almost certainly insufficient. When you accept a contract with inadequate allowances, the cost difference will come out of your pocket as a change order when you make the actual selection. Negotiate allowances to realistic levels before signing, not after.

## Section 4: Change Order Management

Change orders are where most budget overruns actually occur. Not because they're inherently unexpected, but because homeowners consistently underestimate how many there will be, fail to evaluate whether the pricing is fair, and approve them individually without tracking the cumulative impact on the budget.

Here's the practical dynamic: your contractor is on a fixed-price contract. Every item outside that contract's defined scope is a change order, and change orders are typically priced at higher margin than the original contract work. This isn't a conspiracy — it's the natural economics of uncompetitive pricing. When the original bid was competitive (which is how the contractor won the job), the margins were thin. Change orders are priced without competition, which means the contractor has pricing power. Change orders on ADU projects routinely carry markups of 25–40% above cost, compared to 15–20% in the original bid. [VERIFY: contractor markup ranges on change orders with industry source]

Managing change orders well requires three habits. First, require a written change order with scope description and price before authorizing any work outside the original contract. A verbal authorization to "go ahead and do it" is a blank check. If your contractor begins work without a signed change order, you've lost your pricing leverage — it's far harder to negotiate price after the work is done than before. Second, track every approved change order against your contingency reserve in real time. Keep a running log: original contract price, plus all approved change orders to date, equals the current contract total. Compare that against your budget regularly so you can see the trend before it becomes a crisis. Third, evaluate each change order price before approving it.

**How to evaluate whether a change order price is fair:** Ask for the labor and material breakdown. Any reputable contractor should be willing to show you what the cost components are. For smaller change orders, compare the unit pricing to your original schedule of values — if the GC is charging $120 per square foot for framing in the change order when the original contract implied $85 per square foot, that's worth a conversation. For larger change orders involving specialty trades, you have a right to see the subcontractor quote the GC received, not just the marked-up number passed through to you. As a rule of thumb, a change order cost plus 20–25% overhead and profit is a reasonable range for work done by the contractor's regular crews. More than 30% markup on materials and labor combined is worth pushing back on. You won't always win that negotiation, but asking the question often results in a meaningful reduction.

Owner-initiated changes are easier to control than required changes, because you can choose whether to proceed. Before authorizing any owner change, ask yourself: Is this change worth the cost plus the disruption to the schedule, plus the precedent it sets for future changes? The most expensive ADU projects I've seen weren't the ones that hit unforeseen conditions — they were the ones where the homeowner kept changing their mind on finishes, layout, and fixtures through the construction period. Every change costs money, every change costs time, and time costs money. Lock in your decisions before the contract is signed and hold to them.

## Section 5: Tracking Costs in Real Time

Knowing your budget is not the same as managing it. Managing it requires a tracking system that you update regularly — weekly, at minimum. The tracking methodology described in Chapter 9 applies here directly: your weekly site visit or check-in should include an update to your cost tracking spreadsheet, capturing approved change orders, draw requests submitted and paid, and any anticipated costs not yet in the system.

Your tracking document should show four numbers at all times: the **original contract amount**, the **current contract amount** (original plus all approved change orders), the **amount drawn to date**, and the **remaining budget to complete** (current contract amount minus draws plus contingency remaining). When the remaining budget to complete starts converging with your contingency reserve, that is a warning signal. It means you have less margin for additional change orders than you started with, and you need to tighten up approvals.

Draw reconciliation is the other half of real-time tracking. When your contractor submits a draw request, it should be tied to specific schedule of values line items at specific percentages of completion. A draw request that says "30% complete — please release $60,000" with no further detail is insufficient. Before releasing any draw payment, walk the site and confirm that the work billed is actually in place. This is not about distrust — it's about maintaining the draw schedule as a meaningful tracking instrument. See Chapter 9 for the full draw reconciliation process.

## Section 6: Cash Flow Timing

Budget and cash flow are different problems. You can have the right total budget but run into serious trouble if the cash isn't available when needed. Construction projects draw money in large, lumpy increments — and the timing rarely aligns neatly with the homeowner's income stream or the lending product's draw schedule.

If you're using a construction loan, the lender controls the draw process. Each draw requires a draw request, a lender inspection, and an approval cycle — a process that can take 7–14 days per draw, sometimes longer. If your contractor needs payment to make payroll or release a subcontractor, that timing mismatch can create pressure to release draws early or to approve draws without adequate inspection. The right posture is to communicate the draw schedule to your contractor at the outset of the project so they can plan around the lender's timeline, not be surprised by it.

If you're self-funding or using a HELOC, you control the draw timing but you still need to model cash flow in advance. Look at your anticipated draw schedule — what amounts will be needed, when — and confirm that the funds will be available at each stage. A HELOC with a $300,000 limit sounds like adequate coverage for a $250,000 project, but if only $150,000 is accessible during the draw period (because the home's appraised value changed, or because you've already drawn against it for other purposes), you may face a mid-project liquidity problem. Model this before you start, not when you're facing it.

## Section 7: What Happens When You Run Out of Money

This is the conversation that no one wants to have in advance, but it's far better to think through it before it happens than to be making decisions under duress. ADU projects that run out of funding mid-construction are not rare. They happen when budgets are underestimated, when change orders erode contingency, when owners can't access their financing on the timeline they expected, or when the project hits a genuinely catastrophic unforeseen condition.

If you're approaching the point where you can see the end of your available funds before the end of the project, act early. Early action preserves options. Your choices at that stage include: negotiating with your contractor to defer some final payment in exchange for completion, exploring whether your lender will provide additional funding based on increased project value, seeking a short-term bridge loan, or value-engineering the remaining work to bring costs down. None of these options are ideal, but all of them are better than what happens if you wait until the money is literally gone.

A contractor who stops being paid stops working. More importantly, a contractor who stops being paid has legal options — including mechanics liens on your property — that create complications that can follow you for years. In most states, subcontractors and suppliers have lien rights against your property even if you've paid your GC in full, if the GC hasn't paid them. The standard protection against this risk is to require lien releases from the contractor and all major subcontractors as a condition of each draw payment. If lien releases are not part of your contract, add them before signing. [VERIFY: lien release requirements vary by state — cross-reference Chapter 7 for contract provisions]

## Section 8: Common Budget Traps

The traps are consistent enough that I can list them from experience without needing to look at a specific project.

The first trap is **using a square-foot estimate as a budget**. "I've heard ADUs cost $300 per square foot" is not a budget — it's a ballpark that may or may not apply to your site, your market, your ADU type, your finish level, or your soil conditions. Square-foot figures are useful for rough screening (can this project plausibly pencil out?) but dangerous as planning tools. Build an itemized budget from the ground up.

The second trap is **omitting soft costs and financing costs** from the budget entirely. I've walked through this in detail above, but it bears repeating: in a typical ADU project, the sum of soft costs plus financing costs can easily represent 20–35% of the hard cost contract price. Leaving them out is not conservative budgeting — it's a structurally incomplete financial model.

The third trap is **relying on the low bid as the budget anchor**. If you receive bids of $180,000, $220,000, and $265,000 for the same scope, the instinct is to use $180,000 as the starting point and hope the others are wrong. That is exactly backwards. The low bid is where you should be most skeptical, not most optimistic. See Chapter 7 for how to evaluate bid outliers.

The fourth trap is **treating contingency as optional**. When a homeowner says, "We've budgeted right up to our limit, we can't really afford a contingency," what they're really saying is that they can't afford any surprises. That's not a position — that's a risk that, if realized, ends the project. If you cannot fund the project with contingency, you cannot fund the project.

The fifth trap is **approving change orders without cumulative tracking**. Each individual change order looks manageable. Two thousand dollars for an upgraded electrical panel. Four thousand for an extended footing to avoid a tree root. Fifteen hundred for a different tile in the shower. Add them up over the course of a project and you routinely find $25,000–$40,000 in changes that no one totaled in real time. By the time the total is obvious, the budget is gone.

---

> **Pro Tip:** At the start of every project I've managed, I build a one-page budget dashboard with five rows: original contract, approved changes, current contract total, draws to date, and remaining contingency. I update it every week without exception. When the contingency line starts declining faster than I expected, I start asking questions. That discipline — knowing the number before someone tells you it's a problem — is the single highest-leverage habit in construction financial management.

---

Managing a construction budget well isn't complicated, but it requires consistency. The homeowners who finish ADU projects on budget are not the ones who got lucky with their contractor — they're the ones who built a real budget before they started, tracked every dollar in real time, managed change orders with discipline, and treated contingency as a real financial reserve rather than a wish. With that discipline in place, the final chapter of the construction phase — inspections and closeout — becomes a completion process rather than a stress test.
