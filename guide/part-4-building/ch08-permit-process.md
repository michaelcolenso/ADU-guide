# Chapter 8 — The Permit Process Demystified

Most homeowners think the permit process starts with submitting an application. It actually starts 6–8 weeks earlier — with the kind of homework most people skip entirely. By the time you walk up to that permit counter, you should already know your zoning classification, your applicable development standards, your plan checker's likely concerns, and whether your jurisdiction offers any process shortcuts. The homeowners who cruise through permitting are not lucky. They prepared.

This chapter walks you through how building departments actually work, what to bring to a pre-application meeting, the complete sequence from first phone call to permit issuance, and — in detail — exactly what to say when you're standing at that counter. The permit process is not a black box. It has rules, timelines, and pressure points you can use to your advantage if you understand how the machinery runs.

One number to hold in mind: plan check timelines across the country range from two weeks in a fast jurisdiction to twenty-four weeks in a slow one. That twelve-fold difference is not random. It reflects staffing, workload, political will, and — critically — how well-prepared the applicants in front of you are. A sloppy, incomplete submittal from the last applicant does not slow your file. But your own incomplete submittal will add weeks of delay at the worst possible moment, just when your contractor is standing by and your construction loan is accruing interest.

Let's start with the machinery.

---

## Section 1: How Building Departments Actually Work

### Two Counters, Two Different Conversations

The first thing most homeowners get wrong is conflating two separate agencies: the **planning department** (sometimes called the zoning counter or community development department) and the **building department** (sometimes called the building and safety division or development services). They are different offices with different staff, different code books, and different legal authorities. You need both, and you need to talk to them in the right order.

The planning department controls land use. They enforce your zoning code — the rules that say what can be built where, at what size, at what height, with what setbacks. When you ask whether an ADU is allowed on your lot, and what the development standards are, you're asking a planning question. Planning reviews your project for conformance with local ordinances and, increasingly, state ADU law.

The building department controls construction. They review your structural drawings, energy compliance documentation, and utility plans for conformance with adopted building codes — typically versions of the International Building Code (IBC) or International Residential Code (IRC), with local amendments. Once planning has approved your project (or confirmed it's ministerially approvable), the building department takes over.

In smaller jurisdictions, one office may handle both functions. In larger cities, they are entirely separate departments with separate application queues and separate reviewers. Know which office you're dealing with before you go, and understand that a approval from one counter does not guarantee approval from the other.

### The Pre-Application Meeting

A **pre-application meeting** (sometimes called a pre-app, a pre-submittal conference, or a conceptual review) is an informal session with planning and sometimes building staff before you formally submit your application. Not every jurisdiction offers them, but most jurisdictions with active ADU programs do, and requesting one is almost always worth the effort.

What you bring to a pre-app is a rough site plan, a proposed floor plan concept, and your list of questions. What you get back is informal, non-binding guidance on how staff will likely treat your project, which issues they anticipate, and what your submittal needs to include. This information is priceless. An hour with a planner before you spend $8,000–$15,000 on construction drawings can prevent a completely avoidable redesign after submittal.

To schedule a pre-app meeting, call the planning department directly and ask whether one is available for an ADU project. In many jurisdictions you can request one online. The wait time for a pre-app meeting varies from same-week to four to six weeks, so factor this into your timeline. Some jurisdictions charge a fee for pre-app meetings — typically $100–$500 — which is money well spent.

At the pre-app meeting, bring the questions from Section 4 of this chapter. Take notes. Get the staff person's name and direct contact information. And do not assume that the answers you receive are final — pre-app guidance is not a binding determination, and a different reviewer may reach different conclusions at plan check. That said, documented pre-app guidance is useful leverage if your project encounters unexpected pushback later.

### Ministerial vs. Discretionary Review

This distinction matters enormously for your timeline, and it's one most homeowners don't understand until it costs them months.

**Discretionary review** means a government decision-maker exercises judgment. Hearings may be required. Neighbors may have the right to comment or appeal. Design review boards may weigh in on aesthetics. A discretionary process can add three to twelve months to an ADU project and introduces genuine risk of denial. In the era before state ADU preemption laws swept the country, most ADU applications went through discretionary review. Many were denied on subjective grounds.

**Ministerial review** means the planning department evaluates your project against objective, written standards — and if you meet those standards, they must approve your project. No hearing. No neighbor veto. No design review. The approval is mandatory and non-discretionary. This is the regime most state ADU preemption laws now mandate, and it is a transformational change for ADU applicants.

In California, Government Code § 66333 requires that ADU applications on lots with existing dwellings be processed ministerially — and mandates action within 60 days of a complete application [S1]. In Washington, HB 1337 provisions enacted similar ministerial pathways, with local jurisdictions required to process applications consistent with state standards [S2]. Oregon's ORS 197A.425 establishes ministerial ADU approval requirements in cities over 2,500 population within urban growth boundaries [S3].

What this means for you: if you're in a state with a strong ministerial ADU statute, and your application meets the objective development standards, the city cannot deny your permit for subjective reasons. They cannot say it doesn't fit the neighborhood character. They cannot require design changes to match surrounding architecture. They cannot send you to a hearing. If they try any of these things, you have legal recourse — and the mere fact of citing the statute, calmly and correctly, often resolves the issue on the spot.

> **State Watch:** California's ministerial approval timeline runs from the date of a *complete* application, not from the submission date. If your application is missing documents, the 60-day clock does not start. The city is required to notify you of incompleteness within 30 days of submission. This completeness determination is itself regulated under Government Code § 66333 [S1].

### What "Complete Application" Means

This is a point most homeowners stumble on, and the stumble is expensive. Most jurisdictions with permit timelines defined by statute — including California's 60-day window and Washington's 120-day processing requirement — measure those timelines from the date of a **complete application**, meaning an application that contains every required document and piece of information.

If you submit an incomplete application, the clock does not run. The city will send you a correction notice listing what's missing, and your timeline restarts only after you respond with the outstanding items. In the worst cases, an incomplete submittal results in a void application that you have to resubmit from scratch, with a new queue position.

The practical lesson: before you submit anything, get the jurisdiction's complete submittal checklist. Verify every item on that checklist. If you're uncertain whether a specific document is required, call and ask — or ask at your pre-app meeting. Submit complete the first time, because an incomplete submittal is not a shortcut; it's a detour.

## Section 2: The Pre-Application Checklist

Before you submit an ADU permit application, the following documents and information should be in hand. Not all items are required in every jurisdiction — your pre-application meeting will clarify exactly what your city requires — but assembling this list before you engage an architect or designer will prevent mid-design surprises that cost money and time.

**Site Documentation**

1. Current site survey or assessor's parcel map, showing lot dimensions, area, and existing improvements. A licensed surveyor's plat is preferred over GIS or county assessor data, which is often imprecise enough to create setback problems.
2. Existing structure documentation: year of construction, square footage, number of stories, existing permitted floor plans if available, and any record of prior permits pulled on the property. You can request permit history from the building department — most jurisdictions maintain digital records going back at least 20–30 years.
3. Existing coverage calculations: the total footprint of all existing structures (house, garage, sheds) as a percentage of lot area. You need to know your current lot coverage before your designer can determine how much room you have left.

**Proposed Design Documents**

4. Proposed site plan, drawn to scale and dimensioned, showing the location of the proposed ADU relative to all property lines, existing structures, and any easements or utilities. This should be preliminary but accurate — a sketch your architect produces is fine for a pre-app; the formal version needs to be sealed for permit submittal.
5. Proposed floor plans, drawn to scale and dimensioned, showing room layout, window and door locations, and finished square footage.
6. Proposed elevations for all four sides of the structure, showing exterior materials, window sizes, roofline, and height from grade to peak.

**Engineering and Technical Documents**

7. Structural engineering calculations and drawings, typically required for detached ADUs. The scope varies by jurisdiction, but most will require at minimum a foundation plan, framing plan, and structural calculations bearing an engineer's stamp.
8. Soils or geotechnical report, required if your property is in a geologic hazard area (landslide, liquefaction, or fault zone) or if the soil conditions suggest expansive clay or fill. Check your county's hazard maps before assuming you don't need one — a surprise soils requirement after submittal can add $3,000–$8,000 and four to six weeks to your project.
9. Title 24 energy compliance documentation (California) or your state's equivalent residential energy code compliance report. In California, this is typically a HERS (Home Energy Rating System) or CF1R compliance form prepared by a certified Title 24 consultant. Other states have parallel requirements under the IECC (International Energy Conservation Code) or state-specific energy codes.
10. Utility connection plan, showing proposed connections to water, sewer, gas (if applicable), and electrical service. For attached ADUs, this often means documenting that the existing service has capacity. For detached ADUs, you may need written confirmation from each utility that a connection is available and what it will cost.
11. Stormwater and drainage calculations, required in some jurisdictions — particularly those with municipal separate storm sewer systems (MS4 permits) or in watersheds with known flooding or water quality concerns. Ask during your pre-app meeting whether this applies.

> **Pro Tip:** Request the jurisdiction's ADU permit submittal checklist in writing before your pre-app meeting. Many cities post these on their websites, but the posted version is not always current. Ask for the current version and confirm the date it was last updated. Requirements change, and a checklist that was accurate 18 months ago may be missing a new requirement added since the last legislative session.

## Section 3: The Permit Application Sequence

The permit process is a sequence, not a single transaction. Each step has dependencies, and rushing a step typically creates problems two steps later. Here is the complete sequence, with realistic timing at each stage.

**Step 1: Pre-Application Meeting (Weeks 1–2)**

Contact the planning department to schedule a pre-app meeting. Bring your rough site concept, your parcel information, and the questions outlined in Section 4. Your goal at this meeting is to confirm zoning compliance, identify any site-specific issues the city anticipates, understand the complete application requirements, and get a realistic sense of current plan check timelines. This step is optional but strongly recommended for any project above a simple garage conversion.

**Step 2: Design Refinement (Weeks 2–5)**

Take the feedback from your pre-app meeting and work with your architect or designer to develop complete construction documents. This phase takes longer than most homeowners expect, particularly if structural engineering, energy compliance, or geotechnical work needs to be ordered and completed. Factor in that engineers and Title 24 consultants typically have two- to four-week lead times. Avoid rushing this step — an incomplete or inaccurate set of construction documents is the leading cause of plan check corrections.

**Step 3: Complete Application Assembly (Weeks 5–7)**

Gather every document on the submittal checklist, verify completeness against the checklist item by item, and prepare the application package. Pay the application fee at this stage — most jurisdictions require fee payment at submittal, not at permit issuance. Fees for an ADU permit application typically run $2,000–$8,000 depending on the jurisdiction and project valuation, with some high-cost cities exceeding $10,000 for complex projects [S4].

**Step 4: Application Submission (Week 7–8)**

Submit your complete application package. At submission, confirm the assigned plan checker's name and contact information, and ask the approximate date when you can expect either a completeness determination or the first plan check correction letter. In California, the city must determine completeness within 30 days of submission [S1]. Keep a copy of every document in the submittal package — you will need them.

**Step 5: Plan Check Period**

This is the most variable step in the entire process, and the one most likely to affect your construction start date. Plan check timelines vary dramatically by jurisdiction, staffing, and application volume. The table below gives realistic current ranges for representative markets.

| Jurisdiction | Permit Program | Typical ADU Plan Check Timeline |
|---|---|---|
| Los Angeles (ADU Express Program) | Ministerial, pre-approved plans | 2–4 weeks |
| Denver | Ministerial ADU pathway | 4–8 weeks |
| Portland | Ministerial ADU pathway | 6–10 weeks |
| Seattle | Ministerial ADU pathway | 6–12 weeks |
| San Jose | Ministerial, pre-approved plans available | 6–10 weeks |
| Sacramento | Ministerial, pre-approved plans available | 4–8 weeks |
| San Francisco | Ministerial ADU pathway | 10–20 weeks |
| Oakland | Ministerial ADU pathway | 12–18 weeks |
| Berkeley | Ministerial ADU pathway | 12–24 weeks |

*Note: Timeline data reflects reported applicant experience as of early 2026 and is subject to change as staffing and application volumes shift. Verify current timelines with the jurisdiction before setting your construction schedule.*

The variation between LA's ADU Express program and Berkeley's queue is not primarily a function of state law — all three Bay Area cities operate under the same California ministerial statute. It reflects staffing levels, application volume, and local political investment in ADU processing speed. Some jurisdictions have dedicated ADU plan checkers. Others route ADU applications through the same queue as commercial tenant improvements and major remodels.

**Step 6: Plan Check Corrections**

It is common, particularly for first-time ADU applicants, to receive a plan check correction letter — a document listing specific deficiencies that must be resolved before the permit can issue. This is not a rejection. It is a request for revisions or additional information.

When you receive a correction letter, read it carefully and respond to every item. Most jurisdictions require you to submit a written response that addresses each correction individually, accompanied by revised plans if the correction requires a drawing change. Do not ignore correction letters or let them sit — most jurisdictions treat non-response within 30 days as application abandonment, which means your application can be voided and your fees forfeited.

> **Common Mistake:** Homeowners sometimes receive a correction letter with 12 items and respond to 11, either missing one or assuming one item is a mistake. The plan checker will return the package again, and you lose another cycle. Read every item, respond to every item, and when in doubt, call the plan checker directly to confirm your understanding before you revise.

**Step 7: Permit Issuance**

Once the plan checker is satisfied that all corrections have been addressed, the permit is issued. You will receive a permit card and approved stamped plan set. Post the permit card at the job site — this is a legal requirement in virtually every jurisdiction. The approved plan set must be on-site at all times during construction for the inspector to reference.

**Step 8: Construction and Inspection Milestones**

Once the permit is issued, construction begins. Your permit will specify which inspections are required and at what milestones — typically including foundation, framing, rough-in (electrical, plumbing, mechanical), insulation, and final. Each inspection must be scheduled in advance, and the inspector must sign off on each phase before work proceeds to the next. Do not bury rough-in work before the rough-in inspection is complete and approved. Do not close up walls until framing is signed off. Skipping inspections is not a shortcut — it creates a mandatory re-inspection, potential forced demolition of covered work, and a permanent cloud on your property record.

**Step 9: Final Inspection and Certificate of Occupancy**

When construction is complete, you request a final inspection. The inspector walks the finished ADU and verifies that the constructed work matches the approved plans and meets all applicable code requirements. If the final inspection passes, the city issues a **certificate of occupancy (CO)** — the legal document that authorizes occupancy of the unit. Without a CO, the ADU cannot be legally occupied, rented, or represented to a lender as an existing improvement. The CO is the finish line.

See Chapter 11 for a detailed walkthrough of the final inspection process, common failure points, and how to prepare.

## Section 4: The Permit Counter Script

### What to Say at the Planning Counter

Most homeowners walk into the planning counter and say something vague like "I want to build a garage apartment" or "I'm thinking about an ADU — can I do that?" That approach puts you in the position of a supplicant asking permission. The questions that follow tend to be broad, the answers tend to be broad, and you walk out knowing little more than you did when you walked in.

The homeowners who get useful information at the planning counter come prepared. They ask specific questions in a specific order. They bring paper and write down the answers. They treat the counter visit as an information-gathering mission, not a casual conversation. Here is exactly what to say, in the sequence that produces the most useful results.

---

**CALL OR VISIT OPENING**

Whether you're calling by phone or visiting in person, start with your location and your purpose:

*"I'm at [full property address], a single-family residential lot. I'm researching the feasibility of an ADU before I start any design work. I have a few specific questions — is this a good time, or should I schedule a pre-application meeting?"*

This framing accomplishes three things: it identifies your property immediately so the staff person can pull up your parcel information, it signals that you're organized and early in the process (which planners appreciate — it means they're less likely to be cleaning up a mistake you've already made), and it gives them the option to direct you to a more appropriate channel if your questions are complex enough to warrant a formal pre-app meeting.

---

**QUESTION 1 — Zoning and Approval Pathway**

*"Can you confirm whether this lot is zoned to allow an ADU by right, or is there a discretionary process that applies?"*

What you're listening for: the specific zoning designation, whether ADUs are a permitted use by right in that zone, and whether any overlay districts (historic, hillside, flood, coastal) might trigger additional review. In states with ministerial ADU statutes, the answer should be "by right" — but it's worth confirming, because some jurisdictions have not yet updated their local codes to reflect state law, and you may need to invoke the state statute if staff gives you an incorrect answer.

---

**QUESTION 2 — Objective Development Standards**

*"What are the current objective development standards that apply to this lot? Specifically, I'm asking about maximum ADU size, setback requirements, height limit, and maximum lot coverage."*

What you're listening for: the specific numbers that will govern your design. Write these down exactly as stated. "Four-foot rear setback" versus "five-foot rear setback" versus "no setback for conversions within existing structures" makes a material difference to what you can build. If the staff person is uncertain about any of these numbers, ask them to point you to the relevant code section so you can verify independently.

---

**QUESTION 3 — Pre-Approved Plans**

*"Does your jurisdiction have a pre-approved ADU plan program? If so, can I get a copy of the approved plan catalogue, or a link to where those plans are posted?"*

What you're listening for: whether a pre-approved plan program exists, what the plans look like and whether any might fit your site, and what the plan check timeline is for pre-approved designs versus custom plans. Pre-approved plans can reduce plan check time from weeks to days in jurisdictions that have invested in these programs. Section 6 of this chapter covers how to use them.

---

**QUESTION 4 — Complete Application Requirements**

*"What does a complete ADU permit application require for this jurisdiction? Specifically, I want to confirm whether you require a soils report, structural engineering, and energy code compliance documentation."*

What you're listening for: the specific submittal checklist and any requirements that are jurisdiction-specific rather than standard. Ask them to email you the current submittal checklist if one exists. Get their email address so you can follow up in writing. This question also serves as a natural lead-in to ask about specific items you're uncertain about — for example, whether a licensed surveyor's plat is required or whether the county assessor's map is sufficient for the site plan.

---

**QUESTION 5 — Current Plan Check Timeline**

*"What is your current plan check timeline for ADU applications? I'm trying to set a realistic construction start date."*

What you're listening for: a specific number of weeks, not "it depends." If they say "it depends," ask them what it depends on and what the range is. Ask whether there's a dedicated ADU reviewer or whether applications go into the general plan check queue. Some jurisdictions have separate ADU express queues with committed timelines; others do not. This information directly affects when you can tell your contractor to mobilize.

---

**QUESTION 6 — Pre-Application Meeting**

*"Is there a pre-application meeting available before I submit, and how do I schedule one? Is there a fee?"*

What you're listening for: the process, timeline, and cost to schedule a pre-app meeting. If they confirm a pre-app is available, schedule it before you leave (or before you hang up the phone). If your questions from Questions 1 through 5 were not fully answered in this initial conversation, the pre-app meeting is where you'll get the complete picture.

---

**TAKE NOTES PROTOCOL**

Before you ask a single question, open your phone's notes application or pick up a pen. Write down the name of the staff person you are speaking with, the date, and the time. Then write down their answers to each question verbatim — not your paraphrase, but their exact language.

This is not bureaucratic caution; it's practical protection. Building department staff turn over. Codes get updated. What one planner tells you in March may not be what a different planner tells your contractor in July. If there is ever a conflict between what staff told you and what the code says — or between what one staff member said and what another says later — your contemporaneous, dated notes are your evidence. They won't win every argument, but they demonstrate good faith and create a record that can be escalated if necessary.

If the staff person tells you something important and uncertain — for instance, "I think that setback would apply, but you'd want to confirm with the planning director" — write that down exactly, including the hedge. That verbatim quote matters later if the issue becomes a dispute.

---

**IF THEY SAY "WE DON'T ALLOW ADUs"**

In a state with a ministerial ADU preemption statute — California, Washington, Oregon, Massachusetts, Colorado, and a growing list of others — a staff member who tells you ADUs are not allowed on your lot may simply be wrong. This happens more than it should, particularly in smaller jurisdictions where front-counter staff have not been fully trained on recent state-law changes.

Your response:

*"My understanding is that state law requires ADU approval by right in this zone. Under [California Government Code § 66333 / Washington RCW 36.70A.681 / ORS 197A.425 — whichever applies], ministerial ADU approval is required for lots with existing dwellings. Can I speak with the planning director or a senior planner to confirm whether that state preemption applies here?"*

This is not confrontational — it's a precise, reasonable question. You are not accusing the staff person of lying; you're acknowledging that state law is complex and asking for confirmation from a more senior resource. In most cases, invoking the specific statutory citation resolves the issue. In the rare case it does not, document everything and consult with a land use attorney before investing further in design.

> **Common Mistake:** Homeowners sometimes interpret a front-counter "no" as a definitive, final answer. It is not. It is one staff person's read on a complex legal landscape, at a busy counter, under time pressure. Verify statutory rights before accepting a no.

## Section 5: Common Rejection Reasons and How to Avoid Them

Plan check corrections and outright deferrals are almost always preventable. After reviewing the patterns in ADU permitting across multiple jurisdictions, the same issues recur. Here are the most common reasons ADU applications get rejected or returned for corrections, and the specific prevention steps for each.

**1. Incomplete Submittal**

Missing documents — a soils report, structural calculations, or energy compliance form — are the leading cause of initial plan check deferrals. The plan checker cannot legally begin substantive review on an incomplete submittal in many jurisdictions, so your application sits in a holding pattern until the missing items arrive.

*Prevention:* Obtain the jurisdiction's current submittal checklist before preparing your application package. Verify every item on the list before submitting. If you're uncertain whether a specific document is required, email the planning department and request written confirmation. Submit complete the first time.

**2. Setback Violation on the Site Plan**

The proposed ADU location shown on your site plan violates a setback requirement — most commonly a rear or side setback. This often happens when the designer relies on an assessor's parcel map rather than a licensed survey, or when the actual lot dimensions differ slightly from county records.

*Prevention:* Have a licensed land surveyor confirm your actual lot dimensions and property lines before your architect finalizes the site plan. Survey costs typically run $1,500–$3,500 for a standard residential lot, and they can prevent a design that needs to be moved or reduced in size after plan check. See Chapter 2 for the full discussion of site survey requirements.

**3. Lot Coverage Exceeded**

The proposed ADU, combined with the existing house and other structures, exceeds the maximum lot coverage allowed in the zone.

*Prevention:* Calculate your existing lot coverage before beginning design work. Include all structures: the main house, attached or detached garages, covered patios, sheds, and other roofed structures that count toward coverage under your local code. The definition of what counts as "coverage" varies — some jurisdictions count only the building footprint, others include paved areas or overhangs. Confirm the local definition during your pre-app meeting.

**4. Utility Plan Insufficient**

The proposed utility connections — water, sewer, or electrical service — are described inadequately or the documentation doesn't demonstrate that service is available and adequate.

*Prevention:* Before finalizing your design, contact each utility provider and get written confirmation that service is available to the ADU location and at the proposed load. For water and sewer, ask whether the existing service lateral has capacity for a second unit or whether a separate connection will be required. Utility upgrade costs can run $5,000–$30,000 or more depending on distance and existing infrastructure, and discovering this requirement after design is complete changes your project economics.

**5. Fire Separation Distance Inadequate**

The proposed ADU is too close to the property line to meet fire separation requirements under the adopted building code. This is distinct from the zoning setback — even if you meet the planning department's zoning setback, the building code may require additional separation based on the construction type and wall materials.

*Prevention:* Confirm both the zoning setback and the building code fire separation requirement before finalizing the building location. In many jurisdictions, a one-hour fire-rated wall assembly can allow placement closer to the property line than would otherwise be permitted — but this requires specific construction details that must be shown in your plans.

**6. Non-Compliant Energy Calculations**

The energy compliance documentation doesn't meet current code requirements, typically because the Title 24 report (California) or equivalent was prepared against an outdated code cycle, or because the report was prepared by someone who made incorrect assumptions about the building envelope or mechanical systems.

*Prevention:* Use a licensed Title 24 consultant in California, or a qualified energy compliance specialist in other states. Do not ask your drafter or designer to prepare the energy compliance documentation unless they are specifically certified and current on the applicable code cycle. Energy code requirements change with each code update cycle, and a report prepared under a prior cycle may be invalid at plan check.

**7. Plans Unsigned by Licensed Architect or Engineer**

The jurisdiction requires that construction documents bear the stamp and wet or electronic signature of a California-licensed architect, structural engineer, or both — and the submitted plans are unsigned or signed by someone without the required license.

*Prevention:* During your pre-app meeting or initial inquiry, confirm who is authorized to sign your ADU plans. Requirements vary: some jurisdictions accept plans prepared by a licensed designer or experienced drafter for simple single-story ADUs; others require a licensed architect's stamp regardless of project complexity. This is a non-negotiable at plan check — unsigned or improperly signed plans cannot be approved.

## Section 6: The Pre-Approved Plans Shortcut

### How Pre-Approved Plans Work

A **pre-approved plan program** (sometimes called a standard plan program) is a library of ADU designs that has already been reviewed and approved by the building department. When you build from one of these pre-approved designs, the structural, architectural, and often energy compliance review has already been done — only the site-specific review (setbacks, lot coverage, utility connections, soils) remains. This can reduce plan check time from weeks to days.

Not every jurisdiction offers a pre-approved plan program, but the number doing so has grown significantly as cities look for ways to reduce processing backlogs and incentivize ADU construction. The jurisdictions with the most active programs as of early 2026 include Los Angeles, San Jose, Sacramento, San Diego, and Portland (limited catalog).

### Los Angeles: The Gold Standard

The Los Angeles Standard Plan Program — administered by the Los Angeles Department of Building and Safety (LADBS) — is the most developed pre-approved ADU plan program in the country [S5]. The program offers a catalogue of pre-approved ADU designs ranging from approximately 450 to 1,200 square feet, in multiple configurations including attached, detached, and garage conversion layouts.

When you select a pre-approved Los Angeles standard plan, the plan check time for the structural and architectural review portion is dramatically reduced — in many cases to two to four weeks under the city's ADU Express program, compared to eight to twelve weeks for custom plans. The site-specific components (site plan, soils, utilities) still require review, but these are substantially simpler and faster than a full plan check.

To use the LA Standard Plan Program, you download the pre-approved plans from the LADBS website, verify that the design fits your lot's setback and coverage constraints, and submit the standard plan package along with your site-specific documents [S5]. Your architect or designer typically needs to prepare a site plan and verify compliance with site-specific conditions even when using a standard plan — this is not a no-professional-required shortcut, but it is a meaningful time and cost reduction.

### Other Jurisdictions With Pre-Approved Programs

San Jose offers a pre-approved ADU plan library through its Planning, Building, and Code Enforcement department. Sacramento has developed a standard plan program with an emphasis on lower-cost ADU types that help close the gap between permit approval and actual construction. Portland's program is more limited but covers basic attached ADU configurations.

The challenge with pre-approved plans is fit. These designs are standardized, and your lot is not. If the pre-approved design requires a configuration that doesn't match your site — a particular setback orientation, a foundation type that doesn't work on your soils, or dimensions that don't meet your intended use — then a custom design is the right path. Do not force a pre-approved plan onto a site where it doesn't genuinely work just to save plan check time; the downstream problems in construction are more expensive than the time you saved at the permit counter.

> **Pro Tip:** Even if you plan to build a custom ADU, review your jurisdiction's pre-approved plan catalogue before you start design. The catalogue tells you what the building department has already approved and considered constructible — which is useful design input. You may find that your functional requirements can be met by a pre-approved design with minor modifications, or you may identify design features in the pre-approved plans that your designer can incorporate into a custom design that processes faster because it's familiar to plan checkers.

## Section 7: Communicating With the Building Department

### Get the Right Contact and Keep a Paper Trail

At the time you submit your application, ask the counter staff for the name and direct contact information of the plan checker assigned to your file. In larger jurisdictions, applications may be routed to a team rather than a named individual — ask for the team email address or case number, and clarify how to reach the person actually reviewing your documents. This information is more important than most homeowners realize.

When your correction letter arrives, your response needs to go to the right person, in the right format, with the right case reference number. A response submitted to the general counter email without your case number attached may sit for days before it reaches the correct reviewer. Know your assigned contact.

### Email Over Phone for Everything That Matters

For any communication that involves a factual question, a decision, or an agreement, use email rather than phone. Phone calls are informal and undocumented. Emails create a record. When you ask the plan checker to confirm that a specific condition has been met, ask by email and wait for an email response. When you discuss a proposed solution to a correction item, document the conversation in a follow-up email: "Per our conversation today, you confirmed that [specific solution] would satisfy correction item 4. I'll reflect this in the revised plans."

This is not about being litigious. It is about creating a shared record that prevents misunderstandings two weeks later when a different reviewer picks up your file and doesn't know what was discussed. Good documentation protects both you and the building department.

### Respond Within 30 Days of Every Correction Notice

Most jurisdictions treat non-response to a plan check correction letter as application abandonment after 30 days. This means your permit application is voided, your application fee is forfeited, and you must start the process over from scratch with a new submittal. Set a calendar reminder the day you receive a correction letter: you have 30 days. Even if you cannot fully respond within 30 days — because your engineer needs time to revise structural calculations, for example — contact the plan checker and request an extension in writing. Most jurisdictions will grant reasonable extension requests when asked proactively. They will not automatically grant one when you let the deadline pass without communication.

### Escalating a Stalled Application

If your application has been in plan check for significantly longer than the jurisdiction's stated timeline, or if you've been waiting more than two weeks for a response to a correction resubmittal, it is appropriate to escalate. The escalation path runs: assigned plan checker → plan check supervisor → building official → planning director.

Start at the lowest level. Contact the assigned plan checker first and ask for a status update. If that doesn't produce a response within three business days, contact their supervisor. Be professional and specific: state your case number, the date of submission, the date of the correction response, and the amount of time that has passed since. Ask for a specific date by which you can expect action.

> **Pro Tip:** The phrase that consistently opens doors with building department staff is: "I want to help you help me get this approved. What specifically needs to change to make this a complete, approvable submittal?" This framing positions you as a collaborator rather than an adversary. It makes clear that you accept the department's authority and are asking for their expert guidance — which is what most building department staff want to provide. Confrontational homeowners get slower service. Collaborative homeowners get the same code applied with maximum helpful interpretation.

### If You Believe There Is a Legal Error

Occasionally, a plan checker will apply a code requirement incorrectly, or will require something not in your jurisdiction's stated submittal requirements, or will deny something that state law requires be approved. If you believe a legal error has occurred — not a judgment call you disagree with, but an actual conflict with state statute or adopted code — your first step is to request a written explanation of the requirement and the code section on which it is based.

Read that code section yourself. Look at what the code actually says, not what the staff member says it says. If there is a genuine conflict, escalate to the building official. If escalation doesn't resolve it, consult a land use attorney. In states with strong ministerial ADU statutes, attorneys who specialize in ADU law are increasingly available, and a single letter from counsel often resolves what months of counter conversations could not.

## Closing: The Permit Is the Starting Gun

Getting your permit approved is not the end of anything. It is the beginning of the construction phase — which brings its own complexity, its own timeline pressures, and its own opportunities to protect your investment or lose it. The permit card posted to your fence is authorization, not accomplishment. What happens next — who manages the build, how you track progress, how you handle the inspections that the permit requires — is where most ADU projects succeed or fail.

Chapter 9 covers construction management for homeowners: how to run a project you're not physically building, how to manage a contractor relationship, how to track the draw schedule, and how to catch problems before they become expensive. The permit got you to the starting line. Chapter 9 is about running the race.
