# Chapter 7 — Assembling Your Team: Contractor Selection

You've just gotten three contractor bids. They range from $180,000 to $310,000. One contractor is someone your neighbor used for a kitchen remodel and vouches for enthusiastically. Another dropped off a polished proposal with a company logo and a glossy brochure. The third was the most businesslike: he asked detailed questions about your site, mentioned the permit requirements by name, and his bid took four pages to summarize. You don't know how to evaluate any of them — and the $130,000 spread between the high and low bids makes you nervous in ways you can't articulate yet.

That nervousness is well-placed. The contractor you hire will determine more about your ADU's outcome — its quality, its timeline, its final cost, and whether the whole experience breaks you — than almost any other decision in this project. A good contractor on a mediocre design will usually produce a livable result. A bad contractor on a perfect design will produce a construction nightmare. This chapter is about not making that mistake.

## Section 1: The Contractor Landscape

Before you evaluate any specific contractor, you need to understand the four delivery models available for ADU construction. They differ in how accountability is structured, how costs are distributed, and what risks the homeowner carries. Choosing the wrong model for your project is a mistake you'll pay for throughout construction.

### Traditional General Contractor

A **general contractor (GC)** is a licensed professional who manages the overall construction process, coordinates subcontractors — the electricians, plumbers, framers, and finish trades — and is responsible for delivering the completed structure. In the traditional model, you hire your architect or designer separately, get your plans permitted separately, and then bring in a GC to build from those approved plans.

This model works well when you have a custom design from an architect you trust, want competitive bidding on the construction phase, and are willing to manage the relationship between your designer and your builder. The design-build interface — the handoff from drawings to construction — is your responsibility to manage. When a conflict arises between what the plans show and what the field conditions require, you are in the middle.

The traditional GC model can be cost-competitive because you're separating design and construction into competitive contracts. But that competitive advantage disappears if your design drawings are incomplete or ambiguous, because incomplete drawings produce change orders, and change orders at contractor markup are expensive.

### Design-Build Firm

A **design-build firm** handles both the architectural design and the construction under a single contract. One company is responsible for the whole project, from initial site assessment through final inspection. This consolidates accountability in a way the traditional model cannot: when something goes wrong, there is no finger-pointing between your designer and your builder because they're the same organization.

Design-build firms have proliferated in the ADU market specifically because ADUs are a defined product type that lends itself to systematized delivery. Many design-build firms have pre-approved plan sets they've already permitted in your jurisdiction, which can cut two to four months off your timeline. The tradeoff is less customization — their plans are optimized for their system, not your specific preferences — and often a price premium of 10–20% over what you might achieve by separating design and construction. That premium frequently earns itself back in reduced change orders and faster delivery.

If you value simplicity, clear accountability, and a faster path to occupancy, design-build deserves serious consideration. If you have a complex lot, specific design requirements, or want to engage a local architect you already trust, the traditional GC model may serve you better.

### Prefab and Modular ADU Companies

**Prefab** and **modular ADU companies** manufacture the unit — or significant components of it — in a factory and deliver it to your site for installation. The appeal is speed: factory construction is not subject to weather delays, labor scheduling conflicts, or site conditions. A modular unit can be manufactured while your foundation is being poured, then delivered and set in a single day.

The common underestimation with prefab is the site work. The unit price the manufacturer quotes is only part of the total cost. Site preparation, foundation work, utility connections, and local permitting add $40,000–$100,000 or more to most prefab projects, depending on your jurisdiction and site conditions. In some markets, the all-in cost of a prefab ADU is not materially different from stick-built construction, but with a faster timeline.

Prefab also requires your lot to accommodate large crane or delivery equipment — not all infill lots can do this — and not all jurisdictions have streamlined permitting for factory-built structures. California has, and it has made modular ADU delivery significantly easier in most cities. Other states are less consistent. Before pursuing a prefab company, verify that your jurisdiction treats factory-built structures the same as site-built for permitting purposes. Chapter 5 covers state-level regulatory treatment in detail.

### Owner-Builder

An **owner-builder** is a homeowner who pulls their own permit, hires and manages all subcontractors directly, and takes legal responsibility as the contractor of record. In most states, owner-builder permits are available to homeowners who will occupy the structure and who can demonstrate sufficient knowledge of the work.

The appeal is cost savings — eliminating GC markup, which typically runs 15–25% of construction costs. On a $250,000 project, that's $37,500–$62,500 in potential savings. The reality is that those savings come with enormous risk unless you have professional construction experience. You become responsible for scheduling, quality control, lien management, inspection coordination, and subcontractor disputes. If a subcontractor walks off your job halfway through rough electrical, you need to know how to find a licensed replacement, verify their work, and keep the schedule moving. If the building inspector flags a structural issue, you need to understand what they're flagging and how to resolve it.

Most homeowners without construction management backgrounds underestimate this complexity by a factor of two or three. Owner-builder projects also carry a specific resale risk: in California, owner-built homes must be disclosed as such to buyers for ten years, which some buyers and their lenders treat as a red flag. Other states have similar disclosure requirements. If you are a construction professional considering the owner-builder path, this guide will help you structure the process. If you are not, this is the riskiest path available, and the cost savings rarely survive the project intact.

> **Pro Tip:** The right delivery model depends on your priorities, not the contractor's preferences. Ask any design-build firm why you shouldn't hire a GC separately, and they'll give you design-build advantages. Ask a GC why you shouldn't use design-build, and they'll give you GC advantages. Run this analysis yourself before you start interviewing anyone.

## Section 2: Finding Candidates

The most common way homeowners find contractors is the worst way to find contractors: a neighbor recommendation, a Google search, or a HomeAdvisor/Angi inquiry. None of these are inherently unreliable, but none of them give you what you actually need — a contractor with demonstrated, verifiable ADU project history in your specific jurisdiction.

### Where Not to Start

Yelp and HomeAdvisor reviews tell you about customer satisfaction, not construction quality. A contractor can have five-star reviews for bathroom remodels and be completely unprepared for the complexity of ADU permitting and construction. HomeAdvisor and Angi are lead-generation services: contractors pay to appear in results, and the vetting is cursory. Neighbor referrals are valuable as one data point but dangerous as a sole qualification criterion. Your neighbor's kitchen addition has almost nothing in common with a detached ADU permitted under your city's specific ADU ordinance.

### Where to Start

**State contractor license lookup databases** are your first stop, not your last. Every state with a contractor licensing system maintains a public database where you can search by name, company, or license number and see license status, expiration date, any disciplinary actions, and often insurance status. This is the ground truth on whether a contractor is legally qualified to work in your state. Links for the five most active ADU markets are in the sources section below [S2, S3, S4].

The **National Association of the Remodeling Industry (NARI)** [S5] maintains a contractor directory organized by specialty and geography. NARI membership requires demonstrated experience and adherence to a code of ethics, which is not a guarantee of quality but is a reasonable baseline screen. Your local NARI chapter is also a good source for referrals to contractors who specialize in residential additions and ADUs.

Local chapters of the **Associated General Contractors of America (AGC)** maintain member directories that include residential builders in some regions, though AGC membership skews toward commercial contractors. More useful for ADU work is the local chapter of the **National Association of Home Builders (NAHB)**, which represents residential contractors and often has members who specialize in additions and ADUs.

**Local ADU permit records** are the most underused and most valuable sourcing tool available to homeowners. In most jurisdictions, permit records are public record and accessible under state open records laws. You can request — or in many cities, access online — a list of ADU permits issued in the past 24 months, which typically includes the contractor of record. This gives you a list of contractors who have actually permitted ADUs in your jurisdiction, which is the single most relevant qualification you can verify. In California, permit records are accessible through most city building department portals. In Washington, the L&I permit database is searchable. If online access is limited, submit a public records request — it's free in most jurisdictions and typically fulfilled within 5–10 business days.

> **Pro Tip:** Once you have contractor names from local permit records, cross-reference them against the state license lookup to check for complaints or disciplinary actions. A contractor with 12 ADU permits in your city and a clean license record is a fundamentally stronger candidate than someone your neighbor recommends, even if that neighbor was happy with their bathroom.

### The Minimum Qualification Screen

Before you invite a contractor to bid, run three checks. First, verify their license is active in your state and covers the scope of work. Second, confirm they carry **general liability insurance** (at minimum $1,000,000 per occurrence) and **workers' compensation insurance** for all employees. Ask for a certificate of insurance (COI) from their insurer directly — not a copy they hand you, which can be forged or outdated. Third, confirm they have built at least three ADUs in your jurisdiction or a comparable one within the past 24 months. Contractors who built plenty of room additions in 2019 and are now calling themselves ADU specialists may have limited experience with the specific permit requirements, utility connection processes, and inspection protocols that ADU projects require.

How you verify license status depends on your state. In **California**, the Contractors State License Board (CSLB) database [S2] allows you to look up any contractor by name or license number and see their classification, current status, bonding, and any complaints or disciplinary actions. In **Washington**, the Labor and Industries (L&I) contractor lookup [S3] shows registration status, insurance verification, and any violations. In **Oregon**, the Construction Contractors Board (CCB) [S4] maintains a similar searchable database. In **Massachusetts**, the Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration and the Construction Supervisor License (CSL) are tracked separately — search both through the Office of Consumer Affairs and Business Regulation. In **Colorado**, contractor licensing is primarily municipal rather than state-level, administered through the Department of Regulatory Agencies (DORA) for some trades; verify with your specific city's building department for contractor requirements.

Apply the **three viable candidates minimum** rule without exception. You cannot evaluate a bid in isolation. You need at least three qualified, vetted contractors to bid the same scope if you want meaningful price comparison. Two bids is too few to detect an outlier. One bid is no bid at all — it's just a number with no context. If you can't find three qualified candidates, widen your search radius, extend the permit record lookup to 36 months, or contact NARI for referrals before you start compromising your screening criteria.

## Section 3: The 15-Question Vetting Interview

Once you've assembled three or more qualified candidates through the screening process above, interview each one before inviting them to bid. The goal of this interview is not to be confrontational — it's to give each contractor the opportunity to demonstrate competence, and to give yourself the information you need to evaluate them. A contractor who is genuinely qualified will welcome these questions. A contractor who pushes back, gets defensive, or gives evasive answers is telling you something important.

Conduct all interviews in person or by video, not by email. You want to see how they communicate, how they think on their feet, and how they treat questions they might consider intrusive. Present these as standard questions you're asking all candidates — because they are.

The following 15 questions form a structured vetting interview. After each question, what constitutes a strong answer and what constitutes a red flag are noted. Take notes during the interview; your impressions will blur within 48 hours if you don't.

**1. How many ADUs have you permitted and built in this jurisdiction in the past 24 months?**

A strong answer is a specific number with specifics: "We've pulled seven ADU permits in [your city] in the past two years — five detached, two garage conversions." A red flag is vagueness ("several," "quite a few") or pivoting to adjacent experience ("We've done a lot of room additions"). Jurisdiction-specific ADU experience is not transferable in the way that general residential experience might suggest. The permit requirements, utility connection processes, and inspector relationships are specific to your city.

**2. Can you provide contact information for three ADU owners whose projects closed out in the last 18 months?**

A strong answer is an immediate yes, with names and phone numbers provided within 24 hours. A red flag is any hesitation, any qualification ("I'd need to ask permission"), or references who turn out to be unavailable, give vague answers, or whose projects don't match what the contractor described. You will call all three references. See the note at the end of this section on how to conduct a reference check that actually yields useful information.

**3. Do you carry general liability insurance of at least $1,000,000 per occurrence, and can you provide a certificate of insurance?**

A strong answer is yes, with a COI provided immediately or within 24 hours, from a recognized commercial insurer. A red flag is insurance below $1,000,000 per occurrence (inadequate for residential construction in most markets), a COI that looks like it might have been altered, or any delay in producing documentation. Before you finalize the contractor, call the insurance company listed on the COI to verify the policy is current. Policy numbers can be verified by phone in minutes.

**4. Do you carry workers' compensation for all employees and require it from your subcontractors?**

A strong answer is yes to both, with documentation. A red flag is "I only use 1099 subcontractors" as a workers' comp avoidance strategy — this creates significant liability exposure for you as the property owner if a worker is injured on your site. In most states, if a worker is injured on your property and the contractor has no workers' comp, your homeowner's insurance becomes the fallback, and your insurer may not be happy about it.

**5. How do you handle change orders — what triggers one and how quickly do you price them?**

A strong answer describes a formal written process: any deviation from the original scope triggers a written change order with a price, a timeline impact assessment, and your signature before any work proceeds. A red flag is "we're pretty flexible about those things" or any suggestion that they'll handle small changes verbally. Verbal change orders are not enforceable. Every undocumented change is a dispute waiting to happen.

**6. What is your draw schedule structure?**

A **draw schedule** is the payment plan tied to specific construction milestones — the sequence of payments that move from mobilization through punch list completion. A strong answer describes milestone-based draws tied to verified progress (foundation complete, rough framing complete, MEP rough-in complete, etc.), with the final payment held until after final inspection and punch list completion. A red flag is front-loaded draws (requesting 40–50% upfront), draws based on time elapsed rather than work completed, or vagueness about when draws are due.

**7. Who is the site supervisor, and what is their daily on-site commitment?**

A strong answer identifies a specific named individual with construction management experience and describes their schedule explicitly: "Our site super, [Name], will be on your project daily during active work phases, with scheduled check-ins during slower phases." A red flag is "I handle all my projects personally" from a GC who has five active projects, or inability to name the supervisor at all. The person who sells you the project is rarely the person who manages it day to day. You need to know who that person is before you sign a contract.

**8. What is your current backlog and projected start date?**

A strong answer gives you a realistic timeline with specifics: "We're currently finishing two projects; your mobilization would be approximately [date], subject to permit timing." A red flag is an unrealistically short start date ("We could start next week") — a good contractor is usually booked out 4–12 weeks, and a contractor with no backlog may have that backlog for a reason. Verify the projected start date against the permit timeline; a contractor who doesn't account for permitting in their start date projection hasn't thought carefully about your project.

**9. How do you document inspections and quality checkpoints?**

A strong answer describes a systematic approach: photos at each inspection, written inspection logs, copies of all inspection reports provided to the owner, and a defined process for addressing failed inspections. A red flag is "we take care of all that" without specifics. You are entitled to know what is happening on your project at every stage. A contractor who treats documentation as proprietary is a contractor who doesn't want you to have evidence when something goes wrong.

**10. What is your policy if you miss a construction milestone?**

A strong answer acknowledges that delays happen and describes a specific protocol: immediate notification, a written revised schedule, and accountability measures. A red flag is a defensive reaction or "we always finish on time" — no one always finishes on time in residential construction. The question isn't whether delays will happen; it's how the contractor handles them. You want someone who will tell you about a problem early, not someone who will hide it until it can't be hidden.

**11. How do you handle subcontractor selection — and can I see their license and insurance?**

A strong answer describes a vetting process: licensed subs, required COIs on file, long-term working relationships with specific trade contractors. A red flag is "I use whoever is available" or resistance to providing sub credentials. Subcontractors are doing most of the work on your project. Their license status and insurance coverage matters to you directly.

**12. What percentage of your ADU projects have exceeded original budget? By how much?**

A strong answer is honest and specific: "About 30% of our projects have had budget increases, mostly in the 5–10% range, driven primarily by owner-initiated changes or unforeseen site conditions." A red flag is "never" — that's not credible — or a number significantly higher than 15–20% without a convincing explanation. Budget overruns are normal; scope creep and poor change order management are not.

**13. Have you had any complaints filed with the state contractor board in the past three years?**

A strong answer discloses any complaints proactively and explains the resolution — because you're going to check the state database and find them anyway. A red flag is denial of complaints that you subsequently find in the database, or a pattern of complaints that suggests systematic problems rather than isolated disputes. One complaint in three years from a contractor with 30 projects is different from one complaint per year.

**14. Do you use a construction management platform — Buildertrend, Procore, CoConstruct, or similar — and why or why not?**

A strong answer either describes an active platform with specifics about how you'll have access, or provides a coherent alternative approach (some experienced smaller contractors use well-organized email and photo documentation that functions effectively). A red flag is dismissiveness: "We don't need software for that." Construction management platforms exist because informal communication on construction projects fails at a predictable rate. A contractor who rejects documentation tools is a contractor who hasn't thought carefully about how information gets lost.

**15. What is your process if a serious dispute arises and we can't resolve it directly?**

A strong answer describes a tiered approach: direct discussion first, then mediation, then binding arbitration — with mediation and arbitration specified in the contract. A red flag is "that's never happened with me" or a combative answer that implies litigation is the first resort. Disputes happen on construction projects. The question is how they get resolved. You want a contractor who treats dispute resolution as a professional process, not a personal affront.

> **Common Mistake:** Homeowners conduct vetting interviews and then don't actually call references. References are where the interview is confirmed or contradicted. When you call references, don't ask "were you happy with the work?" Ask: "Did the project finish within 10% of the original budget? Were there surprises in the change order process? If you were starting over today, would you hire this contractor again — and would you do anything differently in how you managed the relationship?" Those questions produce useful information. "Were you happy?" produces "Yes, mostly."

## Section 4: Reading the Bid

Once you've completed the vetting interviews and invited your top candidates to bid, you'll receive documents that look very different from each other and that are not, in their raw form, comparable. Understanding what a bid should contain — and what its absence tells you — is as important as knowing what the numbers say.

### What a Proper Bid Looks Like

The right bid is **line-itemized**, not lump-sum. A lump-sum bid that says "Complete ADU construction: $230,000" tells you almost nothing. You cannot evaluate it against other bids, you cannot identify what is and isn't included, and you have no basis for change order negotiation later because there's no line-item baseline to work from.

A properly structured ADU bid should include separate line items for: site preparation and demolition (if any); foundation (type specified — slab, crawlspace, or perimeter with floor system); rough framing including exterior sheathing; roofing; windows and exterior doors; exterior finishes (siding, trim, painting); insulation; **MEP rough-in** (the installation of mechanical, electrical, and plumbing systems before walls are closed — specified by trade with subcontractor identified); drywall and plaster; interior finishes (flooring, cabinetry, counters, interior doors and trim, painting); MEP trim-out (fixtures, outlets, switches, appliances); landscaping and site restoration; permit fees; and **soft costs** (design, engineering, inspections, and other non-construction costs). There should also be an explicit **contingency line** — typically 10–15% of construction costs — that acknowledges the inherent uncertainty in any construction project.

If a contractor's bid is missing any of these categories, that doesn't necessarily mean they aren't doing the work — it may mean they've buried it in another line or folded it into an allowance. But missing categories require explicit clarification before you can evaluate the bid.

### Allowances: The Hiding Place for Risk

Pay particular attention to **allowances** — line items where the contractor has allocated a budget but left the actual specification open. "Flooring allowance: $8,000" means the contractor has budgeted $8,000 for flooring but hasn't specified what kind. If you choose flooring that costs $14,000 installed, you'll pay a $6,000 change order. Allowances are not inherently dishonest — some specifications genuinely haven't been made at bid time — but they are a place where risk lives. Compare allowances across bids carefully: if one contractor allows $8,000 for flooring and another allows $15,000 for the same space, the lower allowance will likely produce a change order.

> **Common Mistake:** Comparing bids as if they're describing the same project when they aren't. Before you compare any numbers, go line by line and confirm that each bid is covering the same scope. The low bid often looks low because it's missing things — landscaping restoration, permit fees, engineering, or contingency. The contractor who left those out will add them back as change orders. You're not comparing three bids on the same project; you're comparing three different descriptions of three different projects that you happen to want to build.

### The Low Bid Usually Becomes the Expensive Bid

The $130,000 spread between your lowest and highest bid almost certainly reflects differences in scope, specification, and risk allocation — not differences in the contractor's skill at buying lumber cheaply. The low bidder has probably assumed more favorable site conditions than exist, used lower allowances, excluded line items the higher bidders included, or planned to price change orders at a markup that will erode the apparent savings within the first two months of construction. This is not speculation; it is the documented pattern of low-bid ADU projects across every market I have worked in.

This doesn't mean the highest bid is right, either. Overbidding exists, particularly from contractors who are busy enough that they're pricing to decline. The right answer lives in understanding exactly what each bid includes and adjusting for scope differences before you compare numbers. After scope normalization, if one bid is still 15–20% below the others from a contractor who passes the vetting interview, it warrants a direct conversation: ask them to walk you through their assumptions on the three biggest line items. Their answer will tell you whether the difference is skill and efficiency, or optimistic assumptions that will surface as change orders.

## Section 5: 12 Red Flags

Some contractor behaviors are warning signs. Others are deal-breakers. The following 12 indicators, in any combination, tell you something important about how a contractor operates — and how they'll behave when your project encounters difficulty.

**1. Cannot provide a license number for immediate verification.** A licensed contractor knows their license number. It's on their truck, their contracts, and their permits. If a contractor can't give you a number you can look up in the state database within 24 hours, stop the conversation.

**2. Asks for more than 30% upfront — or any amount inconsistent with state law.** California Business and Professions Code § 7159 [S1] caps the initial deposit for licensed contractors at $1,000 or 10% of the contract price, whichever is lower. Washington, Oregon, and Massachusetts have similar consumer protection provisions. Any contractor who asks for 40–50% upfront is either unaware of the law or relying on your ignorance of it. Neither is acceptable.

**3. Cannot produce a workers' compensation certificate.** See Section 7 on insurance verification. If there's no workers' comp and someone is injured on your site, the financial consequences can extend to you personally.

**4. Pressures you to start immediately due to a "scheduling opening."** A contractor with a sudden opening in their schedule may be available because another project fell through, another client fired them, or they've been underbooked for months. Manufactured urgency is a sales tactic. A genuine scheduling opportunity doesn't require you to decide in 48 hours.

**5. Unwilling to provide references with direct contact information.** References serve no purpose if you can't call them. A contractor who offers to "arrange" a reference conversation rather than give you a phone number to call directly is controlling the information you receive. That's not acceptable.

**6. Bid is lump-sum only.** As discussed in Section 4, a lump-sum bid is not an evaluable document. It may also be a tool: without line items, you have no baseline for dispute resolution when the contractor claims a change order is warranted.

**7. No written scope of work — just a number.** Related to lump-sum bidding, but worse. If there is no written description of what the contractor proposes to build for the price they're quoting, there is no contract. There is a number on a page, which is not a legally enforceable agreement in any state.

**8. Cannot name the permit jurisdiction contact or explain the permit process.** A contractor who has pulled ADU permits in your jurisdiction knows the process. They know the name of the permit office, the typical review timeline, the plan check requirements, and the inspection sequence. If a contractor is vague or incorrect about your local permit process, their claimed ADU experience in your jurisdiction is questionable.

**9. References don't return calls or give vague, scripted answers.** Happy clients return calls from researchers asking about contractors who served them well. When references are consistently unreachable, or when calls that do connect produce "They were great, I can't think of anything negative," that pattern is information. Ask specific questions that require specific answers: What was the final cost versus original contract price? How many change orders were there?

**10. Suggests you pull the permit yourself to "save money."** When you pull a permit, you become the contractor of record. You take legal responsibility for the construction, including compliance with building codes. You also typically void any claims under the contractor's license and insurance if something goes wrong. A licensed contractor who suggests you pull your own permit to avoid the permit process or to help them avoid scrutiny is not protecting your interests.

**11. No construction management software or systematic documentation process.** Projects managed informally lose information. Verbal decisions become disputed. Inspection records disappear. Photos never get taken. You don't need a contractor who uses the most expensive enterprise software; you need a contractor who has a system that creates a record of what happened on your project. A contractor who operates without documentation is operating in a way that will disadvantage you in any dispute.

**12. Pushes back against a fixed-price contract for a well-defined scope.** For a project with complete, permitted plans and a detailed scope of work, a fixed-price contract is appropriate and protects you from unlimited cost exposure. A contractor who resists fixed pricing on a well-defined project is either not confident in their cost estimating or is accustomed to making money on change orders rather than on the base contract. Either interpretation is concerning.

## Section 6: Contract Essentials

The contract is the instrument through which everything in this chapter becomes enforceable. A contractor who passed the vetting interview with strong answers and whose bid survived scope comparison can still cause significant damage if the contract doesn't properly define the project and your rights as an owner.

### Fixed-Price vs. Cost-Plus

A **fixed-price contract** (also called a stipulated sum contract) specifies a total price for a defined scope of work. You pay that price unless the scope changes through a formal change order process. This is the appropriate contract structure when your plans and specifications are complete and permitted, your scope is well-defined, and you want cost certainty. It is the right structure for most ADU projects.

A **cost-plus contract** (also called time-and-materials) has the contractor charge you for their actual costs plus a markup (typically 15–25%). This is appropriate when the scope genuinely cannot be defined upfront — for example, a renovation of a structure with unknown conditions, or a project that begins before design is complete. Cost-plus contracts protect the contractor from cost uncertainty; they do not protect you. Under cost-plus, you absorb all cost risk. Unless your project genuinely involves significant unknowns that preclude fixed pricing, push for a fixed-price contract.

> **State Watch:** California Business and Professions Code § 7159 [S1] specifies mandatory contract requirements for home improvement contracts over $500. These requirements include: a written contract signed by both parties before work begins, a description of the work to be performed, a description of the materials to be used, a schedule of payments, and a notice of your right to cancel within three business days. Violations can void the contract entirely and expose the contractor to license discipline. Oregon, Washington, and Massachusetts have parallel consumer protection requirements. Know your state's specific requirements before you sign anything.

### What Every ADU Contract Must Include

A complete ADU contract should contain all of the following elements, without exception.

**Scope of work** — a written description of what will be built, specific enough to be the basis for a dispute. "Construct a detached ADU per the approved plans dated [date], plan set sheets A1.0 through S3.0" is a scope reference. "Build the ADU we discussed" is not.

**Plans and specifications by reference** — the contract should incorporate the permitted plan set by reference, so that the approved drawings become part of the contract. Any deviation from those drawings requires a change order.

**Schedule with milestones** — specific dates or durations for each major construction phase: mobilization, foundation, rough framing, MEP rough-in, insulation and drywall, finishes, substantial completion, final inspection, and punch list completion.

**Payment schedule tied to milestones** — the draw schedule should release money only upon verified completion of defined milestones, not based on the calendar. Never pay in advance of completed work.

**Change order procedure** — the contract must specify that no change in scope, materials, or schedule is authorized unless documented in a written change order signed by both parties before the work begins. Verbal change orders are unenforceable in virtually every jurisdiction.

**Dispute resolution clause** — specify the sequence: direct negotiation first, then mediation with a mutually agreed mediator, then binding arbitration under specified rules (American Arbitration Association is common). This keeps disputes out of court, which is generally faster and less expensive for both parties.

**Insurance requirements** — specify minimum coverage types and amounts, and require that you be named as an additional insured on the contractor's general liability policy.

**Warranty terms** — specify the warranty period for workmanship (typically one year from substantial completion) and note that manufacturer warranties on materials and systems run separately.

**Lien waiver provisions** — require the contractor to provide a conditional lien waiver upon each draw payment, and an unconditional lien waiver upon final payment. Lien waivers protect you from mechanics' liens filed by subcontractors or suppliers who weren't paid by your GC — a legitimate risk on any construction project.

### The Draw Schedule in Practice

The **draw schedule** is the payment architecture of your project. It is also where contractors attempt to front-load their cash position at your expense. A well-structured ADU draw schedule typically looks something like this:

Upon contract execution and mobilization: 10–15% of the contract price. This covers initial material procurement and site setup. Foundation complete and inspected: 15–20%. Rough framing complete and inspected: 15–20%. MEP rough-in complete and inspected: 15%. Insulation and drywall complete: 10%. Interior finishes substantially complete: 10%. Final inspection passed and substantial completion achieved: 5–10%. Punch list completed and all lien waivers received: the remaining balance — typically 5–10%.

Never pay ahead of the milestone. If a contractor asks for the rough framing draw before the framing inspection has passed, decline. The inspection is the verification that the work is complete. Paying before inspection removes your leverage to ensure the work is done right.

Hold the final payment until everything on the punch list is resolved and all mechanics' lien waivers are received. This is not adversarial — it is standard professional practice. A contractor who treats final payment retention as hostile is a contractor who doesn't understand how construction finance works, or who is hoping you don't.

### Change Order Discipline

Change orders are the financial battleground of residential construction. They are legitimate when scope genuinely changes; they are exploited when scope language was deliberately vague at contract time. Your discipline on change orders should be absolute: nothing happens without a written, signed change order.

When a change order is proposed, you have the right to understand the basis for the price (labor hours, material cost, markup), the schedule impact, and whether the condition that triggered the change order was something a competent contractor should have anticipated from the contract documents. If a contractor missed something obvious in the plans and is asking for a change order to cover it, that is a contractor problem, not an owner problem.

You also have the right to get a second opinion on the price. On any change order over $2,000–$3,000, it is reasonable to ask for three days to verify the pricing before signing. A contractor who demands immediate approval of change orders is exploiting the time pressure of an ongoing construction schedule. Don't let urgency pressure you into approving unreviewed costs.

## Section 7: Insurance Verification

Insurance verification is one of the most consistently mishandled aspects of contractor selection. Homeowners typically look at a certificate of insurance and assume the coverage is real and current. That assumption is wrong often enough to warrant a different approach.

### Builder's Risk Insurance

A **builder's risk policy** (also called course-of-construction insurance) covers the structure under construction against fire, theft, vandalism, and weather damage. It is not the same as your homeowner's policy. Your homeowner's policy typically excludes structures under construction, or provides significantly reduced coverage for them. This creates a gap: if your ADU under construction burns down at the foundation stage, you may find that neither your homeowner's insurer nor the contractor's general liability policy covers the loss.

Who carries the builder's risk policy should be specified in your contract. Either the contractor carries it and names you as an additional insured, or you obtain a separate policy. If your contractor carries it, confirm the policy amount covers the full replacement value of the structure at completion, not just the current construction value. Get the policy number and insurer name and call to verify it's current.

### Your Homeowner's Policy Gap

Notify your homeowner's insurance carrier before construction begins. Describe the project and ask explicitly whether your policy covers: the existing structure during construction, any materials stored on site before installation, and liability for injuries on the construction site during active work hours. Most carriers will either endorse your existing policy for the construction period or suggest a separate policy. Do not assume your existing policy covers all of this — the assumption costs nothing to verify and can cost you enormously if it's wrong.

### Additional Insured Status

Require that your contractor add you as an **additional insured** on their general liability policy for the duration of the project. This means that if a third party is injured on your property during construction and sues, both you and the contractor are covered under the same policy. Without additional insured status, you are relying on the contractor's policy to respond on your behalf without any contractual obligation to do so. Additional insured endorsements are standard in commercial construction and should be routine for any contractor doing residential work at this scale.

### Verifying Workers' Compensation

A workers' compensation certificate is a piece of paper. It proves that a policy existed at some point. It does not prove the policy is current, that premiums are being paid, or that all workers on your site are covered under it.

To actually verify workers' comp coverage, call the insurer listed on the certificate before construction begins. Give them the policy number and ask: Is this policy currently active? Does it cover employees working at residential construction sites? What is the coverage expiration date? This call takes five minutes and confirms something the certificate alone cannot.

Workers' comp for subcontractors requires separate verification. Your GC's workers' comp policy covers GC employees; it does not automatically cover subcontractors who are themselves employers. Require that every subcontractor provide their own COI for workers' comp. Your GC should already be requiring this — if they're not, that tells you something about how they manage their project.

> **State Watch:** In California, hiring a contractor without workers' compensation insurance is a misdemeanor for the contractor and a potentially catastrophic liability for the homeowner. If an uninsured worker is injured on your site, you can be held liable for medical costs, lost wages, and more. The CSLB's license lookup [S2] shows whether a contractor has workers' comp on file. Washington L&I [S3] and Oregon CCB [S4] provide similar information. Check these databases after you receive a COI, not instead of one.

## Closing: From Contractor to Permit

Selecting the right contractor is the first major execution decision of your project — but it is not the last. Once you have a signed contract with a contractor who has passed the vetting process, the project moves immediately into the permit phase. The permit process is not something that happens to your project while you wait; it is something your contractor and you navigate together, and the quality of your contractor's working relationship with your local building department will directly affect your timeline.

The right contractor for an ADU project already knows the name of the plan checker at your local building department, already understands what your jurisdiction requires for ADU plan sets, and has already navigated the pre-application process in your city. When you ask your contractor about the permit timeline in your jurisdiction, they should answer specifically. If they answer vaguely, they haven't built enough projects in your market to have that institutional knowledge — and that gap will show up in your schedule.

Chapter 8 walks through the permit process in detail, including what your plan set must include, how to communicate with building departments to accelerate review, and what to do when your permit application is rejected. Much of what makes Chapter 8 actionable depends on having a contractor who already knows the path. The right contractor selection decision, made well here, pays dividends through every phase that follows.
