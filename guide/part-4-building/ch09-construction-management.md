# Chapter 9 — Construction Management for Homeowners

---

It's a Tuesday afternoon, six weeks into construction. You haven't been out to the site since last Thursday. You text your general contractor: *How's it going?* He replies: *Good, making progress.* You feel fine about that for about 48 hours, and then a nagging thought surfaces — you're not sure exactly what "progress" means, which inspection you're waiting on, how much of the draw you've released, or whether the framing crew actually fixed the header issue the inspector flagged. You realize, with a slow and uncomfortable certainty, that you've been waiting for your contractor to tell you what's happening with your own $200,000 project.

That is the wrong posture.

Professional construction managers don't wait to be updated. They run toward the information. They know the schedule before they arrive on site, so they can see immediately when something's behind. They know the draw balance, the open change orders, the pending inspection requests — not because the contractor tells them, but because they built a system to track it themselves. This chapter is about building that system, even if you've never set foot on a job site before.

The good news is that homeowner-level construction management doesn't require a professional license or a decade of field experience. It requires discipline, documentation, and a willingness to ask direct questions — including the uncomfortable ones. The homeowners who get through construction without major surprises are rarely the ones who hired the best contractor. They're the ones who stayed engaged from the first day of mobilization to the final punch list walk.

## Section 1: The Construction Timeline by Phase

Before you can manage a schedule, you need to understand what a realistic one looks like. The single most common mistake homeowners make at the start of construction is accepting an optimistic timeline without pressure-testing it. A contractor who tells you a detached ADU will be done in 12 weeks is either working on a very small unit, has an unusually favorable jurisdiction for inspection turnaround, or is telling you what you want to hear. Build your expectations around what the phases actually require.

The following timeline applies to a standard detached ADU of 400–800 square feet with a conventional stick-frame build. Prefab and modular ADUs have different schedules (site work is similar, but the unit itself arrives largely finished). Attached ADUs and garage conversions generally compress the foundation and framing phases but may add complexity in the integration with the existing structure.

**Mobilization and site preparation (1–2 weeks).** This phase covers contractor setup: temporary power hookup, portable sanitation, equipment staging, any required tree removal or demolition, and grading. It looks like nothing is happening because very little visible progress occurs. Confirm the contractor has pulled all required permits before any earth is disturbed — work that begins without a permit creates problems that are expensive and sometimes impossible to fully correct.

**Foundation (2–4 weeks).** Timeline varies significantly by foundation type. A simple concrete slab-on-grade is the fastest: forming, pouring, and curing to structural strength typically takes two to three weeks. A raised perimeter foundation with a crawl space — common in areas with expansive soils, high water tables, or seismic requirements — adds time for forming the stem walls and waiting on inspections. A full basement, where permitted, can add four to six weeks beyond that. The foundation inspection is one of the most critical hold points on the project. The city inspector must sign off before concrete is poured, which means you're subject to inspection scheduling windows that are entirely outside your contractor's control.

**Rough framing (2–4 weeks).** This is the phase most homeowners recognize as construction: walls going up, the structure taking shape. For a 600 square foot detached ADU with a straightforward floor plan, an experienced framing crew can rough the walls in two to three weeks. Complex rooflines, cathedral ceilings, and structural complications add time. Do not let the visual progress of framing lull you into complacency — this is also where dimensional errors, wrong header sizes, and structural connection failures get buried inside the walls if you're not watching.

**Roof framing and sheathing (1–2 weeks).** Roof framing follows immediately after wall framing and typically runs concurrent with the rough framing inspection. This phase closes the structure to weather, which is a critical milestone: once the roof is dried in, interior work can begin regardless of weather. If your project is in a wet climate — the Pacific Northwest, New England, the upper Midwest — getting dried in before the rainy season is a scheduling priority worth discussing with your contractor explicitly.

**MEP rough-in (3–5 weeks).** **MEP** stands for mechanical, electrical, and plumbing — the three principal trade categories that will run their rough work simultaneously once the structure is framed. This is the most schedule-intensive phase because three separate subcontractors are coordinating in the same space, each with their own inspection requirements. Electrical rough-in involves running wiring, setting the panel location, and boxing out for fixtures. Plumbing rough-in sets drain lines, vent stacks, and supply runs — all of which must be in place before walls close. HVAC rough-in installs ductwork or mini-split line sets depending on your mechanical system.

The MEP rough-in phase is where schedule compression most often fails. Each trade has its own inspection, and inspectors do not always move fast. In some jurisdictions — Los Angeles, San Francisco, Seattle — the wait for a MEP inspection can run one to two weeks even when the work is ready. A GC who tells you MEP rough-in will be done in two weeks may be right about the installation time but wrong about the calendar time once inspection delays are factored in.

**Insulation (1 week).** Insulation cannot be installed until MEP rough-in inspections are passed. This is a one-week phase in most ADU builds. It sounds trivial, but the pre-insulation checkpoint is one of your most important quality control moments: it's your last chance to see the rough work before it's buried. See Section 5 for what to look for.

**Drywall — hang, tape, and finish (2–3 weeks).** Hanging drywall (screwing the panels to studs) is fast, typically two to three days for an ADU. Taping seams, applying compound coats, and sanding to a paintable surface takes two to three weeks because each compound coat must dry fully before the next can be applied. Do not let the contractor rush this phase — thin compound coats applied too quickly crack, and the failures often don't show up until the house has been through a heating season or two. Texture and primer application follow finish sanding and can add two to three days.

**Interior finishes (3–5 weeks).** This phase covers flooring installation, cabinet and countertop installation, interior doors, and trim carpentry. It is the most finish-quality-sensitive phase of the project, and it involves the most subcontractors in sequence: flooring typically goes before cabinets, cabinets before countertops, countertops before plumbing trim-out. Sequencing errors here — installing flooring in the wrong order, for example — create rework that is both expensive and slow to fix. The total timeline for interior finishes on an ADU is three to five weeks if trades are properly sequenced and materials are on-site. Supply chain delays on cabinet lead times (which can run four to twelve weeks from order) are one of the most common causes of schedule slippage in this phase — your contractor should have ordered cabinets at permit approval, not at rough framing completion.

**MEP trim-out (1–2 weeks).** Trim-out is when the electrical, plumbing, and HVAC subcontractors return to install the finish components: fixtures, outlets, switches, faucets, toilets, thermostats, and equipment connections. This phase runs concurrent with the tail end of interior finishes. It is also the phase where substitutions and omissions from the original specification most often surface — the electrician installed a different fixture than spec'd, the plumber swapped a fixture to something cheaper. Catch substitutions at trim-out, not after final inspection.

**Exterior finishes (2–3 weeks).** Exterior work — siding, exterior paint, any decking or covered entry, and landscaping restoration — often runs concurrently with interior finish work. Landscaping restoration (restoring disturbed soil, replacing vegetation damaged during construction) is frequently underscoped in contracts and becomes a dispute item at project closeout. Make sure your original contract specifies the extent of landscaping restoration explicitly, including seed or sod on disturbed areas, replacement of any plants damaged by equipment staging, and final grading to drain away from the structure.

**Final inspection and punch list (1–3 weeks).** The final inspection is the last official hold point. Before calling for final inspection, you and the contractor should walk the project together and generate the **punch list** — the itemized list of incomplete or deficient items that must be corrected before the building department will issue a **certificate of occupancy (CO)**. The CO is the legal document that certifies the ADU meets code and is habitable. Do not accept occupancy of the unit or release the final payment draw until the CO is in hand.

The total realistic timeline for a standard detached ADU is **20–35 weeks from mobilization to CO**, depending on complexity, jurisdiction inspection wait times, contractor capacity, and weather. A 600 square foot slab-on-grade ADU in a jurisdiction with fast inspections and an organized contractor can hit the lower end of that range. A complex 800 square foot crawl-space foundation build in a jurisdiction with two-week inspection waits will push toward or past the upper end.

> **Pro Tip:** When you're reviewing a contractor's proposed schedule, ask them to account for inspection wait times explicitly. Ask: "What's the typical inspection turnaround in this jurisdiction, and how have you built that into this schedule?" A contractor who has done multiple ADUs in your city will know. One who gives you a vague answer hasn't thought it through.

## Section 2: The Homeowner PM Dashboard

Every construction project generates a flood of information: inspection requests, subcontractor schedules, material deliveries, verbal decisions, text messages, emails, photos, invoices. Without a system to organize it, critical items fall through the cracks. You miss a failed inspection note. You can't remember whether you approved that window change verbally or in writing. You realize at closeout that the contractor made three scope changes you don't remember agreeing to.

The homeowner PM dashboard is a simple, low-technology system designed to give you command of your project's status at any given moment. You don't need Procore or Buildertrend (though Buildertrend does offer a homeowner-accessible tier that some organized GCs use with their clients [S5]). A well-maintained Google Sheet and a running Google Doc will accomplish the same thing for a single project.

The dashboard has six components you update at least weekly:

**Scheduled versus actual progress by phase.** List every phase from Section 1 above with the planned start date, actual start date, planned completion date, and current status. Seeing the planned versus actual comparison forces the question: if we started foundation a week late, and MEP rough-in has a two-week inspection lag, are we still hitting the original substantial completion date? Usually not. The earlier you see schedule slippage, the more options you have to address it.

**Inspection log.** For every required inspection, track: the inspection type, the date requested, the scheduled date, the outcome (pass/fail/correction notice), and the date any corrections were approved. Inspection failures are not disasters — they happen on well-managed projects — but they do add time, and they need to be tracked. A pattern of repeated failures on the same contractor's work is information you need.

**Draw schedule: requested, released, and remaining.** List every draw milestone, the amount associated with it, the date requested by the contractor, the date you verified completion, the date you released payment, and the running remaining balance. Your draw schedule should show at any moment how much money has left your account, how much work has been verified complete, and how much you're holding for completion. If draw releases are running ahead of verified work, that's a warning sign — see Section 3.

**Open requests for information (RFIs) and their status.** An **RFI** is a formal question from the contractor to you (or to the design team) asking for clarification on a design or specification item. "The plans show a window centered on the north wall, but there's a load path conflict — where do you want it?" Every open RFI represents a decision that hasn't been made, and unmade decisions stall work. Track every RFI with the date opened, the question, your response, and the date closed.

**Open change orders and their status.** Every proposed change order gets a line in the dashboard: date submitted, description, proposed cost, your response (approved/negotiating/rejected), and if approved, the date signed and the cumulative budget impact. Do not let change orders accumulate unsigned. An unsigned change order that the contractor executes anyway is a dispute waiting to happen.

**Weather delays logged.** Keep a log of any days the contractor formally flags as weather delays. Review it. A contractor who logs every rainy day as a weather delay when the work that day was interior work that weather doesn't affect is padding their schedule for a time extension claim later. You don't have to accept every logged delay as legitimate.

Alongside the dashboard, maintain a **project log** — a running document, not a spreadsheet. Every site visit, phone call, meeting, decision, and observation gets a dated entry. Include the time. Include who was present. Include what was said and what was decided. This is your legal protection if the project goes sideways. Courts and contractor license boards look very favorably on homeowners who can produce a contemporaneous log showing exactly what happened and when.

Your minimum site visit cadence is once per week. Bring the project log. Walk every area of active work. Take photos. Note anything that doesn't match the plans or the spec. A 20-minute weekly site visit is not micromanagement — it is the minimum responsible engagement with a project of this scale.

## Section 3: Draw Schedule Management

A **draw schedule** is the payment plan tied to verified construction milestones. Instead of paying your contractor a lump sum at the start or in equal monthly installments, you release funds incrementally as defined portions of the work are completed and verified. This is the primary financial protection mechanism available to homeowners in a construction contract.

How draws are structured depends on how you're financing the project. If you have a **construction loan**, your lender manages the draw process formally: the contractor requests a draw, the lender sends a third-party inspector to verify the percentage of completion, and the lender releases funds directly to the contractor (or to a controlled disbursement account) only after the inspection confirms the claimed work is complete. This adds a layer of protection — a neutral third party verifying completion before funds move — but it also adds two to five business days to each draw release, which can create tension with contractors who have payroll obligations. Factor this into your project scheduling discussions at the contract stage.

If you're financing with cash, a HELOC, or a home equity loan, there is no lender-mandated draw inspection. You are your own draw inspector. This is a significant responsibility, and most homeowners underestimate it. Your draw schedule should be spelled out in the original contract with specific milestones — not percentage completions, which are subjective, but objective, observable events: foundation poured and inspected, framing inspection passed, drywall hung, certificate of occupancy issued. Tie each draw to something you can physically verify with your own eyes.

> **Common Mistake:** Accepting a draw schedule that has large front-loaded payments. A contractor asking for 30–40% of the total contract value before significant work is complete is asking you to finance their business operations and materials, and substantially weakens your leverage if problems arise later. A reasonable front-loaded draw covers mobilization costs and verified material deposits — typically 10–15% of contract value at most.

The draw verification process, when you're functioning as your own draw inspector, works like this. The contractor submits a draw request with the amount and the milestone it corresponds to. Before releasing any funds, you walk the site and verify with your own eyes that the milestone is complete. If you're not confident in your ability to evaluate a specific milestone — pre-slab rebar placement, for example, or the completeness of MEP rough-in — this is a legitimate moment to hire a third-party construction consultant for a single inspection. A $300–$500 independent inspection that confirms the work before you release $25,000 is money very well spent.

When a contractor requests a draw ahead of the documented milestone, the professional response is direct and non-apologetic: "I can release this draw once I've verified [milestone]. Can we schedule a walk-through for [date]?" That's it. You don't need to explain further, and you don't need to apologize. The draw schedule was agreed to in the contract. The contractor knew the terms when they signed.

There are two legitimate reasons to advance a draw ahead of a completion milestone. The first is a verified material deposit: the contractor has placed a significant material order — structural steel, windows, a custom cabinet package — and the supplier requires a deposit that the contractor must fund before the draw milestone is reached. In this case, ask for the supplier invoice showing the deposit requirement, and release only the verified deposit amount. The second is legitimate schedule sequencing: in some cases, a contractor genuinely needs funds to pay subcontractors whose work is substantially complete within a multi-trade phase, even if the overall phase isn't yet at the contractual draw milestone. This is a judgment call — evaluate it on the merits, with eyes on the site and documentation in hand.

Never release a draw based on a contractor's verbal representation of completion. Never release a draw because the contractor says they need the money to make payroll. These are cash-flow problems that belong to the contractor's business, not your project.

> **State Watch:** Some states have statutory provisions governing payment schedules and draw timing in residential construction contracts. California Business and Professions Code § 7159 specifies requirements for home improvement contracts, including limitations on advance payments. [VERIFY current thresholds with your state's contractor licensing board before signing.] Washington and Oregon have similar consumer protection provisions in their contractor licensing statutes. Confirm with your contract attorney before finalizing payment terms.

## Section 4: Change Order Evaluation and the Change Order Risk Framework

No section of this chapter matters more than this one. Change orders — written modifications to the original contract scope, schedule, or price — are the single largest source of budget overruns and contractor disputes in residential construction. They are also normal and expected on any project that involves opening walls, digging foundations, and integrating systems into an existing property. The goal is not to eliminate change orders. The goal is to evaluate them precisely, approve the legitimate ones at fair prices, and reject or negotiate the ones that are padded, premature, or improperly scoped.

Before discussing how to evaluate a change order, it's worth defining what a **legitimate change order** looks like, because a well-structured one gives you everything you need to make an informed decision.

A legitimate change order is written before any additional work begins. It describes the specific scope change or the unforeseen condition that triggered it. It is itemized: labor hours, labor rate, materials with quantities and unit costs, overhead, and margin are all broken out separately. The total is traceable back to those line items. It is signed by both parties before any execution of the changed work. And it references the original contract to establish clearly that the added scope was not already included.

When a contractor hands you a change order, you have exactly three possible responses.

**Approve** it when the scope is legitimate and the pricing is reasonable. If a framing crew opens a wall and finds existing electrical running where the plans showed clear space for a structural post, that is a genuine unforeseen condition that requires a change. If the labor and materials pricing is in line with current market rates and there's no obvious padding, approve it promptly — holding a legitimate change order for days or weeks while the crew waits is unfair and damages your working relationship with a contractor who is operating honestly.

**Negotiate** it when the scope is legitimate but the pricing appears padded. This is more common than contractors would like to admit. Padding shows up as inflated labor hours ("8 hours to move an outlet"), excessive markup on materials that can be easily priced (a trip to Home Depot's website takes two minutes), or margin rates above the typical 15–20% range on a straightforward change. The script here is simple: "The scope makes sense to me — can you walk me through the labor and materials breakdown in more detail?" Then compare what you're seeing against a second opinion quote for the same scope, or against the RS Means unit cost data your contract attorney or construction consultant can pull. You're not accusing the contractor of dishonesty — you're doing your job as an owner.

**Reject** it when the scope was already included in the original contract, when the condition was clearly foreseeable with a reasonable site investigation before construction started, or when work has already been done and the contractor is presenting the change order after the fact requesting retroactive approval. Scope already in the contract is a contractor's responsibility to execute at the contract price. A foreseeable condition — termite damage in a structure with visible signs of infestation, for example, or undersized electrical service in a home where the panel is clearly original equipment from the 1960s — is something a competent contractor should have identified during scope development and priced into the original bid. A retroactive change order is almost never acceptable: you lost the chance to approve, negotiate, or get competing bids the moment work began without authorization.

### The 5% Threshold Rule

If a single change order exceeds 5% of the original contract value, pause before approving it. A $250,000 contract means any single change order over $12,500 warrants a second look before you sign. This is not automatically a red flag — genuine discoveries can produce large changes, and a remediation of unexpected soil conditions or structural damage legitimately costs what it costs. But a change order of that magnitude warrants independent verification of both the condition and the pricing before you sign. Get a third-party construction consultant to review it. Ask for the contractor to hold the work while you get a comparison quote from a specialty subcontractor. Take the time this decision deserves.

### The Cumulative Change Order Warning Threshold

If the total of all approved change orders reaches 15% of the original contract value, something went wrong in the original scope definition. At that point, the question to ask is not "should I approve this next change order?" but "what happened during estimating and design development that left so much scope undefined?" A well-scoped ADU project with thorough plans should see total change orders in the 5–10% range from genuine unforeseen conditions. Change orders above 15% cumulative often indicate either an incomplete bid (the contractor deliberately underpriced to win the job), inadequate design documents (plans that didn't resolve key coordination issues before construction started), or scope creep driven by homeowner decisions during construction. All three causes have different solutions, but all three require you to stop, inventory the situation, and make a deliberate decision about how to proceed before the total escalation gets out of hand.

### The Script for "We Found Something Unexpected"

A contractor calls you and says: "We opened the wall and found dry rot in the rim joist. That's going to cost an extra $8,000."

Your response is not "okay" and it's not an argument. It is: "Let me see what you found before we discuss cost. Can you walk me through it today?"

When you're on site, you look at what they found — with your own eyes, not through a photo on a phone. Then: "What's the itemized breakdown — labor, materials, and time?" And then the harder question: "Is this something a reasonable site inspection would have caught before we started?" That last question matters because if the answer is yes, the cost may belong to the contractor, not to you.

After you've reviewed the condition, the scope of repair, and the pricing, you apply the framework above: approve, negotiate, or reject. Document your decision in the project log immediately.

### Change Order Documentation Protocol

Every approved change order goes into the project log with the following information: date submitted, change order number, description of the scope change, the triggering condition (unforeseen discovery, owner-directed change, or design clarification), the original line item or scope that this changes, the proposed price, the approved price after any negotiation, the cumulative budget impact to date, your signature, and the contractor's signature. Keep a separate running total of all approved change orders as a percentage of the original contract.

A change order that is not signed by both parties before work begins is not a change order — it is a verbal agreement, and verbal agreements in construction are a path to litigation.

### Red Flags in Change Orders

Several patterns in change orders signal that something is wrong and warrant a harder look before you sign anything.

The first red flag is a change order with no itemization — just a new number. "Additional work to date: $6,400." That tells you nothing. A contractor who won't itemize a change order is asking you to trust them blindly on pricing. Require itemization before any discussion of approval proceeds.

The second red flag is a change order that covers scope already described in the original contract. Pull the contract and plans before you respond. If the scope is there in writing, the answer is no.

The third — and most serious — red flag is work that was already completed before the change order was presented. Once work is done, your ability to negotiate pricing or reject the change disappears. A contractor who does unauthorized work and then hands you a change order is extracting money from you by fait accompli. The professional response is to note clearly, in writing, that you are evaluating work that was completed without prior authorization, and that approval of the pricing is not implied by the completion of the work.

The fourth red flag is a verbal approval request: "Just text me yes and we'll get started." No. Every change order is in writing, signed, before work begins. This is not negotiable.

## Section 5: 8 Critical Inspection Quality Checkpoints

The building department's inspector is not your quality control agent. The inspector's job is to verify code compliance — that structural members are the right size, that electrical wiring meets minimum safety standards, that plumbing is properly vented. The inspector is not checking that the contractor executed the plans correctly, that the window was centered as designed, or that the cabinet layout matches your specification. That's your job.

These eight checkpoints are the moments in the construction sequence where you — or a hired construction consultant if you want professional eyes — should be on site with the plans in hand, looking at the work before it's covered up.

**1. Pre-slab or pre-foundation inspection.** Before concrete is poured, verify that rebar placement matches the structural engineer's plan: size, spacing, and coverage depth. Verify that any utility rough-ins (electrical conduit sleeves, plumbing sleeves through the slab, anchor bolt placement) are correctly positioned relative to the floor plan. A sleeve in the wrong location means chipping out cured concrete — a slow, expensive repair. This is also the moment to verify that the sub-grade surface is prepared correctly: compacted aggregate base, moisture barrier, and edge insulation where required by energy code.

**2. Post-framing inspection (rough framing sign-off).** Walk the framed structure with the architectural and structural plans. Verify that exterior wall dimensions match the plans — measure them. Verify header sizes over window and door openings against the structural engineer's schedule. Look at every structural connection: anchor bolts, hold-downs, hurricane ties, ridge beam connections. These are the connections that determine whether the structure performs in a seismic or wind event. Verify that rough openings for windows and doors are correctly sized for the specified units — wrong rough openings are caught now, not after the windows arrive.

**3. MEP rough-in checkpoint.** With all three trades roughed in and before walls close, verify the following. On electrical: the panel size and location, the circuit layout relative to the electrical plan, and that rough wiring runs are properly supported and protected where they pass through framing members. On plumbing: that drain lines have correct slope (typically 1/4 inch per foot for horizontal runs), that the vent stack is properly connected, and that supply runs are in place. On HVAC: that ductwork or mini-split line sets are in place and that the air handler location and condensate drain routing are as designed. Ask the HVAC subcontractor directly where the condensate drains — a condensate line that drains incorrectly creates moisture damage inside the wall cavity.

**4. Pre-insulation checkpoint.** This is your last clear view of rough-in work before insulation goes in. Verify that all penetrations through the top and bottom plates — wiring, plumbing, HVAC — are properly sealed with fire caulk or rated fire-stopping material. Verify that **blocking** (short pieces of framing lumber installed between studs to provide a solid backing for fixtures) is in place where needed: towel bars, toilet paper holders, shower valve handles, and anywhere else a fixture will be wall-mounted. Blocking missed here means future fixtures mounted into drywall only — a chronic durability problem.

**5. Pre-drywall checkpoint.** Once blocking is confirmed, do a final walk focused on anything that will be harder to fix after drywall is installed. If the ADU is intended for aging-in-place use — a parent moving in, or designed with future accessibility in mind — verify that blocking for future grab bars is installed at all bathroom walls: toilet, shower/tub perimeter, and transfer surfaces. Grab bar blocking costs about $50 in materials and one hour of framing labor. Installing a grab bar without backing in a finished bathroom later requires surgery into the drywall. Also verify exhaust fan blocking and rough-in locations relative to fixture centerlines.

**6. Weatherproofing inspection.** Before exterior cladding is applied, verify that all water-resistive barrier material (house wrap or felt) is properly lapped, taped at seams, and correctly integrated at window and door openings. Window and door flashing is one of the most common sources of water infiltration in new construction [S1]. Verify that the sill flashing at every window opening slopes to drain away from the building and is integrated with the barrier beneath. Verify roof-to-wall transitions, any penetrations through the wall (hose bibs, electrical conduit, HVAC penetrations), and the flashing at the roof edge if a porch or covered entry is involved. Water that gets behind cladding will find its way into the structure, and the damage is typically invisible for years.

**7. Pre-final walkthrough with the contractor.** Before calling for final inspection, schedule a dedicated walkthrough with your GC to generate the punch list. Walk every room. Test every outlet. Operate every door and window. Run every faucet. Flush every toilet. Turn on the HVAC and verify it functions. Look at every paint surface for missed coverage, roller spatters, and trim cut-in quality. Look at every tile installation for grout consistency and lippage. Look at every cabinet door and drawer for alignment and smooth operation. The punch list you generate on this walkthrough should be written, numbered, and acknowledged by the contractor in writing. Items on the punch list must be corrected before the final draw is released.

**8. Final building department inspection.** Make every effort to be present when the building department conducts its final inspection. You are permitted to observe — you are not permitted to intervene in the inspection itself. If the inspector identifies correction items, get them in writing before the inspector leaves the site. Every correction item becomes a dated item in your project log. Correction items from the final inspection must be reinspected before the CO is issued — the clock starts over, and you'll need to factor additional inspection scheduling time into your closeout timeline.

> **Pro Tip:** The building department's final inspection checklist is usually available as a public document on the city's building and safety website. Pull it before your pre-final walkthrough with the contractor and use it as a checklist framework. You're not trying to do the inspector's job — you're trying to catch the things the inspector would catch before the official visit, so corrections happen on your schedule rather than the inspector's.

## Section 6: Communication Protocols

Construction projects fail on communication failures more often than on technical failures. The good news is that communication systems are the part of construction management most fully within your control as a homeowner. You cannot make your inspector show up faster. You can control how consistently you document every conversation and decision.

The minimum communication cadence is a weekly site meeting. Twenty minutes walking the active work areas with your GC is enough to catch problems while they're still cheap to fix. Bring your project log. Walk with a checklist: what was supposed to happen this week, what did happen, what's scheduled for next week, what decisions or information is the contractor waiting on from you. A weekly meeting that becomes routine feels like a small time commitment. Skipping two or three of them in a row creates information gaps that become problems.

Photo documentation is non-negotiable. At every site visit, take photos of every area of active work. The discipline to take photos and file them systematically feels tedious until the moment you need them — which might be in a dispute about what the rough-in looked like before the walls closed, or in an insurance claim, or in a contractor board complaint. The filing convention: organize by phase, then by date, then by descriptive label. A folder structure of `/phase-name/YYYY-MM-DD/description` takes five minutes to set up at the start of the project and makes every photo locatable by context when you need it.

The **decision log** is a subset of your project log that captures every decision that affects scope, cost, or schedule: who made the decision, what was decided, what alternatives were considered, and the date. Homeowner-directed decisions during construction — upgrading to a different tile, adding an exterior outlet, changing a window to a door — are the owner's responsibility to document. Contractor-initiated decisions — substituting a different brand of insulation, changing a duct routing — must be pre-approved by you and logged. A decision that nobody remembers making is a dispute waiting to happen.

The communication hierarchy is important to establish at the start of the project. **Text messages** are for scheduling, quick status checks, and logistics: "Can we meet Thursday at 10?" **Email** is for decisions, scope discussions, and anything you want documented in a durable, searchable format: "Confirming our conversation today — we agreed to substitute the [specific window brand] for the [specified window brand], at no change in price, per your representation that the units are equivalent in performance specification." Every verbal decision of consequence gets confirmed by email within 24 hours. **In-person or video calls** are for disputes, complex technical discussions, and relationship management — these conversations need tone and nuance that text strips out.

Do not allow the contractor to make the email-versus-text distinction for you. A contractor who prefers to manage everything by text, and who resists email, is often avoiding documentation. That resistance is itself information.

When you've had the same conversation with a contractor three times without resolution — the same missing item on the punch list not addressed, the same question about draw documentation not answered, the same schedule slippage not explained — it is time to bring in professional help. A construction consultant can be hired for a project assessment for $500–$1,500 and will produce a written report that resets the dynamic entirely. If the issue involves money or contract interpretation, a construction attorney's demand letter can be the fastest path to resolution for $300–$800. Do not wait until you're past the point of resolution to make this call.

## Section 7: Dispute Resolution and Contractor Replacement

Even on well-managed projects, disputes happen. How you respond to a dispute — the speed, the documentation, the escalation path — determines whether you resolve it in weeks or in months.

The dispute escalation ladder has four rungs, and you should exhaust each one before moving to the next.

The first rung is direct conversation. A clear, documented conversation with the contractor about the specific issue, what resolution you're requesting, and the timeline for resolution. This conversation happens in person or on a video call, not by text. After the conversation, you send an email summarizing what was discussed and what was agreed. Most disputes resolve here if approached directly and professionally.

The second rung is a written demand. If the direct conversation does not produce resolution within a reasonable time — typically five to seven business days — a formal written demand letter from you to the contractor, sent by email and certified mail, documenting the issue, the contract provisions at stake, your requested resolution, and the deadline for response. Keep the tone professional and the language precise. Angry demands that overstate the case give the contractor grounds to dismiss them.

The third rung is mediation. Many construction contracts include a mediation requirement before arbitration or litigation. Mediation is a facilitated negotiation with a neutral third party — typically a retired judge or an experienced construction attorney — who helps both sides find a resolution. It is significantly faster and cheaper than litigation. If your contract requires it, follow the process. If your contract doesn't mention it, you can propose it voluntarily — many contractors will agree to mediation rather than face a contractor board complaint or litigation.

The fourth rung is **arbitration** (if required by contract) or litigation and contractor board complaints. These are not interchangeable. Arbitration is a private, binding process defined by the contract — review your contract carefully to understand what you agreed to. Litigation in small claims court is available for lower-dollar disputes (limits vary by state: $10,000–$25,000 in most jurisdictions). For larger disputes, you'll need a construction attorney.

The **contractor board complaint** is a parallel track, not a replacement for contract dispute resolution. In California, the **Contractors State License Board (CSLB)** accepts complaints against licensed contractors and has enforcement authority that includes suspension and revocation of license, mediation, and — in cases involving financial harm — the Contractor's Recovery Fund [S2]. In Washington, **Labor & Industries (L&I)** handles contractor licensing complaints [S3]. In Oregon, the **Construction Contractors Board (CCB)** is the enforcement agency [S4]. Filing a board complaint costs nothing, creates an official record, and sometimes motivates resolution faster than legal process alone.

### When to Stop Work

If your contractor abandons the project — meaning the crew leaves and does not return without explanation for more than five consecutive business days — that is typically a material breach of contract. In California, abandonment is specifically defined under Business and Professions Code § 7107 as grounds for license revocation and civil liability [S1]. Other states have equivalent provisions. Do not immediately terminate the contractor the moment a crew is absent — confirm through written communication that you are noting the absence and requesting return to the site by a specific date. If no response and no return, document the date and time you sent that communication, photograph the site condition, and consult a construction attorney before taking further action.

### Replacing a Mid-Project Contractor

Replacing a contractor mid-project is costly, slow, and disruptive. It is also sometimes necessary. If you reach the point of formal termination, take these steps before you sign the termination notice.

Document everything. Photograph every square inch of the existing work — inside, outside, every mechanical system, every structural connection that's visible. Your photographs are the baseline against which the replacement contractor will assess completion percentage.

Get a third-party professional to assess what percentage of the work is complete, what work passes code, and what work will need to be corrected before a new contractor can proceed. This assessment is your negotiating position in the termination dispute and your baseline for the replacement contractor's bid.

Collect all contract documents, permits, plans, and inspection records. The replacement contractor will need all of these, and you are responsible for ensuring they're available.

Get completion bids from at least two replacement contractors who have experience completing mid-project work. Mid-project GCs price differently than start-of-project GCs — they are taking on someone else's risk — and their bids will likely be higher per unit of work than your original contract. The difference is part of the cost of the original contractor failure.

### Mechanic's Liens and Lien Waivers

Before you finalize the termination, understand your exposure to **mechanic's liens**. A mechanic's lien is a legal claim that a subcontractor or materials supplier can file against your property when they haven't been paid — even if you paid your GC in full. If your GC used your draw payments for something other than paying his subcontractors, those subs can lien your property for the unpaid amounts.

The protection against this is **lien waivers**. At each draw, before you release payment, request a conditional lien waiver from the GC and from each named subcontractor. The conditional waiver states that upon receipt of the specified payment, the signing party releases all lien rights for work completed through a specified date. Make this a routine part of every draw release — not a special request when a problem arises.

If you're already in a dispute situation and you haven't been collecting lien waivers, consult a construction attorney immediately. Your attorney can advise you on notice procedures, joint check arrangements, and other protective steps available in your state.

> **Common Mistake:** Assuming that because you paid the GC, you're protected from subcontractor liens. You are not. The GC's obligation to pay his subcontractors is a separate legal relationship from your payment to the GC, and in most states, subcontractors and material suppliers have statutory lien rights against your property regardless of whether you paid the GC. Lien waivers at each draw are the standard of care — build them into your draw release process from the first payment.

## Closing: From Construction Management to Cost Control

The discipline you build during construction — the weekly cadence, the documentation habits, the draw verification process — flows directly into the budget tracking framework in Chapter 10. Managing construction without tracking cost concurrently is like steering without looking at the speedometer. The PM dashboard and the draw schedule you've built in this chapter are the inputs to the budget tracking system: every released draw, every approved change order, every lien waiver feeds the running financial picture that tells you, at any moment, whether you're on budget or not.

The homeowners who finish within budget are almost never the ones who got the lowest bids. They're the ones who managed the information closely enough to catch drift early — in Week 4, not Week 20. They built the documentation habit that gave them negotiating leverage on change orders. They ran toward the information instead of waiting to receive it.

Chapter 10 will give you the budget tracking framework and the contingency methodology to complete that picture. But the raw material — the verified draw releases, the documented change orders, the running project log — comes from what you've built here.
