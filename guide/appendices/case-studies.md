# Appendix B — Case Studies: Four ADU Projects From the Field

These are real projects. The details have been anonymized — no names, no street addresses, no identifying information that would compromise anyone's privacy. The numbers, timelines, problems, and outcomes are accurate.

I've managed construction projects ranging from $12 million to $200 million over a twenty-year career. ADUs are small by dollar value and enormous by personal consequence. When a $280 million office building goes 15% over budget, the developer adjusts the capital stack and moves on. When a homeowner's $280,000 ADU project goes 30% over budget, the family may be looking at a materially worse financial position for years. That asymmetry is why this appendix exists.

Four projects. Two went reasonably well. Two were expensive education. All four produced lessons worth passing on.

---

## Case Study 1: The Bellevue Garage

**ADU type:** Garage conversion (detached, full conversion)
**Size:** 520 square feet
**Location:** Eastside suburb, King County, Washington
**Initial projected budget:** $165,000
**Final cost:** $172,000
**Budget variance:** +$7,000 (4.2% over)
**Projected timeline:** 14 months from permit submission to CO
**Actual timeline:** 14 months from permit submission to CO
**Current rental rate:** $2,100/month

### Background

The homeowners had a two-car detached garage they'd been using as a combination storage unit and gym. The structure was in good condition — concrete foundation, engineered roof, T1-11 siding that was weathered but sound. Their lot was in an unincorporated area of King County with a zoning code that allowed ADUs by right, no owner-occupancy requirement, and setbacks the existing structure already satisfied. They had owned the home for eleven years and had roughly $380,000 in equity.

I came in as a construction management consultant during design development, about eight weeks before permit submission.

### What They Did Right

**They started with feasibility, not enthusiasm.** Before hiring an architect, before calling contractors, before telling anyone about their ADU plan, they spent three weeks on the basics: confirming zoning, pulling the King County parcel report, walking the property with a tape measure, and getting a preliminary utility screen from their local utility district. That screen identified that their electrical service was 100-amp with capacity to support the ADU without a panel upgrade — a detail that would have been a $10,000–$14,000 surprise mid-project if they'd found it later.

**They hired an architect with local ADU experience.** The designer they used had permitted fourteen ADUs in King County in the previous two years. She knew the specific plan check comments that routinely came back from that jurisdiction, she had established relationships with the building department, and she produced a drawing set tight enough that they sailed through plan check with one minor correction. That correction — a note clarifying the egress window dimensions — was resolved in four days.

**They built a real budget, not an optimistic one.** The initial $165,000 budget included a 15% contingency line ($21,500). They built the contingency in before any scope was defined, not as a residual after they'd already allocated everything. The hard costs came in at $143,000 against a budgeted $138,000 — soft costs (design, engineering, permits, inspections) were $22,000 versus a budgeted $20,000. The $7,000 overage came from two change orders: a plumbing rough-in reconfiguration when they found a roof drain that conflicted with the planned bathroom location ($4,200), and upgraded casement windows when the homeowner decided the original spec felt cheap once she saw samples ($2,800). Both were conscious decisions. Neither was a surprise.

**They vetted three GCs and picked the second-lowest bid.** The bids came in at $118,000, $131,000, and $147,000 for the construction scope (excluding soft costs). They eliminated the lowest bidder after a reference check revealed he had a pattern of strong starts and distracted finishes — two references mentioned he'd started new projects before theirs were complete. The $131,000 bidder had two ADU references in the county both of whom gave unprompted praise for his communication and punch list follow-through. They chose him. He came in at $143,000, driven by those two change orders described above.

### The Key Decision

Hiring the architect with King County ADU experience was the single decision that shaped this outcome most. She compressed the plan check timeline, prevented scope creep during design, and wrote specifications clear enough that the GC couldn't claim ambiguity during construction. Good specifications don't just protect you legally — they prevent the low-grade disputes that bleed time and goodwill from a project.

### Lessons for Homeowners

**Lesson 1: The feasibility work you do before you spend a dollar on design is the highest-value work on the project.** This homeowner spent roughly twelve hours on due diligence — zoning research, the parcel report, the utility screen, a conversation with the building department — before writing a single check. That twelve hours identified that their setbacks were already compliant, their electrical was serviceable, and their sewer lateral was in good condition. It also confirmed they were in a ministerial review jurisdiction, not a discretionary one. Every hour of that upfront work was worth more than hours spent later negotiating change orders.

**Lesson 2: A 15% contingency is not padding — it is your margin of sanity.** This project used 33% of its contingency ($7,000 of $21,500) on two change orders that were partly elective and partly unavoidable. That's a normal outcome. Projects that start with a 5% contingency and hit a single unexpected plumbing condition don't just go over budget — they create conflict with the contractor, pressure the homeowner to cut scope elsewhere, and erode the relationship that needs to stay functional for another six months. Budget the contingency before you define the scope, not after.

**Lesson 3: Reference checks on contractors are not a formality.** The homeowners called six references across the three bidding contractors. Two of those calls were uncomfortable — one reference was lukewarm in a way that was clearly restrained, and another reference gave a response so glowing it felt canned. Both of those calls told them something. The references they got for the contractor they chose were specific, candid, and consistent with each other. That consistency is what you're looking for. Vague praise tells you nothing. Specific details — "he showed up for every inspection, he called me before I called him when there was an issue" — tell you what you need to know.

## Case Study 2: The $90,000 Surprise

**ADU type:** Detached ADU (new construction)
**Size:** 640 square feet
**Location:** Coastal community, Santa Barbara County, California
**Initial projected budget:** $280,000
**Final cost:** $362,000
**Budget variance:** +$82,000 (29.3% over)
**Projected timeline:** 18 months from permit submission to CO
**Actual timeline:** 22 months from permit submission to CO
**Break-even analysis:** Projected 7 years at $3,200/month rental. Actual break-even: 11+ years at current rental rate.

### Background

The homeowners had a large rear yard on a lot in a coastal community with excellent ADU ordinances — California law pre-empted most of the restrictive local provisions, and their parcel was well-suited for a 640 square foot detached unit. They planned to rent the ADU as a long-term rental to help offset the mortgage on a house they'd bought at the peak of a rising market.

The design was handled by a designer who did excellent work on the unit itself — good layout, appropriate specifications, permitted without incident. The mistake was not in the design. The mistake was in what nobody looked at before the permit was submitted.

### The Utility Screen That Didn't Happen

The homeowners never got a utility screen. Their designer didn't require one. Their GC, who was selected based largely on a friend's recommendation and a low bid, didn't flag it. The building department's plan check focused on zoning compliance and building code, not existing infrastructure condition. Nobody looked at the sewer lateral.

During construction, the GC opened a trench to connect the ADU to the main sewer lateral and discovered that the existing 4-inch cast iron main lateral was severely deteriorated — root intrusion throughout its entire 74-foot run, multiple offset joints, and a section near the street connection that had partially collapsed. The lateral served both the main house and the planned ADU connection.

The city required full replacement before they would permit the ADU connection. The scope: excavate and replace 74 feet of lateral with PVC, new cleanouts, city tap connection, and pavement restoration where the trench crossed the driveway. Final invoice: $28,400.

Six weeks later, the electrical inspection flagged a second problem. The main panel was a 100-amp service — installed in 1974 and at the end of its serviceable life — and the inspector's notation made clear that the new load from the ADU's dedicated circuits would require upgrading to 200-amp service. The upgrade required a new meter base, new panel, new service entrance conductors, coordination with the utility for a temporary disconnect, and the utility's own connection fee. Final invoice: $13,800.

Total unexpected utility costs: $42,200.

The remaining $39,800 in overages came from a combination of factors: a four-month construction delay caused by the lateral replacement, which pushed finish work into a period when roofing and concrete subcontractors were backlogged; two change orders driven by scope items that were underspecified in the original contract; and a cost escalation in lumber that hit during the framing delay.

### What a Pre-Design Utility Screen Would Have Looked Like

A utility screen is not complicated. Before design begins — ideally before you hire a designer — you do the following:

Contact your local sewer utility and ask them to pull the maintenance history on your lateral. Some utilities will do a camera inspection for a nominal fee; others will provide records showing when the lateral was last inspected or flagged. A lateral installed before 1980 in cast iron, serving a house that hasn't had documented lateral work, is a risk factor worth investigating before you commit to an ADU budget.

Contact your electrical utility and ask them to review the load calculation for your planned ADU against your existing service capacity. This is a ten-minute conversation. Tell them the square footage, tell them you're planning a full kitchen and HVAC, and ask whether your current service size can accommodate the additional load. If the answer is uncertain, hire a licensed electrician to do a panel load calculation before you build your budget. That calculation costs $300–$500 and can save you $10,000–$15,000 in budget surprises.

The specific questions that would have caught this: "When was your main sewer lateral installed, and has it ever been camera-inspected?" and "What is your current electrical service size, and do you know if it has been upgraded since the house was built?"

The homeowner in this case study didn't know to ask those questions. Now they do. So do you.

### The Key Decision

The key decision — the one that shaped everything — was selecting the GC based on a personal recommendation and a low bid without independently verifying his scope of pre-construction due diligence. A more experienced contractor or a construction manager brought in at the right moment would have flagged the lateral risk before the permit was filed. The lateral condition was knowable before construction started. It was discovered during construction because nobody looked before the dirt moved.

### Lessons for Homeowners

**Lesson 1: Conduct a pre-design utility screen before you commit to a budget.** This is not optional. The utility infrastructure that serves your existing house — sewer lateral, electrical service, water supply, gas service if applicable — may or may not have the capacity and condition to support an ADU. Finding a failed lateral or undersized electrical service during construction is a budget crisis. Finding it during pre-design is a line item. The questions are simple: How old is the lateral? Has it been inspected? What is the current service amperage, and can it handle additional load? Ask these questions before you draw a line on a site plan.

**Lesson 2: A low bid from an unknown contractor on a recommendation is a risk, not a value.** The contractor in this case study was technically competent at building — the finished ADU is a good unit. What he wasn't was a construction manager. He didn't see his role as helping his client understand and anticipate project risks. He saw his role as building what was on the drawings. For a simple, well-scoped project with no unknowns, that might be sufficient. For a project with aging infrastructure, that approach will find the problems when it's most expensive to solve them. Your contractor should be asking the utility questions too. If they're not, ask why.

**Lesson 3: Understand what your break-even actually means before you start.** This homeowner projected a 7-year break-even based on $280,000 in costs and $3,200/month in projected rental income. The 7-year projection was already optimistic — it assumed immediate occupancy, no vacancy, and no maintenance costs. At $362,000 and the same rental income, break-even is now 11+ years. That's a fundamentally different financial decision than the one they made. The difference between a 7-year and an 11-year payback is not academic. It's the difference between a project that makes financial sense and one that needs to be reconsidered. The lesson is not to avoid ADU projects — it's to build the budget honestly before you commit, not after.

## Case Study 3: The Two-Year Permit

**ADU type:** Detached ADU (new construction)
**Size:** 710 square feet
**Location:** Bay Area city, Alameda County, California
**Initial projected budget:** $315,000
**Final cost:** $391,000 (after cost escalation during delay)
**Budget variance:** +$76,000 (24.1% over, driven primarily by cost escalation during permit delay)
**Permit filed:** January 2022
**Permit issued:** March 2024
**Total permit timeline:** 26 months
**Project status:** Built and occupied as of November 2024

### Background

These homeowners did their research. They read the California ADU statutes. They understood that SB 9, AB 68, AB 3182, and the subsequent legislative package had substantially constrained cities' ability to reject ADU applications or impose discretionary review conditions. They believed — reasonably, based on what they'd read — that their project would go through a ministerial review process.

They were wrong about their specific city.

The city had a pre-existing discretionary review process tied to a neighborhood compatibility overlay that predated the California ADU reform legislation. The city's position, when the homeowners filed in January 2022, was that their overlay still applied. The homeowners' designer — who had ADU experience in other Bay Area cities but had not worked in this specific jurisdiction — was not aware of the overlay and had not caught it in her pre-application research.

The permit was filed. The building department accepted it. Six weeks later came the first letter: the application was being routed to the planning department for design review under the overlay. The planning commission had a four-week review cycle. The first hearing was continued because the commission needed additional materials. The second hearing produced a conditional approval with six design conditions — changes to the roofline, window placement, and exterior materials for neighborhood compatibility.

The designer revised the drawings to meet the conditions. Revised drawings submitted. Plan check comments came back three months later requesting changes to how the structural engineer had addressed a condition. Another revision cycle. The permit was finally issued in March 2024, twenty-six months after filing.

### What the Construction Cost Escalation Looked Like

In January 2022, the homeowners had three contractor bids in hand ranging from $248,000 to $291,000. They had selected the middle bidder and were planning to sign a contract upon permit issuance.

By March 2024, that contractor had not held his number. His revised quote was $331,000 — a 19% increase reflecting two years of labor cost inflation and material cost increases, particularly in rough framing lumber, insulation, and HVAC equipment. The other two original bidders had similar increases. The project went forward with the contractor at $331,000; with soft costs that had also grown during the two-year design revision cycle, total project cost reached $391,000.

The original business case had been built on a $315,000 investment with a projected 9-year break-even at $3,500/month rental income. At $391,000 and the same rental income, break-even extended to approximately 12 years. The homeowners also financed the project, and the interest rate environment in early 2024 was materially worse than it would have been in mid-2022 had the project proceeded on schedule.

### The Discretionary vs. Ministerial Distinction

This is the most important thing to understand about permit risk in California, and it applies in modified form across every state with ADU reform legislation.

State law, in California and elsewhere, generally mandates that ADUs that meet objective development standards must be approved ministerially — meaning a planning official reviews the application against published criteria and approves or denies based solely on whether the objective standards are met. There is no public hearing, no neighborhood notification, no discretionary judgment about whether the ADU is "compatible" with the neighborhood. It either meets the standards or it doesn't.

But local governments have layered overlay zones, specific plan areas, historic districts, design review areas, and other regulatory frameworks on top of zoning, some of which predate ADU reform legislation and some of which exist in legal gray areas about whether state law pre-empts them. Whether a given overlay survives California's ADU pre-emption framework is often a legal question that hasn't been definitively answered until a project tests it.

The homeowners in this case study ended up on the wrong side of that uncertainty for twenty-six months.

**Before filing any ADU application, ask the building or planning department directly:** "Is ADU review in this jurisdiction ministerial or discretionary for a project that meets your objective development standards? Are there any overlays, specific plan areas, or design review requirements that would route my application to the planning commission?" Ask this question at the counter, in writing, and keep the response. If the answer is ambiguous, consult a land use attorney before you spend money on design development.

### The Carrying Cost of a Delayed Permit

The homeowners in this case study were not able to put the project on hold during the permit process — they had already spent money on design and had made decisions based on a project timeline they now couldn't meet. During the 26-month delay, they paid:

- Carrying costs on project financing they'd arranged ($0, because they waited to draw on financing — one good decision in a bad situation)
- Designer fees for the multiple revision cycles driven by planning conditions: approximately $14,000 in fees beyond the original design contract
- The opportunity cost of 26 months without rental income from a unit they'd planned to have occupied by mid-2022

The lesson is not that permit delays are inevitable. It's that the cost of a permit delay is not just the delay itself — it's the cost escalation on a project that was sized for a different market, the financing terms available in a different rate environment, and the revenue lost during the period when you'd planned to be collecting rent.

### The Key Decision

The key decision that would have changed this outcome was verifying the specific permit process in this specific city before selecting a designer and before spending money on design development. The homeowners verified California state law. They did not verify whether their city had overlays that created a different process from what state law intended. Those are two different inquiries, and only one of them was done.

### Lessons for Homeowners

**Lesson 1: State ADU law and your city's actual ADU process are not the same thing.** Most homeowners who research ADU feasibility stop at the state level — they find the ADU reform legislation, confirm their state has it, and proceed. That is necessary but not sufficient. Your city may have overlays, historic districts, design review areas, or interpretive positions that create a different permitting experience from what state law contemplates. Before you spend money on design, call your planning department and ask specifically whether your project will go through ministerial or discretionary review. Get the answer in writing if possible.

**Lesson 2: Verify the specific jurisdiction's process, not the state's process.** When you hire a designer, ask them how many ADUs they have permitted in your specific city or county — not the region, not the state, not similar jurisdictions. Local process knowledge is not transferable. A designer who has permitted twenty ADUs in Oakland may walk into your Emeryville project with the same confident assumptions the designer in this case study brought — and discover the same gap between what state law says and what the local process actually is.

**Lesson 3: Understand the full cost of a delay before it happens.** A delayed permit doesn't just push your move-in date. It pushes it into a market with different construction costs, potentially different financing rates, and it extends the period during which you're not collecting rental income you'd planned on. When you build your project timeline, include a sensitivity analysis: what does my project look like if the permit takes six months longer than expected? Twelve months? What happens to my construction budget if costs escalate 10% during a delay? The project in this case study was financially viable at its original budget and rate environment. It became marginally viable at completion. The homeowners proceeded because they'd already committed, but they did so with a worse outcome than the one they signed up for.

## Case Study 4: The Denver Addition

**ADU type:** Attached ADU (converted garage plus addition)
**Size:** 680 square feet
**Location:** Denver metro suburb, Jefferson County, Colorado
**Initial projected budget:** $195,000
**Final cost:** $208,000
**Budget variance:** +$13,000 (6.7% over)
**Projected timeline:** 16 months from permit submission to CO
**Actual timeline:** 16 months from permit submission to CO
**Current use:** Multigenerational (owner's mother)
**Cash rent collected:** $0
**Avoided cost:** $5,400/month (equivalent assisted living rate in the Denver metro)

### Background

The homeowners' mother was 74, in reasonably good health, but beginning to show the early signs of the cognitive and mobility changes that often foreshadow a need for more structured care. She had been living alone in a house forty minutes away. The adult children were making that drive twice a week and worrying about it the other five days.

The decision to build the ADU was not primarily financial — it was about keeping the mother close, maintaining her independence and dignity, and avoiding the rupture of moving her into a facility before that was genuinely necessary. The financial analysis was done rigorously anyway, because the homeowners were careful people, and because a $195,000 commitment requires justification regardless of the motivation.

### The Project

The existing two-car garage was converted to living space and a 280 square foot addition was constructed on the rear of the garage to create a full one-bedroom unit with a kitchenette, accessible bathroom, and private entrance. The design incorporated accessible features throughout: wider doorways, curbless shower, lever hardware, blocking in the shower walls for future grab bars, and a step-free path from the private entrance to the main house.

The project came in at $208,000, with the $13,000 overage attributable to a geotechnical soil report that the structural engineer required when they discovered the rear addition would be built over fill soil (this added $6,500 in engineering and $4,800 in additional foundation work), and a change order to upgrade the HVAC system to a split-system heat pump when the original forced-air extension spec proved impractical given the ceiling heights in the converted garage ($1,700 net after a value-engineering adjustment elsewhere).

The project was permitted in Jefferson County, which had adopted a relatively permissive ADU ordinance aligned with Colorado's 2024 HB 1175, and moved through plan check without significant issues.

### Quantifying Multigenerational Value

Most ADU financial analyses are built on rental income projections. When the ADU's intended use is multigenerational — housing a parent, an adult child, an in-law — the financial model needs a different framework. Here is the one applied to this project, using the approach described in Chapter 3.

**Step 1: Establish the avoided-cost baseline.** What would the family pay for equivalent care or housing if the ADU didn't exist? The relevant comparison here was not an apartment — the mother needed some level of proximity and, eventually, care access. The realistic alternative was an independent living community in the Denver metro. Current rates in 2024 ranged from $4,200 to $6,800 per month. A conservative middle estimate of $5,400 per month was used.

**Step 2: Calculate the annual avoided cost.** At $5,400/month, the annual value of keeping the mother in the ADU rather than an independent living facility is $64,800. This is not income — it's an avoided expense — but it is economically equivalent. The family has $64,800/year more in aggregate purchasing power than they would have if the mother were paying facility rates.

**Step 3: Apply the avoided cost to break-even.** The total project cost was $208,000. At $64,800/year in avoided cost, the effective break-even is 3.2 years. The homeowners had initially modeled break-even at year 4 under rental assumptions ($2,800/month = $33,600/year, 6.2 years to break-even at $208,000). The multigenerational avoided-cost model reached effective break-even faster because the avoided cost exceeded typical market rental rates for the unit.

**Step 4: Account for non-quantifiable value.** The homeowners made a deliberate decision to include a line in their analysis acknowledging that reduced caregiver stress, the mother's improved quality of life, and the ability to monitor her health closely have value that doesn't appear in a financial model. They did not attempt to quantify this. They noted it, accepted that it made the project more worthwhile than the financial model alone showed, and moved on.

**Step 5: Build a transition model.** The ADU's value is contingent on the mother's continued ability to live independently. The homeowners modeled a scenario in which, at year 7, she transitions to memory care (estimated $8,000–$12,000/month). At that point, the ADU converts to a rental at $2,600–$2,800/month. Under this model, the ADU continues to generate value throughout its useful life under two different use scenarios. The question "what happens when she needs more care than the ADU can provide?" was answered in advance, not deferred.

### The Key Decision

The key decision in this project was treating the financial analysis with the same rigor they would have applied if this were a rental investment, even though the primary motivation was not financial. That discipline — running the numbers honestly, including the break-even analysis and the transition model — gave the homeowners confidence that they were making a sound decision and clarity about what they were committing to. ADUs built for multigenerational use are sometimes rationalized rather than analyzed. This family analyzed it, which meant they had no illusions and no regrets.

### Lessons for Homeowners

**Lesson 1: Multigenerational value is quantifiable — use the avoided-cost framework.** The most common error in multigenerational ADU analysis is treating the financial case as unanswerable because there's no cash rent. There is almost always a legitimate financial case to be made through avoided costs: assisted living rates, in-home care rates, the cost of a parent renting a separate unit in a market they could otherwise age out of, adult children reducing paid caregiver hours. Build the model honestly. If the numbers work, proceed with confidence. If they don't, proceed with eyes open.

**Lesson 2: Design for the use case, not just the square footage.** The accessible features in this ADU — the curbless shower, the blocking for grab bars, the lever hardware, the step-free entry — added approximately $4,500 to construction cost. They would add tens of thousands to retrofit cost if installed after the fact, and they make the unit usable for the mother's likely future needs rather than just her current condition. Designing for the use case means thinking about who will live in the unit five years from now, not just on move-in day.

**Lesson 3: Model the transition before you build, not after.** Every multigenerational ADU will eventually transition — to a rental, to a different family member, to sale. Knowing what that transition looks like, and whether the ADU's design and location support multiple use cases, is information you need before you break ground. An ADU designed specifically for a 74-year-old parent's current needs may be difficult to rent at market rate if it lacks a full kitchen or has an awkward floor plan. The ADU in this case study was designed for both present and future use. That was a deliberate choice that required a small amount of additional design thought and no additional construction cost.
