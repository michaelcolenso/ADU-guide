# Appendix C — 40 Questions to Bring to Every Meeting

The homeowner who asks specific questions gets specific answers. The homeowner who shows up without questions gets managed. Every professional you'll deal with in an ADU project — planners, architects, contractors, lenders, inspectors — is accustomed to being in the room with the most domain knowledge. That asymmetry works against you unless you close it. This list closes it.

Print this appendix and bring it to every relevant meeting. Highlight the questions that apply to your situation. Add your own. The goal is not to interrogate anyone — it's to establish early that you are a prepared owner who will be paying close attention for the duration of this project.

---

## Part 1: Planning Counter / Building Department
*Ask these questions before you hire a designer. The answers will shape every decision that follows.*

**1. Is my property zoned to allow an ADU by right, or is there a discretionary process?**

This is the most important question you will ask at the planning counter, and you should not move past it until you have a clear answer. You want to hear the words "ministerial review" or "by right." That means a planning official checks your application against published objective standards and approves or denies based solely on compliance — no public hearing, no neighbor notification, no planning commission discretion.

*Good answer:* "Your parcel is zoned [zone designation], which allows ADUs by right. As long as your project meets the objective development standards, it goes through ministerial plan check." *Red flag:* "It depends — we'll have to evaluate the application and see if any review is triggered." That answer means there is a discretionary element somewhere in the process, and you need to find out exactly what it is and what triggers it before you commit to a design.

---

**2. What are the current objective development standards: maximum size, setbacks, height, and lot coverage?**

You want specific numbers. These will set the parameters for everything your architect designs.

*Good answer:* "Maximum ADU size is 1,200 square feet or 50% of the primary dwelling, whichever is less. Rear setback is four feet. Side setback is four feet. Maximum height is sixteen feet for a detached ADU. You can check your current lot coverage on the parcel report." *Red flag:* Any answer that references "it varies by situation" without providing the underlying standards. The standards either exist in published form or they don't. If they exist, the planner should be able to hand you the code section or a reference sheet.

---

**3. Do you have a pre-approved ADU plan program?**

Many jurisdictions offer pre-approved plan libraries — sets of architect-drawn ADU plans that have already passed plan check for building code compliance. If you use a pre-approved plan, your permit processing time is typically cut in half or more, and your plan check fees may be reduced. This is one of the most underutilized shortcuts in the ADU permitting process.

*Good answer:* "Yes, we have [number] approved plans available on our website. They still require a site-specific plan check for utilities and setback compliance, but the architectural drawings are pre-cleared." *Concerning answer:* "We used to have a program but it hasn't been updated." Outdated plans may not comply with current code — confirm before you invest time in a pre-approved set.

---

**4. What constitutes a complete application? What documents are required at submission?**

Incomplete applications are the single most common source of plan check delay. Many jurisdictions will not begin the review clock until they receive every required document. Get the complete checklist in writing before your designer starts work so the drawing set is built to meet every submission requirement.

*Good answer:* A printed or emailed checklist of every required document. *Red flag:* A vague verbal list with no written documentation. Follow up in writing after the meeting: "I want to confirm the submission requirements for an ADU application. Can you email me the current checklist or point me to where it's published?" A written record protects you if the requirements change between your conversation and your submission.

---

**5. What is your current plan check timeline for ADU applications?**

Timelines vary enormously — from three weeks in a streamlined jurisdiction to six months or more in a backlogged one. Some jurisdictions have tiered timelines depending on whether the project triggers over-the-counter review or full plan check. Get a current estimate, not a historical average, and ask when the last batch of ADU applications was approved.

*Good answer:* "Our current over-the-counter timeline is approximately four weeks. Full plan check is running eight to ten weeks." *Concerning answer:* "It really depends." Press for a current estimate. Ask: "What was the last ADU application that came through? How long did it take?" Specific recent data is more useful than a general policy statement.

---

**6. Is there a pre-application meeting available, and how do I schedule it?**

Many building and planning departments offer pre-application meetings — a formal or informal session with a planner or plans examiner before you file. These meetings are free or low-cost and can surface problems with your project concept before you've spent $8,000–$15,000 on design development. If one is available, schedule it before your designer finalizes the schematic.

*Good answer:* "Yes, we offer pre-application meetings for ADUs. They're scheduled on [specific day/time], there's no fee, and you can book online." *Concerning answer:* "We don't have the capacity to do pre-application meetings right now." If pre-application meetings aren't available, ask whether you can submit written questions to a planner by email before filing. A written response becomes documentation you can reference if there's a dispute about what you were told.

---

**7. Are there any overlay zones, historic districts, flood zones, or hillside restrictions that apply to my parcel?**

These overlays can fundamentally change the permit process, the design requirements, or the project's feasibility. A historic district overlay may require design review for exterior changes. A hillside overlay may require geotechnical engineering and trigger site plan review. A flood zone designation may affect foundation design and insurance. You need to know what layers apply to your specific parcel before you design anything.

*Good answer:* "I can pull your parcel up now. It's not in any overlay, it's outside the flood zone, and there are no special conditions on this parcel." *Red flag:* Uncertainty about what overlays apply, or a suggestion that you look it up yourself without guidance on where to find accurate parcel-level data. Ask for the parcel report or GIS layers that show your parcel's specific conditions, and get the source so you can verify independently.

---

**8. What are the permit fees for an ADU of approximately [your estimated size] square feet?**

Permit fees for ADUs include plan check fees, building permit fees, and potentially inspection fees, utility connection fees, and school impact fees. The total can range from $2,000 to $15,000 or more depending on jurisdiction, unit size, and applicable fee categories. Get a fee estimate at the planning counter and ask whether impact fees apply to your project size — California, Washington, and Oregon have specific exemptions for smaller ADUs, but the details vary.

*Good answer:* A fee schedule or a rough estimate broken down by category (plan check, building permit, inspections, utilities). *Red flag:* "We can't estimate fees until we see the drawings." While precise fees require an application, ballpark estimates by unit size are standard practice. If the planner genuinely can't give you any estimate, ask for the fee schedule document so your designer can help you estimate.

## Part 2: Architect / Designer
*Ask these questions during your initial interview, before you sign any design contract.*

**1. How many ADUs have you designed that have been permitted and built in this specific jurisdiction?**

Not the region. Not similar cities. This specific jurisdiction. Permit process knowledge is hyper-local — an architect with thirty ADU permits in Los Angeles may not know the specific plan check comments that come back routinely in Santa Monica or Culver City. Jurisdiction-specific experience compresses timelines and reduces the revision cycles that add cost.

*Good answer:* A specific number, with willingness to provide references from projects in your jurisdiction. Three or more completed ADUs in your specific city or county is a meaningful benchmark. *Concerning answer:* "I've done a lot of work in this area" without a specific number. Follow up: "Can you tell me the addresses of three ADUs you've designed in [your city/county] that are currently permitted and built?" If they can't name three, ask what they've designed in the closest comparable jurisdiction and why they're confident the process will transfer.

---

**2. Can you show me three examples of completed ADU projects you've designed, with photos?**

Portfolio review is standard practice. You want to see completed, built projects — not renderings, not projects that are in design. Photos of finished units tell you about the designer's sense of space, materiality, and livability. Ask about the square footage, the construction cost, and whether the project came in on budget.

*Good answer:* A portfolio of built projects with photos, willingness to discuss budget outcomes. *Red flag:* A portfolio of renderings or projects that are "in permitting." Rendering skill and design-build execution skill are different capabilities. You're hiring someone to get a building built, not to produce beautiful visualizations.

---

**3. What does your fee cover — schematic design only, or through construction documents and permit support?**

Design fees are quoted in different ways and cover different scopes. Schematic design produces a concept. Construction documents produce the drawing set needed for permit and for your contractor to build from. Permit support means the designer responds to plan check comments and manages the correction cycle. Clarify exactly where their scope ends. If their contract ends at permit submission and your permit comes back with corrections, who pays for the revision work?

*Good answer:* A clear written scope that specifies: schematic design, design development, construction documents, permit coordination, and any limitations on plan check revision rounds. *Red flag:* A verbal description without a written scope. Before signing any design contract, the scope of services should be spelled out in writing.

---

**4. How do you handle plan check corrections — is revision work included in your fee?**

Most ADU projects receive at least one round of plan check comments. Some receive two or three. Each correction cycle requires the designer to revise drawings and resubmit. If that work is billed hourly on top of the design fee, corrections can add $2,000–$5,000 to your soft costs. Clarify this before you sign.

*Good answer:* "My fee includes up to two rounds of plan check corrections. Additional rounds are billed at [hourly rate], but I'll let you know before that threshold is reached." *Concerning answer:* "We'll deal with corrections when we get there." That answer means you have no visibility into the cost of corrections before they happen. Negotiate a specific number of included correction rounds into the contract.

---

**5. Do you coordinate with structural engineering, or do I hire that separately?**

Most ADU projects require structural engineering — at minimum, a foundation plan and shear wall specifications; often a complete structural set. Some designers include structural engineering coordination in their scope. Others provide architectural drawings and leave you to hire and coordinate a structural engineer separately. Neither is inherently better, but you need to know which model applies so you're not surprised when the structural engineer's invoice arrives.

*Good answer:* A clear statement of whether structural engineering is included in the fee or separate, and who manages the coordination if it's a separate contract. *Red flag:* Ambiguity about whether structural is included. Follow up: "If structural engineering is required for this project, who provides it, who manages the relationship, and where does that cost appear in the budget?"

---

**6. What is your typical timeline from kickoff to complete permit-ready drawing set?**

Design timelines vary from eight weeks to six months depending on the designer's workload, project complexity, and how much back-and-forth is required during design development. Get a specific estimate and ask what drives variability — a designer who has a clear timeline and can articulate the dependencies is better organized than one who gives you a vague range.

*Good answer:* "For a project of this scope, our typical timeline from kickoff to permit-ready drawings is twelve to fourteen weeks. That assumes two design review rounds with you and no significant scope changes after design development." *Concerning answer:* "It depends on a lot of factors." That's true but uninformative. Press for a baseline estimate and a list of what would extend it.

---

**7. If the plans are rejected during plan check, what is your process and cost for corrections?**

This is a follow-on to question 4, but worth asking explicitly: what does your correction process look like? How quickly do you turn around revision sets? Do you communicate with the building department on your client's behalf, or do you hand the corrected drawings to the client and let them manage resubmission? In a jurisdiction with a 6–8 week plan check cycle, each correction round that's mismanaged can add weeks to your timeline.

*Good answer:* "When plan check comments come back, I review them, prepare a written response documenting how each comment is addressed, revise the drawings, and resubmit with a correction matrix. My typical turnaround on corrections is two to three weeks." *Concerning answer:* "We address the comments and resubmit." That tells you nothing about their process or timeline. Efficiency in the correction cycle directly affects your project timeline.

## Part 3: General Contractor
*Ask these questions before you accept any bid. These are not optional questions for large projects.*

**1. How many ADUs have you permitted and built in this jurisdiction in the past 24 months?**

Jurisdictional experience matters as much for contractors as it does for designers. A contractor who has pulled permits in your city knows the inspectors, knows what the inspectors look for, and knows which subcontractors work well in that jurisdiction. That local knowledge translates into fewer failed inspections and smoother project execution. The 24-month window matters — ADU ordinances change, and you want current experience.

*Good answer:* A specific number with willingness to provide project references. Two or more completed ADUs in your jurisdiction in the past 24 months is a meaningful floor for an ADU specialist. *Concerning answer:* "I've done a lot of residential work in this area." Residential construction experience and ADU-specific permit experience are different things. Press for specifics.

---

**2. Can you provide three ADU owner references with contact information, from projects completed in the last 18 months?**

Request references before you accept a bid, not after. References from the past 18 months reflect the contractor's current organization, current crew, and current subcontractors — which may be different from the team that completed projects two or three years ago. When you call the references, ask about budget adherence, communication, and punch list follow-through. See Part 6 of this appendix for the specific questions to ask.

*Good answer:* Three references provided without hesitation. *Red flag:* Reluctance, delay, or references that are consistently from more than two years ago. Some contractors provide references who are friends or family — call the references and listen for specificity. Genuine project owners describe specific details. Coached references give general praise.

---

**3. What is your license number, and are you bonded and insured with general liability of at least $1 million per occurrence?**

This is not optional. Verify the license number independently through your state contractor licensing board website before you sign a contract. Confirm that the general liability policy is current and covers at least $1 million per occurrence — not aggregate, per occurrence. Ask for a certificate of insurance naming you as additional insured. If the contractor is unwilling to provide this, stop the conversation.

*Good answer:* A license number provided immediately, willingness to provide a certificate of insurance without delay. *Red flag:* Hesitation, vague answers about insurance "being handled," or any suggestion that this documentation isn't available right now. A licensed, insured contractor has this documentation readily available. A contractor who doesn't is either uninsured or disorganized — either is disqualifying.

---

**4. Do you carry workers' compensation for all direct employees and require it from all subcontractors?**

If a worker is injured on your property and the contractor doesn't have workers' compensation coverage, you may be exposed to liability. Workers' comp coverage for direct employees is required by law in most states, but enforcement varies. Subcontractor coverage is a separate question — a general contractor who doesn't require workers' comp certificates from every subcontractor is leaving you exposed. Ask to see the GC's workers' comp certificate and their standard subcontract language requiring coverage.

*Good answer:* "Yes, we carry workers' comp for all direct employees and require certificates of insurance from every subcontractor before they're allowed on site." *Red flag:* Uncertainty about subcontractor coverage, or a suggestion that this is handled informally.

---

**5. What does your draw schedule look like, and what triggers each draw?**

A draw schedule is the payment plan tied to construction milestones — you pay the contractor as specific phases of work are completed and verified. Never pay in full upfront, and be cautious of any draw schedule that front-loads payments heavily before work is completed. A reasonable structure ties each draw to verifiable completion milestones: permit issuance, foundation complete, framing complete, rough MEP inspections passed, insulation and drywall complete, substantial completion.

*Good answer:* A written draw schedule with specific milestone triggers for each payment, tied to inspection or completion events you can verify independently. *Red flag:* A draw schedule that requests more than 10–15% down before work begins, or payments tied to calendar dates rather than completion milestones. Calendar-based payments reward the contractor for being on-site, not for completing work.

---

**6. Who is the site supervisor, and what is their daily on-site presence?**

On larger residential projects, the person who sells you the job may not be the person managing the job. Ask specifically who the site supervisor or project manager is, how many other active projects they're managing, and what their expected daily presence on your site looks like. A supervisor managing six projects simultaneously will not give yours adequate attention.

*Good answer:* A named individual with a clear role, a reasonable number of concurrent projects, and a specific on-site schedule — ideally daily at minimum during active phases. *Red flag:* Vague answers about "the team" without a named supervisor, or confirmation that the supervisor is also managing multiple other projects simultaneously.

---

**7. What is your change order process — when does a CO get issued, and how quickly is it priced?**

Change orders (COs) are written amendments to the contract that cover work not in the original scope. You want a contractor who issues change orders promptly and in writing for every scope change, no matter how small. Oral agreements about changes are disputes waiting to happen. Ask how quickly they price COs after a change is identified, what markup they charge on CO work, and whether you approve the CO before the work proceeds.

*Good answer:* "We issue change orders in writing within 48 hours of identifying a scope change. We don't proceed with CO work until the homeowner has signed it. Our markup on CO work is [specific percentage]." *Red flag:* A contractor who suggests that minor changes will be handled verbally and settled at the end, or who is vague about CO markup rates. The markup on change order work — typically 10–20% overhead and profit — should be defined in the contract, not negotiated after the fact.

---

**8. What percentage of your ADU projects have exceeded original budget, and by how much on average?**

This question disarms contractors who would otherwise present themselves as infallible. A good contractor will give you a candid answer: most projects experience some change orders, and a small percentage exceed the original contract by more than 5–10% due to unforeseen conditions or owner-requested changes. An answer of "none" is not credible. An answer of "20–30% on average" is a red flag.

*Good answer:* "Most of our projects come in within 5–10% of contract. The exceptions are usually unforeseen site conditions or owner-requested scope changes. We document everything as it comes up." *Red flag:* Implausible perfection ("we never go over budget") or defensiveness ("it depends on the homeowner"). Both answers tell you something about how this contractor manages client communication.

---

**9. Do you use a construction management platform (Buildertrend, Procore, or similar)? Can I have access?**

Construction management platforms give homeowners real-time visibility into schedule, documents, photos, and change orders without requiring constant check-in calls. The better GCs use them as standard practice. Access to the platform means you can verify progress against schedule, download inspection reports, and maintain your own record of the project's documentation without relying entirely on the contractor's word.

*Good answer:* "Yes, we use [platform]. We'll set you up with owner access at kickoff. You'll be able to see the schedule, photos, documents, and change orders in real time." *Concerning answer:* "We manage everything by email and phone." Email and phone are functional, but they put the documentation burden on you. If the contractor doesn't use a platform, establish a written communication protocol and a photo documentation schedule in the contract.

---

**10. Have you had any complaints filed with the state contractor board in the past 3 years?**

Ask directly. Then verify independently through your state's contractor license board lookup, which typically includes complaint and citation history. A single resolved complaint from several years ago is different from a pattern of unresolved complaints or active citations. The answer the contractor gives you should match what the license board record shows.

*Good answer:* A candid response — either "no" (verifiable) or an explanation of a past complaint that's consistent with the public record. *Red flag:* Defensiveness, a claim of ignorance ("I don't know, you'd have to check"), or a response that contradicts what you find in the license board record.

## Part 4: Lender
*Ask these questions before completing any loan application. Loan products and terms vary more than most homeowners expect.*

**1. Do you offer ADU-specific construction loan products, or are you using a standard construction loan framework?**

Some lenders have developed ADU-specific loan products with underwriting criteria and draw processes designed for the ADU construction cycle. Others use standard construction loan frameworks that may not account for ADU-specific factors: the fact that the ADU shares a parcel with the primary residence, the after-renovation valuation methodology, or the shorter construction timeline. Knowing which framework applies will affect everything else in this conversation.

*Good answer:* A clear description of the specific loan product, its underwriting criteria, and how it's structured for ADU construction. *Concerning answer:* "A construction loan is a construction loan — they all work the same way." They don't. Press for specifics.

---

**2. What is the maximum loan-to-value or loan-to-cost for an ADU construction loan?**

Lenders cap construction loans as a percentage of either the property's current value (loan-to-value) or the total project cost (loan-to-cost). Understanding both ratios tells you how much of the project cost you can finance versus how much you need to bring as equity. A lender who caps at 80% of after-renovation value on a project where the ADU adds $200,000 in value to a property worth $800,000 will behave very differently from one who caps at 75% of total project cost.

*Good answer:* Specific ratios with an explanation of how they apply to your project scenario. *Concerning answer:* Vague references to "it depends on the appraisal." While the appraisal does affect the final number, a lender should be able to tell you the policy ratios before the appraisal is ordered.

---

**3. How is the "after-renovation value" or "as-completed value" determined for the appraisal?**

The as-completed value appraisal estimates what your property will be worth once the ADU is built. This number drives your maximum loan amount. Ask how the appraisal is ordered, who the appraiser will be, and how ADU value is typically calculated by that appraiser. In markets where ADU comparables are limited, appraisers may use income capitalization methods to value the ADU's rental income — ask how this has played out on recent comparable loans.

*Good answer:* An explanation of the appraisal methodology, the appraiser selection process, and examples from recent similar loans. *Concerning answer:* "The appraiser will figure it out." You are paying for the appraisal. You are entitled to understand how the value will be determined before you order it.

---

**4. What is your draw schedule process — how many draws, what triggers each, and what does each inspection cost?**

Construction loan draws are disbursements from your loan made at construction milestones. Each draw typically requires an inspection by a third-party inspector hired by the lender. That inspection has a fee — usually $150–$500 per draw — and can take one to two weeks to schedule, which affects your contractor's cash flow and your construction schedule. Understand the draw process before you finalize your project schedule.

*Good answer:* A specific number of draws (typically 4–8 for an ADU), a description of the milestone triggers for each, the inspection fee, and the typical turnaround time from draw request to funds released. *Red flag:* Vague answers about draw timing. A slow draw process can create cash flow problems for your contractor that then become your problems.

---

**5. What are the interest rate and term during construction, and how does the loan convert after the certificate of occupancy?**

Construction loans are typically interest-only during the construction period, with the full loan balance converting to a permanent loan (or being paid off through a refinance) after the certificate of occupancy is issued. Understand the rate during construction, the conversion process, whether conversion is automatic or requires a new application, and what the permanent loan terms will be.

*Good answer:* A specific interest rate (or rate structure, such as prime plus margin) for the construction period, an explanation of the conversion mechanism, and the expected permanent loan terms. *Concerning answer:* "The permanent rate will be determined at conversion based on market rates at that time." While market rates do change, you should understand whether you can lock the permanent rate at origination and what options you have if rates move against you during construction.

---

**6. What documentation do you need for a complete application?**

Get the full documentation list before you apply. Incomplete applications delay underwriting. Typical requirements include income documentation, tax returns, credit authorization, the signed construction contract, the draw schedule, the contractor's license and insurance, and the approved plans and permits. Some lenders also require a title report, survey, or soil report. Knowing the full list in advance prevents the "one more document" delays that extend your application timeline.

*Good answer:* A written checklist of every required document, organized by category. *Red flag:* A verbal list without a written checklist. Request the written checklist before you leave the meeting.

---

**7. What are the total origination costs, and are any of them rolled into the loan?**

Lender origination fees, appraisal fees, title insurance, and other closing costs can add 1–3% of the loan amount to your project cost. Some lenders allow these costs to be rolled into the loan balance; others require them paid at closing. You need to know the total cost of financing before you compare lenders.

*Good answer:* A loan estimate that itemizes all origination costs and a clear statement of which costs can be financed and which must be paid out of pocket at closing. *Red flag:* Reluctance to provide cost estimates before the application is submitted. Lenders are required to provide a Loan Estimate within three business days of application; ask for a preliminary estimate before you apply so you can compare lenders.

---

**8. If construction runs over budget, what are my options?**

Cost overruns happen. Before you finalize your loan, understand what your options are if the project comes in over the original loan amount. Can you request a loan modification to increase the principal? Can you use your contingency reserve? Do you need to bring additional cash at completion to close out the construction loan? Knowing the answer in advance means you're not making this decision under pressure in the middle of a project.

*Good answer:* A clear explanation of your options — whether the loan can be modified, what the process is, and whether there are any pre-approved mechanisms for handling reasonable cost overruns. *Concerning answer:* "Let's cross that bridge when we come to it." That answer means the lender hasn't thought through this scenario and you may be on your own if it happens.

---

## Part 5: Inspector
*Ask these questions at each scheduled inspection. Inspectors are your independent check on construction quality.*

**1. What specifically are you looking at in this inspection?**

Every inspection has a defined scope — framing, rough plumbing, rough electrical, insulation, drywall, final, and so on. Asking this question at the start of each inspection ensures you understand what will be reviewed and gives you an opportunity to flag any areas of concern before the inspector begins. It also establishes that you're paying attention, which inspectors generally respond to with additional care.

*Good answer:* A specific description of the inspection scope tied to the current phase of construction. *Use this question as an opener, not a test* — most inspectors will answer it helpfully. The goal is to understand what's being reviewed, not to catch anyone out.

---

**2. Are there any items you'd flag as marginal or that you'd like to see corrected proactively?**

Inspectors see a lot of construction. They often notice things that are technically within tolerance but that represent lower-quality workmanship or borderline conditions. Some inspectors will volunteer this information; others won't unless asked. Asking directly invites them to share informal observations that can prevent problems later.

*Good answer:* Any candid observation, even minor. An inspector who notices that a joist hanger is missing a few nails and mentions it before the drywall goes up has saved you a deficiency correction. An inspector who says "looks fine" and moves on may simply mean everything is genuinely fine — or may mean they're moving quickly through a full inspection schedule.

---

**3. If anything fails today, what exactly needs to be corrected and re-inspected?**

When an inspection fails, the correction notice should specify exactly what needs to be fixed and whether the correction requires a full re-inspection or a simple documentation correction. Understanding this immediately after a failed inspection means your contractor can prioritize corrections efficiently rather than interpreting ambiguous notes.

*Good answer:* A specific list of corrections with clear pass/fail criteria. Ask for the correction notice in writing before you leave. *Follow-up action:* Review the correction notice with your contractor the same day. Ambiguous correction language should be clarified with the inspector before the contractor does any remediation work.

---

**4. Is there a re-inspection fee, and what is the timeline?**

Re-inspection fees vary by jurisdiction — some charge a flat fee per re-inspection, others charge nothing for the first re-inspection and a fee for subsequent ones. The timeline for a re-inspection appointment can also vary from next-day to two weeks, depending on inspector workload. Understanding this before a failure happens means you can build it into your contingency planning.

*Good answer:* A specific fee structure and an estimated timeline for scheduling a re-inspection. *Note:* If you're already at an inspection when something fails, this question is time-sensitive — ask before you leave the site so you can schedule the re-inspection immediately rather than losing a week.

---

## Part 6: Contractor References
*Call every reference before you sign any construction contract. These are the three questions that matter most.*

**1. Did the project come in at or near the original contract price? If not, what drove the overages?**

Budget adherence is the most concrete measure of a contractor's estimate quality and change order discipline. A project that came in 25% over budget without a proportional explanation (significant owner-requested changes or major unforeseen site conditions) tells you something important about how this contractor operates. A project that came in within 10% with clear explanations for what drove changes tells you something else. Listen for specificity in the answer — a reference who can explain exactly what drove overages and why they were reasonable is giving you useful information. A reference who says "I don't really remember" or gives a vague positive response is not.

*Follow-up:* "Were the change orders priced fairly? Did you feel you understood what you were signing before you signed it?" The change order process is where contractor integrity is most tested.

---

**2. Did the contractor respond to your concerns promptly and professionally during construction?**

Construction projects generate concerns. Homeowners notice things, have questions, and occasionally have legitimate complaints. A contractor's responsiveness to those concerns — not just to emergencies, but to ordinary questions and non-urgent concerns — tells you a great deal about how they'll handle the six to sixteen months you'll spend in a contractual relationship with them.

*Follow-up:* "Was there a moment during the project when you were frustrated with the contractor's communication? How was it resolved?" Most projects have at least one friction point. How the contractor handles friction is the real question. A reference who says "there was never any friction" is either describing a perfectly managed project or is not being forthcoming.

---

**3. Would you hire this contractor again for a similar project?**

This is the closing question, and it should be asked after you've established rapport through the first two questions. "Would you hire them again" is binary and direct, and most people will answer it honestly. A yes from a reference who has also described some friction points is meaningful. A yes from a reference who has described nothing but perfection may be less so. A hesitation or a qualified "probably, but..." followed by an explanation is worth listening to carefully.

*Follow-up if the answer is anything other than an unqualified yes:* "What would you want a future owner to know before hiring this contractor?" That question opens the door to information the reference might not have volunteered unprompted.

---

*Use this list as a starting point. Add your own questions specific to your project. The homeowner who comes prepared gets better answers and better outcomes.*
