# Appendix A — Glossary of ADU and Construction Terms

These definitions are written for homeowners, not construction professionals. If a term appears in this guide in a context that requires more nuance than the definition below, the surrounding text will explain it. Use this glossary to get oriented quickly; use the relevant chapter to understand the full implications.

---

**Accessory Dwelling Unit (ADU)**
An independent residential unit on the same lot as a primary home, with its own living, sleeping, cooking, and sanitation facilities. An ADU may be detached (a separate structure), attached (sharing a wall with the primary home), or created within the existing structure (garage conversion). The unit is "accessory" in the legal sense — it is secondary to, and dependent on, the primary residence for purposes of land use and permitting.

**ADU Amnesty Program**
A local or state program that allows homeowners to legalize existing unpermitted or non-conforming ADUs — often called "in-law units" or informal rentals — by bringing them into compliance with current code. Amnesty programs typically offer reduced fees, simplified inspections, and limited liability for pre-existing code violations. California, Seattle, and Portland have all operated amnesty programs at various times. Check Chapter 5 for your state's current status.

**ADU Loan (ADU-Specific Lending Product)**
A specialized financing product designed for ADU construction, typically offered by credit unions, community banks, and a small number of national lenders. These products differ from standard construction loans in their underwriting approach, which accounts for the ADU's projected rental income in qualifying calculations and uses after-renovation value for the combined parcel. See Chapter 6 for a comparison of ADU-specific and conventional financing options.

**Architect of Record**
The licensed architect who stamps and takes professional responsibility for the construction documents submitted for permit. On smaller ADU projects, this may be the same person who designed the unit; on projects where a designer or draftsperson prepares the drawings, a separate licensed architect may stamp them. Whoever holds this role is legally responsible for the design's compliance with applicable codes.

**As-Built Drawings**
The final set of construction documents updated to reflect what was actually built, including any deviations from the permitted drawings. As-built drawings are created at project close and should be filed with your local building department if required. They become the authoritative record of the ADU's systems — critical if you ever need to locate electrical panels, plumbing runs, or structural elements during a future renovation.

**Attached ADU**
An ADU that shares one or more walls with the primary dwelling. An attached ADU typically costs less per square foot than a detached ADU because it shares structure and may share some systems, but layout compromises are common when working around the existing building geometry.

**By-Right Approval**
An approval process in which a permit application is reviewed only against published, objective development standards — no discretionary judgment, no public hearing, no neighbor notification required. If your project meets the objective standards, it must be approved. Most state ADU reform legislation has pushed jurisdictions toward by-right approval for qualifying ADUs. The opposite of by-right is discretionary review. See Chapter 5 and the entry for Ministerial Review.

**Cash-Out Refinance**
A refinancing transaction in which you replace your existing mortgage with a larger loan, taking the difference between the new loan amount and the old balance as cash. If you have significant equity in your home and current interest rates are favorable, a cash-out refinance can fund ADU construction without a separate construction loan. The risk: it replaces your entire mortgage, not just the ADU portion, so it's sensitive to rate changes. See Chapter 6.

**Certificate of Occupancy (CO)**
The official document issued by the building department certifying that construction is complete, all inspections have been passed, and the structure is legal for occupancy. You cannot legally rent an ADU or allow anyone to live in it without a CO. The CO is also required by most lenders as a condition for converting a construction loan to permanent financing. See Chapter 11.

**Change Order (CO)**
A written amendment to the construction contract that authorizes additional work, changes to existing scope, or adjustments to the contract price. Change orders are how scope and cost changes are formally tracked and approved. Never allow work to proceed outside the original contract scope without a signed change order. Verbal agreements about additional work are disputes waiting to happen. See Chapter 9.

**Conditional Use Permit (CUP)**
A special permit required in some jurisdictions before certain land uses — including, historically, ADUs — are allowed on a parcel. A CUP typically requires a public hearing and findings by a planning body. Most state ADU reform legislation has eliminated or substantially limited the use of CUPs for by-right ADUs, but some jurisdictions still attempt to apply them. If you are told you need a CUP for a standard ADU, consult Chapter 5 and, if necessary, a land use attorney.

**Construction Loan**
A short-term loan that funds construction in stages, disbursed through a draw schedule tied to construction milestones rather than paid out in a lump sum. Construction loans are interest-only during the build phase and typically convert to a permanent mortgage upon issuance of the certificate of occupancy. See Draw Schedule and Chapter 6.

**Contingency**
A budget reserve set aside to cover unforeseen costs — conditions discovered during construction, material price changes, or scope changes you didn't anticipate when the project was bid. A well-managed contingency is not padding; it is the financial margin that keeps the project from becoming a crisis when the unexpected happens. Budget 10–15% of total project cost as contingency before you define scope. Projects that skip contingency don't avoid surprises — they just have no capacity to absorb them. See Chapter 10.

**Cost-Plus Contract**
A construction contract in which the owner pays the contractor's actual costs (labor, materials, subcontractors) plus a fixed fee or percentage markup for overhead and profit. Cost-plus contracts expose the owner to budget risk if costs run high but can be appropriate for projects with significant unknown conditions. Compare to Fixed-Price Contract. See Chapter 7.

**Detached ADU**
A freestanding accessory dwelling unit that does not share any walls with the primary residence. Detached ADUs typically offer the best privacy and strongest rental appeal but carry higher construction costs because they require their own foundation, exterior envelope, and utility connections. They also typically require the most site area.

**Discretionary Review**
A permit review process that involves planning staff or a planning commission exercising judgment — considering neighborhood compatibility, design quality, or other factors beyond objective development standards. Discretionary review creates uncertainty: the outcome depends on how the reviewing body interprets subjective criteria. Most state ADU reform legislation has moved standard ADU applications out of discretionary review and into ministerial (by-right) review, but local overlays and historic districts sometimes reintroduce discretionary elements. See Case Study 3 for what a discretionary review process looks like in practice.

**Draw Schedule**
The payment plan in a construction loan or construction contract that ties disbursements to verified construction milestones. In a construction loan context, each draw requires an inspection by a lender-appointed inspector before funds are released. In a construction contract context, the draw schedule is the agreed sequence of progress payments you make to your contractor as phases of work are completed. Never use a draw schedule that front-loads payments before work is complete. See Chapter 9.

**Easement**
A legal right that allows a third party to use a portion of your property for a specific purpose — utility access, drainage, pedestrian access, or similar. Easements can restrict where an ADU can be placed, even if setback and lot coverage requirements are otherwise met. Pull your property's title report and parcel map to identify any easements before you site an ADU.

**FAR (Floor Area Ratio)**
The ratio of a building's total floor area to the area of the lot it sits on. If a jurisdiction limits FAR to 0.5 and your lot is 6,000 square feet, the maximum total building floor area on that lot is 3,000 square feet — across all structures. An ADU adds to the lot's total floor area, so FAR limits can constrain ADU size even when other requirements are met.

**Fixed-Price Contract (Lump Sum Contract)**
A construction contract in which the total price is agreed upon before work begins, regardless of actual costs. Fixed-price contracts shift cost risk to the contractor — if they underbid, that's their problem, not yours. They work best with a well-defined scope and thorough construction documents. They work poorly when scope is vague, because ambiguous specifications become contractor opportunities to argue for change orders. See Chapter 7.

**Garage Conversion ADU**
A garage that has been converted to residential use. Garage conversions are often the lowest-cost ADU pathway because the structure already exists, but they regularly require significant investment in insulation, slab work, utility upgrades, and heating. A garage conversion that looks inexpensive on paper can approach the cost of new construction once the full scope is defined. See Chapter 1 and Chapter 10.

**Geotechnical Engineering (Geotech)**
Engineering analysis of soil conditions to determine bearing capacity, drainage characteristics, and foundation requirements. Required when a project involves new construction on unknown or potentially problematic soil — fill areas, steep slopes, expansive clay soils, or proximity to water. A geotech report costs $1,500–$5,000 and can add significantly to foundation costs if problematic conditions are discovered. See Case Study 4.

**Hard Costs**
The direct costs of physical construction: labor, materials, and subcontractor costs. Hard costs are everything the contractor does and buys to build the structure. They're distinguished from soft costs, which are everything else. A typical ADU budget breaks into roughly 80–85% hard costs and 15–20% soft costs, though this varies significantly by project type and jurisdiction.

**HELOC (Home Equity Line of Credit)**
A revolving line of credit secured by your home's equity, drawn against as needed during construction and repaid over time. HELOCs typically have variable interest rates and interest-only payments during the draw period. They are flexible but rate-sensitive, and the variable rate creates exposure if construction takes longer than expected or rates rise. See Chapter 6.

**HELoan (Home Equity Loan)**
A fixed-rate lump sum loan secured by your home's equity. Unlike a HELOC, which is drawn against as needed, a HELoan is disbursed in full at origination and carries fixed monthly payments. Appropriate when the project scope and cost are well-defined and you want rate certainty. Sometimes called a second mortgage. See Chapter 6.

**Impact Fees**
One-time fees charged by municipalities to offset the cost of public infrastructure (schools, parks, roads, utilities) required by new development. Impact fees for ADUs have been restricted or eliminated in many states — California eliminated them for ADUs under 750 square feet in 2020, Washington prohibited them under HB 1337, and Oregon has broader exemptions for ADUs in cities over 2,500 population. Check your jurisdiction's current fee schedule and Chapter 5 for state-specific rules.

**JADU (Junior Accessory Dwelling Unit)**
A smaller ADU created within the walls of the existing primary dwelling — typically from a bedroom or garage that shares a wall with the house. JADUs are limited to 500 square feet in California (the state that defines the category most precisely). They must include a cooking facility but may share a bathroom with the primary dwelling under some state regulations. Owner-occupancy requirements often apply to JADUs even in jurisdictions that have eliminated them for standard ADUs. See Chapter 1.

**Lien Waiver**
A document signed by a contractor, subcontractor, or supplier releasing their right to file a mechanic's lien against your property in exchange for payment. Collecting lien waivers from every party paid on your project is essential — a subcontractor you've never met can place a lien on your property if your general contractor fails to pay them, even if you've paid the GC in full. See Chapter 7.

**Lot Coverage**
The percentage of your lot's area that may be covered by buildings and other impervious structures, as defined by your zoning ordinance. Lot coverage limits constrain the footprint of new construction — an ADU that fits your setbacks may still be prohibited if it would push lot coverage over the allowed maximum. Coverage calculations vary by jurisdiction; some count only roofed structures, others include patios and driveways.

**Mechanic's Lien**
A legal claim placed against a property by a contractor, subcontractor, or supplier who has not been paid for work performed or materials provided. A mechanic's lien can cloud your title and complicate sale or refinancing. Protect against them by collecting signed lien waivers from every party on the project before making each progress payment.

**MEP (Mechanical, Electrical, Plumbing)**
The three primary trade categories in construction. Mechanical refers to HVAC (heating, ventilation, and air conditioning) systems. Electrical covers the panel, wiring, outlets, lighting, and related systems. Plumbing covers water supply, drain-waste-vent systems, fixtures, and related infrastructure. MEP work is typically performed by licensed subcontractors hired by your general contractor and inspected at the rough-in stage before walls are closed. MEP costs are frequently underestimated in early ADU budgets.

**Ministerial Review**
An approval process in which a permit is reviewed against published objective standards only, with no discretion exercised by the reviewing official. If your project meets the standards, it must be approved; if it doesn't, it must be denied. There is no public hearing, no neighbor notification, and no subjective judgment about neighborhood character or design quality. Most ADU reform legislation mandates ministerial review for qualifying projects. Synonymous with by-right approval.

**Owner-Occupancy Requirement**
A zoning condition requiring that the owner of the parcel live in either the primary dwelling or the ADU as their primary residence. Owner-occupancy requirements have been eliminated in many jurisdictions following ADU reform legislation — California eliminated them statewide for most ADUs, Washington eliminated them under HB 1337 — but they persist in some states and many local codes for JADUs and other subcategories. Verify your jurisdiction's current requirement before proceeding. See Chapter 5.

**Permit**
The official authorization from a local building department to proceed with construction. A building permit is issued after plans are reviewed and found to be in compliance with applicable codes. Permits authorize work; inspections verify that the work was done correctly. An ADU built without a permit cannot be legally rented, is excluded from the official property record, and can create significant complications at sale.

**Pre-Approved ADU Plans**
A library of ADU design plans that have already completed the building code plan review process for a given jurisdiction. Using a pre-approved plan can cut permit processing time significantly. The site-specific elements (setbacks, utilities, foundation) still require review, but the architectural and structural drawings are pre-cleared. Many cities now offer pre-approved plan programs; ask your building department. See Chapter 8.

**Punch List**
The final list of incomplete or deficient items that must be corrected before the project is considered substantially complete and the final payment is made. The punch list is generated during the final walkthrough with your contractor — typically done before the final inspection. A well-managed punch list is negotiated, documented in writing, and tied to a specific completion deadline and final payment release. See Chapter 11.

**RFI (Request for Information)**
A formal written question from the contractor to the designer or owner seeking clarification on something in the construction documents. RFIs document questions and their answers — they create a paper trail that protects both parties if there's a later dispute about what was communicated. If your contractor is not using RFIs for anything beyond casual conversation, ask why.

**Rough-In**
The stage of construction when electrical, plumbing, and HVAC systems are installed inside walls and ceilings — after framing is complete but before drywall is installed. The rough-in phase is the last opportunity to inspect and correct MEP systems without demolishing finished surfaces. All three trade rough-ins require inspections before drywall can be closed. Missing a rough-in inspection is a serious construction error.

**Schedule of Values**
A line-item breakdown of a construction contract showing how the total contract amount is allocated across work phases or categories. The schedule of values is the basis for the draw schedule — it determines what percentage of the contract price has been "earned" when a given phase of work is complete. Request a schedule of values from every contractor before signing a contract. A contractor who cannot provide one is not organized enough to manage your project.

**Setback**
The minimum required distance between a structure and the property line (or in some jurisdictions, the centerline of a street or another structure). Setbacks are defined by zoning and vary by property line type: front setbacks are typically larger than rear and side setbacks. ADU-specific setback requirements have been reduced in many states following reform legislation — California requires jurisdictions to allow at least 4-foot side and rear setbacks for ADUs. Your specific setbacks govern what your site can accommodate.

**Soft Costs**
All project costs that are not direct construction: design fees, engineering fees, permit fees, plan check fees, inspection fees, lender fees, survey costs, geotechnical reports, and insurance. Soft costs are frequently underestimated in early ADU budgets because homeowners focus on the contractor's scope. Budget 15–20% of total project cost for soft costs, depending on project complexity and jurisdiction.

**Soils Report**
See Geotechnical Engineering.

**Submittal**
A document, sample, or product data sheet submitted by the contractor to the designer or owner for review and approval before a material or system is installed. Submittals confirm that what the contractor plans to install matches what the design requires. Common submittals include product cut sheets for windows, fixtures, HVAC equipment, and finish materials. Reviewing and approving submittals is the owner's last opportunity to influence product selection before purchase and installation.

**Title 24**
California's energy efficiency building code, formally the California Energy Code. Title 24 applies to all new construction and certain renovations in California and governs insulation, windows, mechanical systems, lighting, and related components. ADUs in California must comply with current Title 24 requirements. Other states have their own energy codes; Title 24 is used in this guide because California represents the most common ADU market.

**Variance**
A formal exemption from a zoning standard — typically setback, height, or lot coverage — granted by a local planning body when strict application of the standard creates an undue hardship. Variances require a hearing and findings and are rarely granted. If your ADU project requires a variance to be viable, treat that as a significant project risk: variances can be denied, can be appealed by neighbors, and add timeline and cost. Design the project to comply with standards rather than relying on a variance.

**Value Engineering (VE)**
The process of systematically identifying ways to reduce cost without reducing essential function or long-term value. Value engineering is most effective when done during design development, before scope is locked — changing a specification from tile to LVP flooring during design costs nothing; changing it during construction can generate a change order. The guide covers the specific areas where VE typically yields results without sacrificing durability in Chapter 10.

---

*This glossary covers the terms most commonly encountered during ADU planning, permitting, and construction. For jurisdiction-specific terminology, refer to Chapter 5 and your local planning department's published definitions.*
