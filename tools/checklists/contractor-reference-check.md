# Contractor Reference Check Script

---

## Instructions

Run reference checks before you make a final contractor selection — not after. This sounds obvious, but most homeowners treat reference checks as a formality they complete while already mentally committed to a contractor. That is the wrong sequence. References exist to surface problems while you still have leverage and options.

Ask the contractor for at least three references, and ask specifically for projects completed in the **last 18 months**. References from a project the contractor wrapped up four years ago tell you almost nothing about who they are today: crews change, supervisors change, ownership changes, and financial pressure changes. More important, ask specifically for **ADU references if at all possible**. An ADU is a more compressed, relationship-intensive project than a commercial tenant improvement or a custom home — the contractor who did excellent work on a $2,000,000 commercial build-out may be an entirely different operator on a $200,000 owner-occupied residential project with close neighbor proximity and high personal stakes.

**Call, don't email.** People are more candid on the phone. An email response is polished; a phone conversation is real. Plan for 10–15 minutes per reference. Take notes during the call. If a reference gives you a contact number that goes to voicemail repeatedly and is never returned, treat that as data.

Use the 10 questions below in roughly this order. You do not need to read them verbatim — use them as a guide to keep the conversation on track and make sure you cover each topic.

---

## The 10 Questions

### 1. What type of project did you hire them for, and what was the approximate total cost?

**Why you're asking:** You need to know whether their experience with this contractor is relevant to your project. An ADU reference who had a $180,000 detached ADU built is valuable. A reference who had a $25,000 bathroom remodel is not.

**Good answer:** The reference can describe the project clearly — type of work, approximate scope, contract value — and the amount is comparable to your project. They know the contractor personally, not just as a business that did some work on their house.

**Red flag:** The reference is vague about what was actually built, can't give a ballpark on cost, or seems to have limited personal involvement in the project. This can happen when a contractor submits references from friends or family rather than actual clients.

---

### 2. Did the project come in close to the original contract price? If it went over, what drove the change?

**Why you're asking:** Budget overruns are the single most common complaint in residential construction. You want to know whether this contractor's bids are realistic and whether their change orders are legitimate.

**Good answer:** The project came within 10–15% of the original price. If it ran over, the reference can clearly explain the reason — an unforeseen site condition, owner-initiated scope additions, a design change — and the change orders were documented in writing before the work was done.

**Red flag:** The project ran significantly over budget (20%+) with changes the owner did not see coming. Verbal change orders. A sense that the contractor "found" additional costs as the project progressed. Any statement like "the contract price wasn't really what we ended up paying."

---

### 3. Did the project complete close to the original schedule?

**Why you're asking:** Schedule slippage costs you money — extended carrying costs, delayed rental income, additional temporary housing costs if you were displaced. It also signals poor subcontractor management.

**Good answer:** The project completed within 4–6 weeks of the original schedule, and when delays occurred, they were communicated in advance with clear explanations. Weather delays, inspection delays, and material lead time issues are legitimate; unexplained idle time is not.

**Red flag:** The project ran significantly over schedule with little or no advance communication about why. The reference had to push repeatedly to find out what was happening. The contractor was frequently moving their crew to other jobs during the project.

---

### 4. How did the contractor handle unexpected problems that came up during construction?

**Why you're asking:** Every construction project encounters surprises — soil conditions, concealed structural issues, outdated utility connections, unforeseen conditions under a slab. What separates a good contractor from a bad one is not whether problems arise (they always do); it is how they communicate and solve them.

**Good answer:** When problems arose, the contractor came to the owner with a clear explanation, options, and a recommendation. They did not panic, hide the problem, or present a change order as the only possible response without explanation. The owner felt informed and involved.

**Red flag:** The reference describes problems that seemed to appear out of nowhere, after which they received a change order for significant money. Poor communication. A sense that the contractor was managing information strategically rather than sharing it openly.

---

### 5. Was the site supervisor present daily, or did you frequently find the site unattended?

**Why you're asking:** An unattended site is a quality problem, a security problem, and a schedule problem. On a residential ADU project — where your home and yard are adjacent — you will notice if the crew is not showing up.

**Good answer:** The site supervisor was present most days during active phases. When the supervisor was absent, the owner knew in advance and there was a crew lead managing the work. The site was never left unsecured or with work clearly stalled without explanation.

**Red flag:** The reference frequently arrived home to find the site unattended during days when work was supposed to be happening. No advance communication about the supervisor's absence. Work that stalled for days or weeks without explanation.

---

### 6. How responsive was the contractor to your concerns and questions during the project?

**Why you're asking:** You will have questions throughout construction. Some will be urgent. A contractor who does not return calls within a business day during a live project is a contractor who is not prioritizing your job.

**Good answer:** Calls and texts were returned within a few hours for routine questions, same day for urgent issues. The contractor had a clear point of contact for the owner and did not make the owner chase multiple people.

**Red flag:** The reference describes chronic difficulty reaching the contractor or the site supervisor. Multiple days to return calls. Feeling ignored or deprioritized after contract signing.

---

### 7. Did the contractor provide clear documentation — weekly updates, change orders in writing, inspection records?

**Why you're asking:** Documentation is how you protect yourself when a dispute arises. It is also a proxy for how professionally the contractor is managed. An undocumented project is a project you cannot independently verify.

**Good answer:** Change orders were always in writing with itemized costs before work began. The reference received regular updates — weekly email, a project management app, or regular phone calls — and never had to guess where the project stood. Inspection records were provided.

**Red flag:** Change orders were verbal. The reference had to ask repeatedly for updates. Inspection records were incomplete or unavailable. The reference is uncertain whether lien releases were collected from subcontractors.

---

### 8. Were there any payment disputes, billing issues, or concerns about the draw schedule?

**Why you're asking:** Payment disputes are one of the clearest early signals of a contractor relationship that went wrong. They can indicate overbilling, front-loading of the draw schedule, or cash flow problems on the contractor's side.

**Good answer:** The draw schedule matched the contract milestones. The reference never felt pressure to pay ahead of completed work. Final payment was made only after the punch list was complete and the certificate of occupancy was issued.

**Red flag:** The contractor asked for money ahead of the draw schedule. The reference felt pressure to pay to keep the crew on the job. There were disputes over what had been "completed" for purposes of a draw. Any suggestion that the contractor needed funds for reasons outside the project.

---

### 9. Did the work pass all inspections the first time, or were there re-inspections needed?

**Why you're asking:** Repeated inspection failures mean your project is being built to a lower standard than code requires, your timeline is extending, and your contractor may be rushing or cutting corners. One re-inspection on a complex project is not unusual. A pattern of failures is a serious quality indicator.

**Good answer:** The project passed the majority of inspections on the first call. When a re-inspection was needed, the contractor explained what the inspector flagged and corrected it promptly.

**Red flag:** Multiple re-inspections across phases. Corrections that required opening finished work. Any suggestion that the contractor was in conflict with the building department. Framing inspections failing — this is a basic quality gate that most competent contractors clear on the first call.

---

### 10. Would you hire this contractor again for a similar project? Why or why not?

**Why you're asking:** This is the question that cuts through everything else. Most people will answer it honestly because it is hypothetical, not a direct complaint. The "why" is as important as the yes or no.

**Good answer:** An unambiguous yes, with specifics. "Yes, I've already referred them to two friends" is about as good as it gets. A thoughtful yes with honest caveats ("yes, but I'd push harder upfront on documentation") is also valuable.

**Red flag:** A hesitation before yes. A qualified yes that sounds like diplomatic no ("I mean, the project got finished..."). A no with explanations that minimize ("they're not bad, I just had some frustrations"). Any no, regardless of how softened.

---

## Scoring Guide — What the Answers Tell You

Do not score this like a checklist where seven out of ten "good answers" means the contractor passes. Listen for patterns across the ten questions. A reference who describes good responsiveness, clean documentation, on-schedule delivery, and a budget that tracked the contract is describing a well-managed contractor. A reference who hedges on multiple questions, describes chronic communication problems, or cannot give you clear answers to budget and schedule questions is telling you something important — even if they never say a negative word directly.

The strongest possible outcome from a reference check is three references who each describe similar experiences: the project ran roughly on budget, ran roughly on schedule, change orders were in writing and made sense, the supervisor was present and responsive, and they would hire the contractor again without hesitation. Consistency across references is the signal. One glowing reference and two vague ones is not the same as three glowing references. When references conflict — one says great, one hedges, one gives you qualified positives — that inconsistency is itself a data point worth weighing before you sign a contract.
