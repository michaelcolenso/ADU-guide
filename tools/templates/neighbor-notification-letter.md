# Neighbor Notification Letter — ADU Construction Project

---

## Instructions: Who Gets This Letter, When, and Why

Send this letter to every adjacent property owner — anyone who shares a property line with you, including the properties directly behind you if your lot backs to another residential lot. If your street is narrow and your project will involve significant truck traffic or lane closures, consider sending it to neighbors across the street as well. When in doubt, send it to one more household rather than one fewer.

Timing matters. Send this letter **at least 30 days before your construction start date**, even if your jurisdiction requires less (or no) advance notice. The goal is not legal compliance — it is relationship management. A neighbor who learns about your construction project from the sound of an excavator at 7:00 a.m. is starting from a much worse place than a neighbor who received a clear, friendly letter a month earlier and had a chance to ask questions. Most neighbor objections — the kind that result in calls to the city, complaints to the building department, and general friction throughout your project — are driven by surprise, not by legitimate grievance. You control whether your neighbors are surprised.

Send the letter by hand-delivery with a follow-up conversation at the door if you are on good terms, or by certified mail if the relationship warrants it. Keep a copy of every letter sent, the date sent, and any response received. If a neighbor raises concerns, log those conversations — date, what they said, what you said. That documentation protects you if a dispute escalates.

## When Neighbors Object — What to Do

Even with 30 days of advance notice and a well-written letter, some neighbors will have concerns. Here is how to handle it without making things worse.

**Acknowledge their concern directly; do not dismiss it.** "I understand this is disruptive and I appreciate you letting me know" costs you nothing and keeps the conversation from escalating. Defensiveness closes doors. Most neighbor concerns are about noise, truck traffic, privacy, or property value — all of which are legitimate and worth discussing.

**Offer to meet in person before construction starts.** A 20-minute conversation over coffee resolves more than a week of back-and-forth letters. Bring your site plan, explain where the ADU will sit relative to the property line, and answer their questions directly. Most people are more reasonable in person than in writing.

**Know your rights.** A properly permitted ADU that meets all zoning, setback, and building code requirements cannot be blocked by neighbor objection in most jurisdictions. California, Washington, Oregon, and Massachusetts have enacted state preemption laws that explicitly limit local governments' ability to restrict compliant ADUs — and those laws apply even if neighbors object. A neighbor calling the city to complain about a permitted, code-compliant project will receive the same answer from the building department: the permit is valid and the project can proceed.

That said, knowing your rights and leading with your rights are two different things. Telling a concerned neighbor "I don't have to listen to you" is both unpleasant and counterproductive. You will be living next to this person after the project is complete.

**Keep a log of all neighbor communications.** Note the date, the neighbor's name, what they said, and what you said or agreed to. If a complaint does escalate to the building department, a documented record of your good-faith communication efforts is valuable. If a neighbor makes representations about property damage, blocked views, or other claimed harms, you want those claims documented in writing.
