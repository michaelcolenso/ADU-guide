# Change Order Log — ADU Construction Project

> **Disclaimer:** This template is provided for educational and reference purposes only. It does not constitute legal advice. Consult a licensed attorney in your jurisdiction before using any contract for an actual construction project.

---

## How to Use This Log

Every change to your contract scope — whether it costs more, costs less, or affects only the timeline — is a change order (CO). Change orders are the primary mechanism through which construction budgets spiral. A project that starts at $220,000 and accumulates $60,000 in unvetted change orders is not a surprise when it happens. It's a management failure.

Open this log on the day you sign your contract and keep it current throughout the project. Every time your contractor mentions something that sounds like "that's going to cost extra," that is the beginning of a change order — and it belongs in this log before you respond. The log forces a written record of every requested change, your decision, and the cumulative budget impact. That running cumulative column is the one that matters most: it shows you exactly where you stand against your original contract at any point in the project.

## Red Flag Checklist — Five Situations That Demand Extra Scrutiny

Before approving any change order, run through this checklist. If you check any of these boxes, slow down.

- [ ] **The CO is verbal, not written.** Your contract should require all change orders in writing. If your contractor is describing scope changes in conversation rather than submitting a written CO with itemized costs, you have a documentation problem. Do not authorize the work until a written CO is in hand.

- [ ] **The CO is submitted after the work is already done.** "We ran into a problem and went ahead and fixed it — it's going to be $4,200" is not an acceptable process. Unless the situation was a genuine safety emergency, work should not begin on changed scope until you have approved a written CO. A contractor who routinely does this is building budget leverage against you.

- [ ] **The CO covers work that arguably should have been included in the original bid.** Contractors with estimating problems — or those who deliberately bid low to win the job — will attempt to recapture margin through change orders. Ask yourself: would a competent contractor inspecting this site and scope have reasonably anticipated this? If yes, push back hard.

- [ ] **The CO references a condition that was visible during the bidding walk.** If you gave the contractor site access before the bid and the "unforeseen condition" they're now billing for was visible at the time, that is not an unforeseen condition. It is an estimating failure.

- [ ] **You are being pressured to approve quickly.** "We need to know today or it will delay the project" is a negotiation tactic, not a scheduling reality in most cases. Urgency that is real will still be real tomorrow, after you have taken 24 hours to review the request.

## The Three Responses Framework

Every change order gets one of three responses. No other response is acceptable.

**Approve** — You have reviewed the written CO, confirmed the scope is legitimate and not already covered by the original contract, received an itemized cost breakdown, and you agree the price is reasonable. Sign and document it.

**Negotiate** — The scope is legitimate, but the price is not. Request an itemized breakdown (materials, labor, equipment, markup — each as separate line items), get a comparison quote if the amount is significant, and counter. Most change orders have room in them.

**Reject** — The scope is not legitimate (it was already in the original bid, it was foreseeable, or the cause is disputed). Put your rejection in writing with a clear explanation. This creates the record you will need if the dispute escalates.

---

## The 5% Threshold Rule

Any single change order that exceeds **5% of your original contract value** should trigger a mandatory pause before you respond. A 5% CO on a $200,000 contract is $10,000 — that is not a routine paperwork item. At that level, you should:

1. Request a full itemized cost breakdown before responding.
2. Get at least one competitive quote from a qualified subcontractor or estimating reference.
3. Verify with your architect or designer (if you have one) whether the underlying condition was reasonably foreseeable from the original documents.
4. Confirm that your contingency budget can absorb the amount if approved.
5. Consider whether the CO indicates a broader estimating or site-condition problem that will generate additional COs.

A single large CO is sometimes legitimate. A pattern of large COs — even individually below 5% — is usually a sign that either the original scope was underestimated or the project is being managed poorly. Watch the cumulative column.
