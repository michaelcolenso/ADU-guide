# ADU Guide — Writing Style Guide

## Voice Calibration

Think of the voice as **Mike Holmes meets a helpful neighbor**: technically precise, experienced enough to be blunt about what matters, and genuinely invested in the reader's success. The reader is a smart adult making one of the biggest financial decisions of their life. Respect that.

### Sentence-Level Examples

**Too vague:** "Costs can vary significantly depending on your area."

**Just right:** "A detached 600 sq ft ADU costs $150,000–$250,000 in most West Coast markets, but $100,000–$180,000 in the Mountain West. The biggest variables are foundation type, utility connections, and local labor rates — not the finishes most people obsess over."

**Too promotional:** "With our comprehensive budget calculator, you'll never be surprised by hidden costs again!"

**Just right:** "The budget calculator in this guide breaks costs into 14 categories. The ones that surprise most homeowners are utility upgrades ($5,000–$15,000), engineering and surveys ($8,000–$12,000), and permit fees ($2,000–$8,000 depending on jurisdiction)."

## Structural Conventions

### Chapter Openings

Start every chapter with one of these patterns:
- **A scenario** that puts the reader in the moment ("You've just gotten three contractor bids. They range from $180,000 to $310,000. How do you evaluate them?")
- **A revealing statistic** that reframes the topic ("42% of ADU projects exceed their original budget by more than 20%. Here's why — and how to be in the other 58%.")
- **A common misconception** that the chapter will correct ("Most homeowners think the permit process starts with submitting an application. It actually starts 6–8 weeks earlier.")

Never start with a definition. The reader already knows what an ADU is by Chapter 2.

### Section Transitions

Connect sections with a forward reference that explains why the next topic matters: "Now that you understand setback requirements, the next question is whether your lot has the utility capacity to support an ADU — because a sewer upgrade can add $15,000–$30,000 to your project before you even break ground."

### Numbers and Data

- Always provide ranges, not single figures, for costs. A single figure invites "but that's not what I was quoted" objections.
- Cite the source inline when the number is surprising or counterintuitive.
- Round to meaningful precision. "$8,347" is false precision for an estimate. "$8,000–$12,000" is useful.
- Use dollar signs and commas for all monetary values. Write "$150,000" not "$150K" or "150k."

### State-Specific Content

When information varies by state or city, use this pattern:

> In most states, ADUs under 750 sq ft are exempt from impact fees. California eliminated all impact fees for ADUs under 750 sq ft effective 2020 (AB 68). Washington prohibits impact fees on ADUs as of HB 1337 (2023). Oregon has exempted ADUs from system development charges in cities over 2,500 population since 2021. Check your state's specific provisions in Chapter 5.

This gives the reader the national default, names the key states with specific references, and points to the regulatory chapter for full details.

### Cross-References

Use natural language cross-references, not page numbers (which shift in digital formats):

- "See Chapter 7 for the contractor vetting process."
- "The financing comparison matrix (included in Builder and Complete tiers) models this scenario."
- "We cover this in detail in the Permit Process chapter."

## Terminology

### Preferred Terms

| Use this | Not this | Why |
|----------|----------|-----|
| ADU | accessory dwelling unit (after first mention) | Established abbreviation |
| Detached ADU | backyard cottage, granny flat | Professional terminology |
| JADU | junior accessory dwelling unit (after first mention) | Established abbreviation |
| General contractor (GC) | builder, contractor (alone) | Precision matters |
| Design-build firm | one-stop shop | Professional terminology |
| Prefab / modular ADU | tiny house | Different product category |
| Owner-occupancy requirement | live-in requirement | Legal terminology |
| Impact fees | development fees | Standard industry term |
| Certificate of occupancy (CO) | final approval | Legal terminology |
| Change order | extra work, add-on | Contract terminology |

### Terms to Define on First Use

These terms are familiar to construction professionals but may confuse homeowners. Define them in context on first appearance:

- Setback (the minimum distance between your ADU and the property line)
- Lot coverage (the percentage of your lot that can be covered by structures)
- Title 24 (California's energy efficiency requirements — other states have equivalents)
- Draw schedule (the payment plan tied to construction milestones)
- Rough-in (when electrical, plumbing, and HVAC are installed before walls are closed up)
- Punch list (the final list of small items to fix before the project is officially complete)
- MEP (mechanical, electrical, plumbing — the three main trade categories)
- Soft costs (everything that isn't physical construction: design, engineering, permits, inspections)

## Formatting Rules

- **Headings:** Use `##` for major sections within a chapter, `###` for subsections. Never skip heading levels.
- **Bold:** Use for key terms on first definition, critical warnings, and emphasis on essential numbers. Do not bold entire sentences.
- **Italics:** Use for book titles, technical terms in context, and gentle emphasis.
- **Tables:** Use for comparisons (financing options, cost ranges by region, contractor evaluation criteria). Always include a brief intro sentence before the table explaining what it shows.
- **Blockquotes:** Reserve for direct quotes from real sources (legislation text, industry reports). Attribute immediately.
- **Code blocks:** Never use in the guide content (this isn't a technical manual).
- **Callout boxes:** Use sparingly for "Pro Tip" (insider knowledge from construction experience), "Common Mistake" (expensive errors to avoid), and "State Watch" (jurisdiction-specific alerts).

## Fact-Checking Standards

Every factual claim in the guide falls into one of three categories:

**Category A — Must verify with primary source before publishing:**
- Legislative provisions (bill numbers, effective dates, specific requirements)
- Government fee schedules and timelines
- Financial product terms (loan limits, interest rates, qualification criteria)
- Statistics cited with specific numbers

**Category B — Verify with industry sources:**
- Construction cost ranges
- Timeline estimates
- Market statistics (permit counts, growth rates)

**Category C — Author expertise (no external source required):**
- Construction process descriptions
- Best practice recommendations
- Decision frameworks
- Common mistake descriptions based on professional experience

When in doubt, search and verify. It's better to spend 2 minutes confirming a number than to publish something a reader can disprove in 30 seconds.
