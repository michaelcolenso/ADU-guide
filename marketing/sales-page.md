## The Problem

Most homeowners approach an ADU project the way they approach home renovation: they get a few contractor bids, pick the middle one, and figure they'll manage problems as they come up. That works reasonably well when you're replacing a kitchen. It is a reliable path to disaster when you're building a separate dwelling unit, navigating a permit process that can run 6–18 months, managing subcontractors you've never vetted, and funding a $150,000–$300,000 project on a draw schedule you don't fully understand.

The mistakes aren't obvious until they're expensive. A utility upgrade that wasn't in the original scope adds $20,000 to a budget with no contingency. A lump-sum bid from a contractor who underestimated lumber costs becomes a change order conversation every three weeks. A permit application gets rejected — twice — because the site plans didn't account for a drainage easement that was always in the title report. These aren't freak events. They're patterns that show up on project after project, and the reason they keep happening is that homeowners go into ADU projects with renovation-level knowledge and project-management-level risk.

There's also a quiet information problem. The ADU legal landscape changed dramatically between 2019 and 2024 — California, Washington, Massachusetts, Oregon, Colorado, and a dozen other states passed legislation that rewrote the rules on lot coverage, setbacks, owner-occupancy requirements, and impact fees. The only book-length guide to ADU construction that currently exists — Kol Peterson's *Backdoor Revolution* — was published in 2018, covers Portland specifically, and predates most of this legislation. There are a handful of online courses, but they're taught by lenders pitching loan products and design-build firms selling their own services. The information is out there, but it's fragmented, outdated, and often comes with an agenda.
