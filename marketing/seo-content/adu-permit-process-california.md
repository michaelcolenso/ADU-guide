# How to Permit an ADU in California: A Step-by-Step Guide (2025–2026)

**Target keyword:** adu permit process california
**Word count target:** 1,500–2,000 words

---

California has the clearest ADU permitting framework in the country. That's not an accident — the state has spent seven years systematically dismantling the local barriers that once made ADU approvals arbitrary, slow, and politically fraught. What emerged is a statutory structure that gives homeowners real procedural protections. The 60-day clock is real. The prohibition on discretionary review is real. The right to build is, in most cases, genuinely yours.

But "clearest framework" does not mean "simple process." You still have to navigate local plan check queues, state energy code compliance, structural requirements, and utility connection logistics — none of which the legislature has streamlined for you. Homeowners who understand the statutory protections and the practical sequence move faster and spend less. Homeowners who don't understand them let plan checkers slow-walk their applications and miss deadlines that are legally mandatory.

This guide covers both: the law you need to know and the step-by-step process for getting a permit issued.

---

## What Ministerial Approval Means — and Why It Matters

Before walking through the permit sequence, you need to understand the legal architecture that makes California ADU permitting different from almost every other state.

Under California Government Code § 66333 (formerly § 65852.2), ADU applications must receive "ministerial" approval — not "discretionary" approval. The distinction is fundamental. A discretionary decision means a planning body can weigh aesthetics, neighborhood character, traffic, and dozens of other subjective factors and simply say no. A ministerial decision means the approval must be issued if the application meets the objective, codified standards. No design review board, no public hearing, no neighbor input that can derail your project.

The 60-day clock enforces this: your city must act on a complete ADU application within 60 days. If they don't act, the application is deemed approved. That provision exists precisely because some jurisdictions dragged their feet indefinitely before the state intervened.

What ministerial approval protects you from: discretionary design review, Planning Commission hearings, neighbor appeals, and most historical or environmental review under CEQA for qualifying projects. What it does not protect you from: building department plan check timelines (which are separate from planning), utility agency delays, and construction-phase inspections.

## Key Legislation to Know

Three bills define the current landscape. You don't need to read them in full, but knowing what they do helps you respond when a local planner tells you something that contradicts state law.

**AB 68 (effective January 1, 2020):** Eliminated minimum lot size requirements for ADUs, eliminated owner-occupancy requirements for most ADUs, reduced setbacks to 4 feet rear and side for detached ADUs, and prohibited cities from requiring replacement parking when a garage is converted. This bill fundamentally opened up ADU eligibility for millions of California homeowners.

**AB 976 (effective January 1, 2023):** Eliminated owner-occupancy requirements permanently, removing a provision that had previously been allowed to sunset. As of this bill's effective date, you do not need to live on the property to build or rent an ADU.

**SB 897 (effective January 1, 2023):** Raised the maximum height for detached ADUs to 18 feet (and higher in some circumstances when within half a mile of a major transit stop), eliminated setback requirements for ADU replacement in the same footprint as an existing structure, and closed several remaining local override loopholes. SB 897 is the bill that makes replacement-structure ADUs — converting an existing garage or other outbuilding — particularly straightforward.

If a planner cites a local ordinance that appears to contradict these statutes, you have grounds to push back. State ADU law preempts inconsistent local ordinances.

## The Permit Sequence: Step by Step

### Step 1: Pre-Application Meeting

Most California jurisdictions offer a pre-application meeting with planning and/or building staff. Take it. It costs little or nothing and buys you clarity on local requirements before you spend money on drawings.

Bring a site plan sketch, a description of your proposed ADU (type, square footage, location on the parcel), and a list of questions. Ask specifically about: utility connection requirements and fees, school impact fees (which are separately assessed from permit fees), and whether the city has a pre-approved plan program (more on that below). Come out of this meeting with the local handout or checklist of application requirements.

### Step 2: Design — From Schematic to Permit-Ready Drawings

Your permit application requires permit-ready drawings, not conceptual sketches. The gap between those two things is where most homeowners are surprised by cost and time.

Permit-ready drawings for an ADU in California typically include: a site plan showing the parcel boundary, existing structures, the proposed ADU footprint, setbacks, and utility connections; floor plans at 1/4" scale showing dimensions, room labels, and door and window locations; exterior elevations (all four sides); roof plan; sections as required; structural drawings (foundation, framing) with engineer stamp; and Title 24 energy compliance documentation.

Title 24 energy compliance is California-specific and not trivial. It requires a certified energy analyst or software modeling to demonstrate the building envelope, insulation, and HVAC meet current standards. Factor this into your design timeline and budget — it's a separate deliverable from the architectural drawings.

If your site has expansive soils, a high water table, or other site-specific concerns, the building department may require a soils report before accepting your application. Ask at the pre-application meeting.

### Step 3: Application Assembly

A "complete application" under California law triggers the 60-day clock. An incomplete application does not. Building departments will send back incomplete applications and the clock restarts when you resubmit.

Standard required documents:
- Completed permit application form (jurisdiction-specific)
- Site plan, floor plans, elevations (to local scale and format requirements)
- Structural drawings with engineer's wet stamp
- Title 24 energy compliance package
- Soils report (if required by the department)
- Copy of deed or other proof of ownership
- Owner-builder form or contractor license information
- School impact fee payment or documentation of exemption (ADUs under 500 sq ft are exempt from school fees per California Education Code § 17620)

Review the local checklist against your documents before submittal. Missing one item delays everything.

### Step 4: Application Submittal

Most California jurisdictions now accept electronic submittal, though some smaller cities still require paper or in-person drops. Submit everything the checklist requires. Include a cover sheet that lists every document in the package and the sheet number or file name where it appears.

Once you submit, request written confirmation that the application is received and ask explicitly: "Has this been determined to be a complete application?" That question starts the clock — and if they say no, it gets you a specific list of what's missing rather than a vague rejection weeks later.

### Step 5: Plan Check

This is where California's regional variation becomes significant. The 60-day statutory clock applies, but plan check timelines vary dramatically by jurisdiction:

**Los Angeles (ADU Express Program):** The city launched an ADU Express lane specifically for ADU permit applications. Standard review runs 2–4 weeks for straightforward detached ADUs. If you use an LA pre-approved standard plan, the structural review is largely pre-cleared and review can be faster still.

**San Francisco / Bay Area jurisdictions:** 12–24 weeks is realistic in many Bay Area cities. SF has notoriously slow plan check queues. Oakland and San Jose are faster but still longer than Southern California.

**Sacramento:** Typically 4–8 weeks.

**San Diego:** 4–8 weeks for standard applications; the city has made ADU streamlining a priority.

Plan check generates corrections — a list of items the plan checker identified as non-compliant or unclear. This is normal and expected. It does not mean your project is denied.

### Step 6: Corrections Response

Your architect or designer addresses each correction in writing and reissues the drawings with revision clouds marking changes. The corrections response package goes back to the same reviewer. Turnaround on a corrections re-check is typically 1–3 weeks.

The key to fast corrections cycles: respond to every comment, don't assume any correction is minor, and have your architect use the plan checker's exact language when describing how each issue was resolved. Ambiguous responses generate a second round of corrections.

### Step 7: Permit Issuance

Once plan check is complete, the building department calculates final permit fees and issues the permit. You pay the fees, they hand over the permit card and approved drawings. Post the permit card at the job site. The approved drawings must be on site at every inspection.

### Step 8: Construction Inspections

California ADU construction requires inspections at standard intervals: foundation/footing before concrete pour, framing before sheathing, rough mechanical/electrical/plumbing before insulation, insulation before drywall, and final. Some jurisdictions require additional inspections.

Schedule inspections through your jurisdiction's online system or phone line. Most require 24–48 hours advance notice. Failed inspections require a re-inspection fee in most cities, typically $100–$300.

### Step 9: Final Inspection and Certificate of Occupancy

The final inspection is comprehensive — the inspector verifies that everything permitted was built as approved. After a passed final inspection, the city issues a Certificate of Occupancy (CO). The CO is the legal document that certifies the ADU as habitable space. You need it before a tenant can legally occupy the unit, and your lender will require it if you financed construction.

## The Pre-Approved Plan Shortcut

Several California cities have developed pre-approved ADU plan programs that dramatically reduce plan check time by pre-clearing structural and, in some cases, energy compliance review.

Los Angeles's Standard Plan Program is the most developed in the state. The city has approved a library of ADU designs — mostly detached 400–1,200 sq ft units in a range of configurations. When a homeowner submits using one of these plans, structural review is pre-cleared, which reduces plan check to site-specific items: setbacks, grading, utility connections. Review times under the Standard Plan Program run 2–3 weeks in many cases.

San Jose, Sacramento, and a growing list of other cities have similar programs. At your pre-application meeting, ask explicitly: "Do you have a pre-approved ADU plan library, and what's the plan check timeline for applications using those plans?" If the answer is yes and any of the plans fit your site, use one.

The trade-off: pre-approved plans are standard designs. They may not match exactly what you envisioned, and customization usually removes the pre-approval advantage. For a homeowner building a straightforward detached studio or one-bedroom, the time and fee savings often outweigh the design constraint.

## Common Rejection and Delay Reasons

**Incomplete application at submittal** is the most common cause of delay. Review Step 3 above carefully and compare your documents against the local checklist line by line before submitting.

**Title 24 compliance errors:** energy modeling that doesn't match the drawings, missing HVAC specifications, or incorrect insulation values. Hire a certified energy analyst, not a generalist who will figure it out on the fly.

**Setback non-compliance:** detached ADUs require 4-foot rear and side setbacks under state law, but if you're converting or replacing a structure that sits closer to the property line, SB 897's replacement-structure provisions may apply. Know which rule governs your project before finalizing the site plan.

**Utility issues:** some jurisdictions require a new utility service lateral for the ADU; others allow shared service from the main house. This needs to be resolved at the design phase, not during plan check.

## Permit Fees by City

Permit fees vary substantially across California and are assessed as a combination of base plan check fee, permit issuance fee, and in some cases school and transportation impact fees.

**Los Angeles:** Plan check fee typically $1,500–$3,500 for a standard detached ADU; permit fee $2,000–$4,500. School fees apply to units over 500 sq ft.

**San Francisco:** Plan check and permit fees in aggregate often run $5,000–$12,000 for new detached ADUs, reflecting the city's high unit costs and extensive fee structure.

**Oakland:** $2,500–$6,000 range for plan check and permit combined.

**San Diego:** $2,000–$5,000 range; the city has actively reduced ADU fees in recent years.

**Sacramento:** $1,500–$3,500 for typical detached ADU permits.

These are ranges, not guarantees. Your final fee depends on project square footage, valuation method, and local fee schedules that change annually. Request a fee estimate from the building department during your pre-application meeting.
