## Email 2 — Day 3

**Subject:** The mistake that costs ADU builders $30,000–$90,000
**Preview text:** It's not the contractor. It's the utility screen you skipped.

---

In 2021, I was brought in to review a stalled ADU project in suburban Portland. The homeowners had a beautiful design — a detached 650 sq ft studio over a detached garage, architect-drawn, permit-ready. They were three weeks from submitting when the city utility review flagged that the existing sewer lateral couldn't support an additional dwelling unit. The lateral was undersized and partially collapsed. Full replacement required cutting through 40 feet of driveway and a neighbor's easement.

The cost to fix it: $34,000, not including the two-month delay to permit the lateral replacement separately.

The cost to have caught it before design started: one phone call to the city public works department and one sewer camera inspection, which runs $300–$600.

This is the utility problem, and it's the most reliably expensive surprise in ADU construction because it's the most reliably skipped step in the pre-design process. Homeowners focus on what they can see — the lot, the zoning map, the rough layout — and don't think to ask what's underground until something is already drawn.

**The pre-design utility screen takes 30–60 minutes and four phone calls:**

First, call your city or county public works department and ask two questions: What is the existing sewer capacity for my property, and is the lateral sized for a second dwelling unit? Some jurisdictions will tell you over the phone. Others will require you to pull a record drawing, which is typically available for $20–$50.

Second, call your electric utility and ask whether your current service panel — typically 100 or 200 amps for a single-family home — has capacity for an additional unit, or whether a service upgrade will be required. Service upgrades run $3,000–$8,000 depending on whether the transformer needs upgrading too. If there's a transformer upgrade involved, that timeline can stretch 4–6 months and runs $8,000–$20,000.

Third, call your water utility and ask whether the existing meter size supports an ADU, and whether a capacity fee will be required. Capacity fees vary enormously by jurisdiction — I've seen them range from $0 (in states that have banned them for ADUs) to $22,000 for a new connection in a capacity-constrained district.

Fourth, if your property uses natural gas and the ADU design includes a gas appliance, call your gas utility and ask whether the existing service line has sufficient capacity, or whether a new service line will be required.

This four-call screen takes an afternoon and can save you $20,000–$90,000 in discovery costs that show up mid-construction. Do it before you hire an architect, not after you have permit-ready drawings.

## Email 3 — Day 6

**Subject:** How to read a contractor bid (and why the lowest one usually isn't)
**Preview text:** The distinction that tells you whether a contractor has thought through your project.

---

You've probably heard that you shouldn't always take the lowest contractor bid. That's true, but it's not very useful advice on its own — it doesn't tell you what to look for instead.

Here's the one distinction that matters most in evaluating an ADU contractor bid: **lump-sum vs. line-item.**

A lump-sum bid is a single number for the whole project. "Detached ADU, 650 sq ft, $248,000, complete." It might include a one-page scope description, but the pricing isn't broken down by trade or category. Most homeowners accept these because they seem clean and easy to compare.

A line-item bid breaks the project into components: site work and foundation, framing, roofing, windows and doors, MEP rough-in, insulation, drywall, flooring, cabinetry and millwork, plumbing fixtures, electrical trim-out, HVAC equipment, exterior finish, landscaping restoration, permit fees, temporary facilities. Each line has a quantity, a unit cost, and a total. The whole thing adds up to the contract price.

The difference matters for one concrete reason: **change orders.**

When a lump-sum contractor hits an unexpected condition — a foundation that requires deeper footings, a sewer lateral that needs relining, framing lumber prices that moved — they come to you with a change order. Because the original contract didn't break out costs, you have no baseline to evaluate whether the change order amount is reasonable. You're negotiating in the dark.

When a line-item contractor hits the same condition, you can look at the affected line, compare it to what was estimated, and have an informed conversation about whether the change is legitimate and whether the pricing is in range. You're negotiating with information.

**Specific red flags in contractor proposals to watch for:**

A bid that arrives within 24 hours of a site visit is almost always a lump-sum guess, not an estimate. Accurate bids take time — for a 600–800 sq ft ADU, a careful contractor needs 3–5 days to price material quantities, confirm subcontractor availability, and build in accurate allowances for finish items.

Any bid that uses "allowances" without defining them is protecting the contractor, not you. An allowance is the contractor's placeholder for a cost that isn't fully specified yet — cabinetry, tile, lighting fixtures. The problem is that allowances are systematically underestimated. A $3,500 allowance for kitchen cabinetry sounds reasonable until you're standing in a cabinet showroom and everything starts at $8,000. Get allowances defined as specifically as possible before you sign.

A contractor who won't provide references from the past 18 months — not older projects, the past 18 months — has something to tell you about how their recent work has been going. Ask for three references and call all three.

## Email 4 — Day 10

**Subject:** What changed in ADU law this year (and why it matters for your project)
**Preview text:** California, Washington, and Massachusetts all moved in 2024–2025. Here's what happened.

---

ADU law is one of the fastest-moving areas of residential land use regulation in the country right now. Between 2019 and 2025, at least 18 states passed legislation that materially changed what homeowners can build, where, and at what cost. The pace of change has been significant enough that information from 2021 or 2022 is often unreliable for planning purposes today.

Here's what moved in the major ADU markets through 2024–2025, and why it matters:

**California** continued to refine its ADU framework, which has been the most permissive and most legislated in the country. The state eliminated impact fees for ADUs under 750 sq ft in 2020 (AB 68), and subsequent legislation continued to restrict local governments' ability to impose fees, delay approvals, or require owner-occupancy. AB 1033, effective 2024, allows ADUs in certain jurisdictions to be sold separately from the main house as condominiums — which is a significant change for homeowners who want to monetize an ADU without selling the whole property. Not all jurisdictions have opted in, and implementation is uneven, but it's a material new option worth tracking.

**Washington** passed HB 1337 in 2023, which took full effect and became the operative framework in 2024. The bill prohibits local governments from requiring owner-occupancy, restricting ADU size below 1,000 sq ft, requiring additional off-street parking for ADUs near transit, or charging impact fees on ADUs. It also requires cities planning under the Growth Management Act to allow at least one ADU on every lot zoned for single-family residential. Washington is now one of the most permissive states in the country for ADU development.

**Massachusetts** has been the sleeper story. The Affordable Homes Act, signed in August 2024, requires that all communities zoned for single-family residential must allow ADUs by right — without requiring a special permit or discretionary approval — up to 900 sq ft or half the main house floor area. This is a significant shift for a state where many communities had historically blocked ADU development entirely. Implementation is rolling out through 2025–2026, and there is still significant local variation in how communities are interpreting the new requirements.

The broader pattern across states: the direction of change is consistently toward permissiveness. Owner-occupancy requirements are being eliminated. Impact fees are being capped or banned. Minimum size floors and maximum size ceilings are being raised or removed. Approval timelines are being shortened by law. This doesn't mean your specific city has implemented everything — local compliance with state mandates is inconsistent and sometimes requires homeowners to push back — but the legal landscape is more favorable for ADU construction than it was five years ago, in most markets.

The challenge is that this landscape changes frequently enough that any static resource goes stale quickly. When a state or major city changes its rules materially, the affected files are updated with primary source citations.
